# USA Inc. — The Sovereign as Corporation

## What this note is

This is the speculative layer. Everything in the Entity Theory up to this point — the 379 entities, the shuffle test (p = 0.017), the charter audit, the progression synchronization, the monetary-break overlay, the SFFAS 47 legal spine — is **verified fact** or **tested inference**. This note takes a step past the evidence and asks: what happens when you read the entire 250-year history of the United States as the lifecycle of a corporation?

The answer is not a conspiracy theory. The answer is that it reads **better** as a corporate lifecycle than as a political narrative — because the corporate frame is the sovereign's own frame, openly adopted since Hamilton, confirmed by the Supreme Court in 1823, and audited under its own consolidation standard (SFFAS 47) since 2017. The speculative part is not the claim that the government is corporate. That is settled law. The speculative part is what happens when you take the corporate frame seriously as an **interpretive lens** for the re-founding — and especially for what the 2025–26 restructuring actually *is*.

Three layers, clearly separated:
- **Black letter** — what the law says, undisputed
- **Strong inference** — where the entity theory reads the evidence a specific way
- **Speculative** — where this note goes past the evidence into interpretive territory

## The factual spine (black letter)

### The sovereign IS a corporation

Chief Justice Marshall, 1823: *"The United States is a government, and, consequently, a body politic and corporate."* And an 1886 case goes further: the United States is *"a great corporation … ordained and established by the American people."* This is not a metaphor. It is a legal holding. The sovereign has corporate personality — the power to contract, to own property, to sue and be sued, to issue instruments — and has had it since the founding.

### Hamilton weaponized the form

The first five years of the republic are a corporate-formation sprint:

- **1789:** The sovereign's articles of incorporation take effect (the Constitution, ratified 1788, operational 1789-03-04).
- **1790:** The first permanent liability: Hamilton's Funding & Assumption Act nationalizes the war debt. An obligation is itself an entity on the sovereign's balance sheet.
- **1791:** The First Bank of the United States — the first government-sponsored banking corporation. Twenty-year charter, $10M capitalization, 80% private ownership. Hamilton's genius: the sovereign charters a private corporation, capitalizes it with its own debt, and uses it to manage the money supply. The corporate form does what the sovereign cannot do directly — create a bank insulated from politics.
- **1792:** The Coinage Act charters the US Mint as a Treasury bureau. The production subsidiary.

Hamilton's template: the state uses the corporate form to do what the crown cannot do barefaced. A bank, a mint, a permanent debt. All of them legal entities chartered by the sovereign and operated through the corporate mechanism. This is not a secret. It is the financial architecture of the republic, built in the open, defended in public debate, and confirmed by the courts.

### The government's own books confirm it

FASAB SFFAS 47 ("Reporting Entity," 2014, effective FY 2017) is the government's own consolidation standard — the accounting rule that says what is and is not "the government." Its control test uses nearly Katie's words: **consolidation turns on control, not ownership.** The Financial Report of the United States consolidates ~150 entities into one reporting entity, discloses five more (the Fed, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, Amtrak, the COVID SPVs), and names several related parties (the FHLBanks, the IMF, the multilateral development banks). The government keeps its own books exactly the way an accountant reads a consolidated group.

The audit note has the details: the government's three-tier scheme (consolidate / disclose / related party) independently draws the Entity Theory's dashed lines at the Fed and the GSEs — then corrects us three times (Amtrak should be dashed, Smithsonian should be solid, the chartered-but-private bodies should be outside entirely). The government drew our diagram before we did.

## The debunking (what "USA Inc." is NOT)

### The 1871 claim

The DC Organic Act of 1871 merged Washington City, Georgetown, and Washington County into one municipal government — **"a body corporate for municipal purposes."** A city charter, replaced in 1874. The act says nothing about the nation. No court has ever read it otherwise. The conspiracy theory that says "the US was secretly incorporated in 1871" gets every particular wrong: wrong act, wrong entity, wrong date, wrong legal effect.

### 28 U.S.C. § 3002(15)

This statute sits inside the Federal Debt Collection Procedures Act (1990). It opens "As used **in this chapter**" and defines "United States" to *include* federal corporations, agencies, and instrumentalities — so the government's debt-collection machinery works for money owed to TVA or Ex-Im Bank. It treats federal corporations as arms of the United States. That is the **opposite** of declaring the United States a corporation. It says the government's subsidiaries are part of the parent for collection purposes.

### What the conspiracists got right anyway

The honest ground the conspiracy theories stand on — and misread — is real:

- The sovereign does have corporate legal personality (Marshall, 1823).
- DC genuinely is a municipal corporation.
- The US charters corporations at will and operates dozens.
- The corporate form has been the sovereign's primary operating instrument since Hamilton.

The interesting claim was never "the US secretly incorporated in 1871." It is that **the corporate form has been the sovereign's instrument since the founding** — openly, legally, audited — and that the 2025–26 restructuring is the latest episode in a 250-year corporate lifecycle. That claim is simply, boringly true. And it is far more interesting than the conspiracy.

## The corporate lifecycle — five eras (strong inference → speculative)

Read the 250 years as five phases of a corporate lifecycle. Each phase maps to a distinct M&A pattern — chartering, consolidation, expansion, deconsolidation, restructuring — and each maps to a chapter of the Monetary Chronicle. This is the speculative frame.

### Era I: Incorporation (1776–1791)

**Corporate analogue: IPO + first capitalization.**

The Declaration is the prospectus. The Articles of Confederation are the first articles of incorporation (in force 1781-03-01). The Constitution is the **charter restatement** — same entity, new articles, new governance structure (stronger board, weaker shareholders). Article VI carries the old entity's debts forward: *"All debts contracted and engagements entered into before the adoption of this Constitution shall be as valid against the United States under this Constitution, as under the Confederation."* The oldest assumption-of-liabilities clause in our story. Same reporting entity, two charter restatements. The continuity doctrine makes it explicit: the *state* dates from 1776; the Articles and the Constitution are successive *governments of the same state*.

Hamilton's sprint is the first capitalization: he turns the war debt into the sovereign's first permanent liability (equity in corporate terms — the obligation that doesn't mature), charters the First Bank as the financial subsidiary, and stands up the Mint as the production arm. By 1792 the corporation has articles, a balance sheet, a bank, and a mint.


### Era II: The Bank Wars (1811–1863)

**Corporate analogue: hostile takeover of the financial subsidiary.**

The First Bank's charter expires in 1811 — death by non-renewal (the shareholders voted against recharter by one vote in the Senate). The War of 1812 is fought without a central bank. The Second Bank is chartered in 1816 — re-chartering, new entity, same role, different articles. Jackson kills it in 1832 — the President vetoes the recharter and removes the deposits. This is a hostile takeover of the financial subsidiary by the executive. The "pet bank" experiment that follows (deposits moved to state banks) is decentralization of the money function to state-level subsidiaries.

The lesson for the Entity Theory: **the sovereign can always kill its own subsidiaries.** The bank was statutory. The president killed it anyway, through the removal power (pulling deposits) rather than the legislative power (veto). The 2025–26 pattern — executive action dissolving statutory entities — has a direct precedent in 1832.


### Era III: The Great Consolidation (1863–1944)

**Corporate analogue: vertical integration + mass chartering.**

The sovereign builds its operating architecture in three waves:

1. **The National Banking Act (1863):** Mass chartering. The OCC — the entity that charters other entities — is born on the chartering degree (21° Libra). An entire class of nationally chartered banks is created. The dual banking system (state + national charters) is the sovereign running two parallel subsidiary structures.

2. **The Federal Reserve Act (1913):** The most complex hybrid corporate form in the group. A federal Board is joined to twelve separately incorporated Reserve Banks capitalized by mandatory member-bank statutory subscription stock. The customers hold the instrument, but it is nontransferable, half callable, dividend-capped and stripped of an ordinary residual claim; voting is one member bank per class regardless of shares. The interesting inversion is therefore not conventional parent ownership. It is private statutory capital and bounded regional governance embedded inside a congressionally created public monetary system. See [Federal Reserve Financial Constitution — Source Document](../sources/federal-reserve-financial-constitution-source-document-203118d0ec33.html).

3. **The New Deal / Bretton Woods (1933–1944):** Peak entity creation. SEC, FDIC, FHA, Fannie Mae, the ESF (1934), the IMF and IBRD (1944). The sovereign's response to systemic crisis: charter MORE entities. Each crisis triggers a chartering wave. And in 1933, the sovereign confiscates gold from citizens — restructuring the citizen-entity relationship itself. The shareholders (citizens) are forced to redeem their hard assets (gold) for the sovereign's paper (dollars). In corporate terms: a mandatory share buyback.


### Era IV: Peak State (1944–1999)

**Corporate analogue: conglomerate diversification.**

The postwar sovereign is a conglomerate. HUD, EPA, the whole social-welfare apparatus, the Great Society agencies — each is a new subsidiary chartered to serve a policy function. The entity count explodes. By 1999 the federal group has ~150+ consolidated entities, dozens of disclosure and related-party entities, and a supranational tier (IMF, World Bank, BIS, the regional development banks) where the sovereign is simultaneously parent, shareholder, and member.

The 1971 break (Nixon closes the gold window) is not a chartering event. It is the **dissolution of the supranational subsidiary's operating mandate.** The IMF survives, but Bretton Woods convertibility — the original charter — dies. The sovereign kills the system's operating rules while keeping the institutional shell. The Glass-Steagall repeal (1999) is similar: the sovereign removes the walls between entity types (bank holding companies can now own securities firms). A deconsolidation — entities that were required to be separate can now merge.


### Era V: The Restructuring (2008–present)

**Corporate analogue: leveraged asset strip with incomplete recapitalization.**

This is the speculative heart of the reading. The 2008 crisis and the 2025–26 restructuring are two halves of the same corporate event:

**2008: The hostile consolidation.** The sovereign takes control of entities it does not own. Fannie and Freddie enter conservatorship — the government seizes control of $7 trillion in balance sheet through a mechanism (conservatorship) that SFFAS 47 explicitly says should be treated as a *disclosure* entity, not a consolidation entity. The auditors treat it one way; CBO treats it the other way. Two official scorekeepers disagree about whether the sovereign owns these subsidiaries. The conservatorship is now 18 years old — the longest "temporary" relationship in the group. In corporate terms: a hostile takeover that was never closed.

**2025–26: The asset strip.** The new administration dissolves statutory subsidiaries (SAMHSA, USAID, DOGE, the Dept of Ed) and replaces them with executive-order vehicles (SBR, Digital Asset Stockpile, the phantom Sovereign Wealth Fund, the Administration for a Healthy America). The charter audit's finding: **of thirteen new vehicles, only one has statutory permanence** (the SCRC from the GENIUS Act). The dissolutions are real but the constructions are sand.

Read this in corporate governance terms and the pattern has a name: **a leveraged asset strip with incomplete recapitalization.**

- The acquirer (the new administration) takes control of the parent (the sovereign).
- The acquirer dissolves subsidiaries for their component parts — staff, budget, authority, and especially *assets* (the forfeiture pipeline that feeds the SBR is the clearest example: the sovereign's enforcement arm seizes crypto, which flows into a new Treasury vehicle that has no charter).
- The acquirer stands up new vehicles to hold the extracted value — but the vehicles have no articles of incorporation (EO-only, no statute, no board, no charter).
- The one exception (GENIUS / SCRC) is the one piece that survives a change of control. And it is the one that creates a new **chartering body** — the entity that certifies other entities. The sovereign's corporate instinct is to create chartering bodies, even when everything else is ad hoc.

**The accountant's question:** is this a re-founding (new articles) or a going-concern liquidation (asset-stripping without charter replacement)?

The answer depends on whether the constructions get papered. If the GENIUS Act is the model — statutory, bipartisan, creating a real chartering body — the re-founding may be durable. If the SBR model is the future — EO-only, no entity, reversible — it dissolves at the next change of control. The difference between a new Era I and the end of the corporate lifecycle is whether the articles get restated.

## What this frame CANNOT do

The corporate lens is interpretive, not predictive. It tells you what kind of event you are watching (chartering, dissolution, restructuring, asset strip). It does not tell you what happens next — only the progression clock and the chartering degree offer forward reads, and those sit in the inference layer, not the speculative one.

The frame also cannot tell you **why.** Why is the sovereign restructuring? The political narrative (populist mandate, executive overreach, institutional capture, ideological project) is outside the corporate lens. The Entity Theory deliberately strips the motivation and reads only the action — *what* is being done to the org chart, not *why*. This is its strength (clean, testable) and its limit (it cannot distinguish a reform from a raid).

## Counter-evidence

- **The corporate analogy is just an analogy.** The United States is a sovereign with the power of taxation, war, and law. A corporation has none of these. Reading the 250 years as a "corporate lifecycle" imports assumptions (profit motive, shareholder primacy, fiduciary duty, going-concern assumption) that do not transfer. The sovereign can print its own currency, charter and dissolve its own subsidiaries at will, and change its own articles of incorporation unilaterally. No corporation can do any of this. The analogy holds at the structural level (parent, subsidiary, charter, M&A) and breaks at the level of power.

- **Hamilton's corporate form was contested from the start.** Jefferson and Madison fought the Bank on strict-constructionist grounds. The Anti-Federalists saw the corporate form as exactly what we're calling it — an instrument for concentrating power outside democratic control. Reading Hamilton's template as "the sovereign's natural operating mode" erases the side that lost. It was a political choice, not a natural form.

- **"Leveraged asset strip" is polemical.** The administration's defenders would say this is a **efficiency restructuring** — eliminating redundant subsidiaries, reducing overhead, consolidating duplicated functions. That is also a valid corporate-governance reading. The Entity Theory doesn't (and shouldn't) adjudicate which frame is correct. It can say the *facts* of the restructuring (what is being dissolved, what is being created, which creations have charters and which don't) — but calling it an "asset strip" rather than an "efficiency restructuring" is a judgment call, not a finding.

- **The "building on sand" frame ignores EO durability.** The ESF (1934), CFIUS (1975), USDS (2014), and dozens of other consequential entities were created by executive order and survived decades. EOs are a legitimate governance instrument. The SBR may persist not because it has a charter but because no future president wants to sell the bitcoin. Durability at change of control is an accountant's test, not a political-science test — and the accountant's test may not be the right one for a sovereign.

- **The progression clock is unproven as a predictor.** The shuffle test shows that *dissolving* entities are at FM above chance (p = 0.017). It does NOT show that FM-zone entities will *become* dissolution targets. The forward instrument inverts the finding (from "dying entities are at FM" to "FM entities will die") and that inversion is not logically guaranteed. The scoring week will begin to test it; but until it does, the forward instrument is a hypothesis, not a finding.

- **The corporate lifecycle frame is post-hoc.** Dividing 250 years into five "eras" and naming each one after a corporate phase is a narrative device, not a discovery. You could equally divide the same history into five political eras, five technological eras, or five demographic eras — and each would look like it explained everything. The corporate frame feels revealing because it's Katie's native tongue. That is not evidence; that is affinity.
