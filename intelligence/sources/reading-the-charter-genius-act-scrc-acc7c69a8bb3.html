Reading the Charter — GENIUS Act — SCRC

The 10-point hunt

1. Immunities & liability shields. None captured in this pass — the SCRC is a committee, not a corporate entity, and carries no immunity clause of its own in the sections captured.

2. Tax exemptions. None captured.

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause. The Stablecoin Certification Review Committee isn't a new appointed body at all — it's three sitting officials wearing a fourth hat: the Secretary of the Treasury (Chair), the Chair of the Federal Reserve Board (or the Vice Chair for Supervision, if delegated), and the Chairperson of the FDIC (§2(27)). No new appointment, no new Senate confirmation, no independent term — the SCRC's membership is entirely parasitic on three other offices' existing appointment/removal rules. Whoever controls those three offices controls the SCRC outright, with zero additional friction.

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations. Not addressed in the sections captured — the SCRC has no budget line of its own visible in this material; it operates as an ad hoc committee of existing officials using their home agencies' resources, similar in structure to FSOC.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs. Not directly addressed in the captured sections, though §4(c)(6) requires the Treasury Secretary to publish and maintain, in the Federal Register and on Treasury's website, a running public list of which States have submitted certifications and recertifications — a built-in transparency requirement on the certification process itself, even though the SCRC's internal deliberations are not made public.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses. N/A — ordinary federal statute, amendable by Congress; no treaty-style withdrawal mechanism.

7. Emergency powers. None as such, but §4(c)(7) — "Expedited certifications of existing regulatory regimes" — functions as a fast-track: States that already had a prudential stablecoin/digital-asset regime in place within 180 days of enactment get first-in-line, accelerated review, effectively grandfathering the states (New York's BitLicense regime and similar) that built stablecoin oversight before Washington did.

8. Definitions section quietly load-bearing. §2(22)'s "payment stablecoin" definition does three things at once: it requires the coin be designed as a means of payment/settlement, requires the issuer be obligated to redeem/convert for a fixed monetary value, AND explicitly writes itself out of the federal securities laws — "no bond, note, evidence of indebtedness, or investment contract... shall qualify as a security solely by virtue of its satisfying the conditions" of being a payment stablecoin (§2(22)(B)(iii)). That single carve-out is doing enormous quiet work: it's the sentence that keeps the SEC out of the regulatory picture for compliant stablecoins entirely, handing the whole field to Treasury/OCC/Fed/FDIC/State regulators instead.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution. N/A — the SCRC is a standing committee under the Act, no term limit or sunset identified in the captured sections.

10. Anything strange. The SCRC's supermajority-plus-unanimity structure is the strange one, and it runs in two different registers depending on the stakes. Ordinary SCRC action defaults to a ⅔ vote (§2(27)(B)) — but the two highest-stakes gates in the captured material both require full unanimity instead: (a) a non-financial public company (think: a Big Tech firm, a retailer) wanting to issue its own stablecoin needs a unanimous SCRC vote finding it poses no material risk to the banking system (§4(a)(12), referenced but not captured verbatim here — flagged for follow-up), and (b) approving a State's certification as "substantially similar" to the federal regime requires the Committee to "unanimously" determine the state regime meets federal standards (§4(c)(5)(A)(i)) — meaning any single one of the three members (Treasury, Fed, FDIC) holds an effective veto over every state's stablecoin sovereignty. That's an unusually concentrated veto point for a certification process billed as preserving state-level regulatory choice: the "option" for state regulation in §4(c)(1) is real only if Treasury, the Fed, AND the FDIC all agree the state earned it.

Layer map

  • 2025 — GENIUS Act, Public Law 119-27 (signed July 18, 2025): the whole apparatus in a single act — creates the payment-stablecoin definition and securities-law carve-out (§2), establishes the SCRC as a three-official standing committee (§2(27)), builds the dual-track Federal/State regulatory system with the $10 billion issuance threshold for state-track eligibility and the "substantially similar" certification gate (§4(c)), and preempts State licensing for federally-approved issuers while preserving State consumer-protection law generally (§5(h), §7(f)). No amendments identified as of retrieval date — this is a brand-new statute.

Entity Theory read

The SCRC is a committee born fully armed with veto power but with no staff, no budget, and no independent existence of its own — it's three federal officials (Treasury, Fed, FDIC) borrowing their day-job authority to gatekeep who gets to issue dollar-pegged digital money in America and on whose rules. The "option" Congress gave the states to run their own stablecoin regimes is a mirage unless all three SCRC members sign off unanimously — meaning genuine federalism in this space survives only at the pleasure of whoever holds those three chairs. And the securities-law carve-out buried in the definitions (§2(22)(B)(iii)) is the real coup: it wrote the SEC out of the room entirely for an entire new asset class, handing stablecoin oversight to the bank regulators and Treasury instead of the securities regulators — a jurisdictional land grab dressed as a definitions clause.
