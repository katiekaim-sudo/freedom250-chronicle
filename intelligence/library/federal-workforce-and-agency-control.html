The short version (draft, not yet reviewed by Katie)

Emptying an agency is not the same as abolishing it

These maps separate five things that often get blurred: reclassifying career jobs, removing top officials, whether an agency still legally exists, moving or stopping its work, and a proposed replacement. An agency can lose its staff and work while the law that created it still stands.

What we found

  • Education: the statute (§ 3411) still names the Department. Interagency agreements move administration to other agencies, but those agencies collect the work without taking legal ownership.
  • USAID: the law (§ 6563) still names the agency even though State emptied it. P.L. 119-28 deals with money, not the agency's charter.
  • HHS and SAMHSA still legally exist. The proposed new entity, AHA, is only a request, and P.L. 119-75 still funds HRSA and SAMHSA.
  • Converting jobs to Schedule Policy/Career changes who can be fired without amending any charter. For independent agencies, FTC for-cause protection fell, while a Fed Governor's protection stands for now on the stay.
  • The maps keep four clocks apart: legal existence, staffing, transfer of functions, and money. A press release is not a repeal, and a House-passed bill is not law.

Why it matters

This is the thesis mechanism in plain view: government is being reshaped through staffing rules, funding and agreements between agencies, while the laws that create agencies mostly stay on the books. Because the legal shell remains, what happens next still runs through Congress and the courts.

What to watch

  • pending · Any enacted law that actually repeals or amends the statutes naming Education, USAID or HHS units, as opposed to transfers, cuts or proposals.
  • pending · Merits rulings in the Cook removal case and in Kennedy, which is so far only a preliminary injunction.
