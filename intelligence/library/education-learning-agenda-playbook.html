Education Department gives states a voluntary planning guide

The Department of Education released a voluntary playbook on September 9 that helps state education agencies tie their goals to evidence and to decisions about programs and money. Pilot states improved their planning, but no student gains or legal transfers are shown.

What we found

  • It was built by OESE, IES and the National Comprehensive Center with eight states: Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Kansas, New Hampshire, Tennessee, Utah and Wisconsin.
  • It has six steps, ending with recording a decision to keep, grow, change or stop a program, with a named owner, timing and follow-up checks. Setting up a first agenda is expected to take about three to six months.
  • Georgia surveyed more than 2,600 educators before adding supports for special education teacher retention. Kansas joined several accountability systems into one shared model. Utah used Claude to draft question wording, with people choosing the final text.
  • The fact sheet ties the release to the administration's aim to return education to states and close the department. The playbook itself is voluntary help, not a legal step toward closure.
  • Its capacity scale is for self-reflection only. It is not a ranking, grant test or compliance gate.

Why it matters

It addresses the receiving end of moving federal education work to states: whether states can decide what works and change course. It is a voluntary tool, so it supports the move without carrying out any legal transfer.

What to watch

  • pending · A state publishing an adopted learning agenda with named decision owners and dates.
  • pending · A documented budget, staffing or program decision that came from the process, and outcome data beyond participation counts.
