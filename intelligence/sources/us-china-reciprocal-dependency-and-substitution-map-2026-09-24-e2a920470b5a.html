U.S.–China Reciprocal Dependency and Substitution Map — 2026

Wave: China deepening wave 3 Evidence cutoff: 2026-09-24 Question: Where does either country possess a real choke point, and what must happen before the other can substitute?

Bottom line

The U.S.–China dependency story is not a scoreboard of import shares. It is a stack of product-specific dependencies with different owners, permissions and replacement clocks.

  1. China's strongest industrial leverage is usually in the middle of a chain. Rare-earth separation and magnet making, graphite purification and anodes, battery materials and cells, solar wafers and cells, electronics components and shipyard scale matter more than the location of the original ore or the final assembly label.
  2. The strongest U.S. leverage is concentrated in frontier permissions and hard-certified technology. Advanced chips, semiconductor design software and equipment, commercial-aircraft engines and avionics, and parts of the AI stack are difficult for China to replace quickly. Much of this is actually U.S.-plus-allies leverage, because Taiwan, Korea, Japan and the Netherlands supply indispensable nodes.
  3. Agriculture and hydrocarbon energy are large trades but weaker bilateral choke points. Cargo can often be redirected within a season or contract cycle, though at real price, logistics and income cost. China's energy exposure is primarily to global seaborne routes and prices, not uniquely to U.S. molecules.
  4. Final assembly is often the fastest layer to move and the least informative layer to count. A U.S. battery, module, drone or data center can still contain China-controlled materials and components. Conversely, a China-assembled aircraft can remain dependent on Western engines, avionics and certification.
  5. Qualification is the hidden clock. A technically available material is not a substitute until the customer, regulator, insurer or safety authority accepts it for the particular product and the supplier can deliver it repeatedly.
  6. Inventory buys time; it does not create independence. A reserve or stockpile changes the shock-absorption clock. It does not replace a missing process, workforce, license, type certificate or supplier ecosystem.
  7. The longest dependencies are ecosystems, not commodities. Shipyards, aircraft types, frontier semiconductor toolchains and integrated magnet or battery chains take years to reproduce because they combine capital, tacit knowledge, supplier depth, workforce and qualification.

The governing question is therefore:

Which exact stage fails, who controls the permission to move it, how much qualified inventory exists, and what is the first substitute that can deliver the same function at acceptable scale?

1. Substitution-clock method

These are planning bands, not forecasts. A specific product can fall outside them.

Band Earliest replacement mechanism Typical elapsed time What must be proved
S0 — absorb or reroute Inventory draw, spot purchase, shipment reroute or temporary waiver days–3 months material exists, can legally move and meets the existing specification
S1 — qualify an existing alternate Approved or near-approved supplier, product or route 3–18 months samples, testing, customer approval, logistics and recurring allocation
S2 — expand or adapt capacity Brownfield expansion, new production line, product redesign with limited recertification 18–48 months finance, equipment, workforce, commissioning, yield and customer delivery
S3 — build an integrated chain New mine-to-material, wafer-to-cell, foundry or major industrial platform 4–8 years every upstream stage, infrastructure, permits, skills, qualification and durable orders
S4 — replace an ecosystem New aircraft/engine type, deep shipbuilding base, frontier toolchain or technology architecture 8–15+ years design, industrial learning, certification, fleet or customer conversion and service network

The clock begins at the first unavailable stage, not at the announcement of a factory or award. Construction completion, commissioning, nameplate capacity, qualified output and recurring delivery are separate receipts.

3. Sector findings

A. Rare-earth separation and permanent magnets

The existing Permanent Magnet Research Package is the controlling deep package. Its stage map shows why mine share is not enough: oxide separation, heavy-rare-earth availability, metallization, alloying, powder processing, magnet manufacture and customer qualification are distinct controls.

DOE's stage baseline estimated China's 2020 shares at about 58% of mining, 89% of separation, 90% of metal refining and 92% of magnet-alloy/manufacturing capacity. The percentages are an older baseline, but the stage pattern remains useful. China's April 2025 controls added transaction-level licensing for specified medium and heavy rare earths and related magnet materials. A license is neither an automatic ban nor a guaranteed delivery.

The United States now has real commercial magnet output from Noveon, MP Materials and eVAC, with additional projects commissioning or financed. Those receipts prove a restart. They do not yet prove that every grade, volume, heavy-rare-earth input and customer qualification can be supplied continuously outside China.

Substitution clock: inventory or licensed shipment can bridge a short disruption (S0); an already qualified Japanese or U.S. part may substitute in months (S1); a new integrated separation-to-magnet line is S3; rare-earth-free motor or magnet architectures at fleet scale are S3–S4. Reducing dysprosium/terbium content is not the same as eliminating neodymium-iron-boron dependence.

B. Semiconductors and manufacturing equipment

This chain is not bilateral. Its critical nodes span U.S. design and software, U.S./Japanese/Dutch equipment, Taiwanese and Korean leading-edge fabrication and memory, Chinese mature-node fabrication and packaging, and global electronics assembly.

BIS's current Part 744 controls reach advanced-node integrated circuits, supercomputer and advanced-computing end uses, semiconductor-manufacturing equipment and certain ECAD/TCAD software when the required destination, end-use or knowledge tests are met. In January 2026 BIS moved some accelerators, including specified Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X-class products, to case-by-case review under conditions. That is a permissioned route, not unrestricted supply. BIS also ended license-free VEU treatment for selected foreign-owned fabs in China in 2025; it said licenses could support existing operation but not expansion or technology upgrades.

China nevertheless matters to U.S. electronics through mature-node chips, packaging/test and lower-tier component integration. GAO found U.S. defense visibility into deep semiconductor tiers insufficient and reported that most production and nearly all assembly, packaging and testing occurred overseas, principally across Taiwan, South Korea and China. This is evidence of foreign concentration, not proof that every chip is China-dependent.

Substitution clock: an electrically compatible chip with available software and inventory may be S1; moving a mature-node design and qualifying a foundry is commonly S1–S2; expanding a foundry or packaging line is S2–S3; reproducing the frontier EDA-equipment-foundry ecosystem is S4. The same silicon is not a functional substitute if firmware, model software, interconnect or safety certification fails.

C. Batteries, graphite and conductive materials

China's advantage lies across multiple linked stages. USGS records complete U.S. net import reliance for natural graphite and identifies China as a principal source; its 2026 commodity summary estimates China at 78% of world natural-graphite mine production in 2025. IEA describes an even stronger manufacturing position: China produced more than 80% of global batteries in 2025, while anode-material and several precursor stages were more concentrated still.

The policy-relevant distinction is between ore, processed material, active material, cell and pack. A U.S.-assembled pack with imported anode active material and cells does not close the chain. Nor does changing cell chemistry automatically remove the constraint: LFP reduces nickel and cobalt exposure but increases dependence on a chain in which Chinese firms have scale; higher-silicon anodes may reduce graphite intensity but create new qualification and conductive-network requirements.

China's graphite export-control system is licensing, not a categorical prohibition. MOFCOM said qualified applications could be approved. The control nevertheless adds a permission and timing layer to a physically concentrated chain.

The controlling niche package for carbon-nanotube-enabled graphite reduction is Carbon Nanotubes and Battery Sovereignty. It shows that a promising lab or pilot material is not yet a qualified mass-market substitute.

Substitution clock: alternate cell allocation may be S0–S1; component and chemistry qualification is S1–S2; new refining, anode/cathode active material and cell capacity is S2–S3; a diversified mine-to-cell chain is S3. Ownership and feedstock origin must be tested separately from plant location.

D. Pharmaceuticals, APIs and starting materials

The correct unit is the molecule and manufacturing step, not “pharmaceuticals.” FDA's October 2025 count placed 9% of API manufacturing locations in the United States, 22% in China and 44% in India. That is a facility-location count, not a volume share or an exposure measure for a particular essential medicine.

The deeper risk can sit upstream. FDA has warned that an API reported as manufactured outside China may still depend on Chinese key starting materials or intermediates. ASPR's 2025 industry roundtable reported that more than 70% of APIs used in essential medicines were sourced overseas, primarily from China and India. The White House's 2025 Strategic API Reserve order separately stated that only about 10% of APIs by volume for U.S. finished products were domestically made and directed planning for roughly 26 critical drugs and six months of API supply, subject to funding and execution. A directive is not proof that the reserve is filled.

Substitution clock: on-hand finished dose or API is S0; an already approved alternate site can be S1; adding or changing a source requires process transfer, analytical comparability, regulatory review and reliable precursor supply, usually S2; reconstructing domestic fine-chemical and API capacity is S2–S3. A national aggregate cannot establish the clock for a specific sterile injectable, antibiotic or precursor.

E. Solar photovoltaics

China's concentration rises upstream. IEA's 2026 assessment places China near 80% of module manufacturing and around 90% of wafer and polysilicon capacity, with wafer capacity still more concentrated in some estimates. DOE reports rapid U.S. module growth and the first U.S. silicon-wafer capacity above one gigawatt coming online in the second half of 2025, while warning that polysilicon, ingot/wafer and cell production remain overwhelmingly concentrated in Chinese or Chinese-controlled firms.

This is a manufacturing and project-cost dependency, not an operating-fuel dependency. Once installed, a solar panel does not require a continuing Chinese fuel flow. The risk appears when replacing, expanding or financing capacity and when trade or customs permissions interrupt delivery.

Substitution clock: existing domestic/allied modules can cover some orders in S1; cells and wafers require S2; a resilient cost-competitive polysilicon-to-wafer-to-cell chain is S2–S3. A module-assembly announcement is not proof of upstream independence.

F. Commercial shipbuilding and maritime logistics

USTR's Section 301 finding described China as capable of producing more than 1,700 ships a year while U.S. yards produced fewer than five and ranked nineteenth globally. MARAD's 2026 national-maritime-strategy study similarly describes China-linked ownership of roughly one-fifth of global commercial tonnage, very large container production and an overwhelming shipbuilding scale advantage. Exact denominators differ across sources, but the industrial asymmetry does not depend on a single percentage.

The U.S. response is not a single tariff. Yard capacity, dry docks, naval architects, welders, component suppliers, financing, cargo preference and recurring orders all affect delivered ships. USTR suspended its responsive Section 301 action for one year beginning November 10, 2025 while negotiations continued. Suspension of the action did not erase the investigation or rebuild U.S. capacity.

Substitution clock: a shipper can charter or reroute some cargo in S0–S1, subject to global fleet availability and port/equipment constraints; expanding a yard or repair line is S2–S3; replacing the commercial yard-workforce-supplier ecosystem is S4. A non-Chinese flag or owner does not by itself prove the vessel, cranes, containers and lower-tier equipment are outside Chinese production.

G. Drones and unmanned systems

The drone problem joins several other chains. A platform can carry China-linked batteries, motors, magnets, cameras, radios, printed-circuit assemblies and mature-node chips even when final assembly occurs elsewhere. GAO's 2025 defense-supply-chain work found Chinese lower-tier integration in a system whose visible top-tier suppliers appeared U.S. or European. That is a warning about tier visibility, not a market-share estimate.

The FCC's December 2025 Covered List action and January 2026 Defense exemption affect authorization of specified foreign-produced UAS and critical components. They do not confiscate existing fleets or prove a domestic substitute is available for every mission. The Defense Innovation Board separately used China's 2024 action against Skydio, including battery restrictions, as an example of component dependency reaching a U.S. platform company.

Substitution clock: complete-platform replacement for mapping, inspection or other bounded missions can be S1 if a qualified product exists; secure components and scaled manufacturing are S2–S3; specialized payloads, autonomy integration and defense qualification may extend the clock. Procurement approval and radio authorization are separate from industrial capacity.

H. Agriculture

China is an important buyer, especially for bulk commodities, but this is a more substitutable chain than magnets or aircraft engines. USDA reports that U.S. agricultural exports to China fell to $8.4 billion in 2025 and China dropped out of the five largest country markets, while Mexico, Canada, the European Union and Japan were larger. That reduces, but does not remove, exposure for particular crops and regions.

China can source soybeans from Brazil, which USDA describes as supplying about 60% of world exports while China accounts for more than 60% of world imports. Seasonal windows, protein/oil characteristics, freight, storage and price still matter. U.S. farmers can redirect fungible commodities, but a new buyer may clear only at a lower basis and may not absorb the same volume at the same time.

Substitution clock: cargo diversion is S0; a crop-marketing season is S1; durable changes in crushing, storage, rail/port and customer patterns are S2. The dependency is often income and price exposure rather than physical inability to produce or consume.

I. Commercial aviation

The May 2026 package exposes a genuine reciprocal dependency. China approved an initial purchase of 200 Boeing aircraft, while the United States said it would maintain supply guarantees for aircraft engines and parts. China's account likewise joined aircraft purchases to U.S. engine and component supply. The commercial bargain therefore contains separate buyer, airframe and component clocks.

U.S. and allied leverage is deepest in certified engines, avionics, components and continued support. FAA records the LEAP engine family as serving the COMAC C919 as well as Airbus and Boeing narrowbodies, and the EAR applies case-by-case controls to specified hot-section engine technology. China has leverage as a major customer and manufacturing market; Boeing, Airbus and their suppliers also face global engine, material and workforce constraints. GAO found that the two large Western aircraft manufacturers and suppliers were already struggling to raise output and that airlines faced shortages of engines and other parts.

Substitution clock: parts inventory and schedule changes are S0–S1; shifting an order between Boeing and Airbus is constrained by backlog and fleet commonality and is generally S2–S3; changing a certified engine/avionics architecture or replacing the global service ecosystem is S4. A domestically assembled airframe is not independent if its engine and critical systems remain foreign and permissioned.

J. Hydrocarbon energy and the clean-energy cross-link

Direct U.S.–China oil and gas trade is not a durable bilateral choke point. EIA records China as the world's largest crude importer, at 11.6 million barrels per day in 2025. That creates exposure to global prices, producers, tankers and maritime chokepoints. It does not mean China uniquely depends on U.S. crude. China's 2025 LNG imports fell while pipeline and domestic production rose; U.S. LNG simultaneously reached a widening global buyer base.

The bilateral dependency is stronger in the equipment and materials needed to change the energy system: batteries, graphite, solar, rare-earth magnets and selected power electronics. Financial sanctions, shipping restrictions and insurance can create U.S.-linked legal leverage, but that is different from physical control of the fuel molecule.

Substitution clock: crude and LNG cargoes can often be redirected in S0–S1, with price and shipping consequences; pipelines, terminals and generation fleets are S2–S3; maritime geography is not substitutable. Clean-energy equipment inherits the longer battery, solar and magnet clocks above.

K. Cloud and AI infrastructure

This stack has four different layers: model/software, chips and servers, data-center operation, and power/cooling/grid equipment. They should not be collapsed.

BIS controls reach advanced-computing chips and servers, certain cloud-provider and in-country transfers, advanced-node design software and specified support when the regulatory tests are met. Its May 2025 guidance expressly addressed foreign infrastructure-as-a-service providers training models for China-headquartered parties, and current rules contain separate treatment for chips, model weights, IaaS and end users. January 2026 case-by-case treatment for certain accelerators created a conditional purchase route, not a general entitlement.

The United States and allies possess strong frontier-chip, hyperscale cloud and model leverage. Yet U.S. AI infrastructure still depends on foreign fabs and on physical inputs shared with other sectors. The White House AI Action Plan identifies chips, data centers and energy as a single buildout problem. DOE reports transformer lead times that had risen from three-to-six months in 2019 to 12-to-30 months in 2023 and separately identifies bottlenecks in transformers, cooling and large power equipment. China-linked solar, batteries, magnets and electronics can therefore enter the physical AI stack even when the cloud operator and model are American.

Substitution clock: a user may move to an allowed chip, model or cloud in S1 if software, data governance and capacity permit; server and data-center expansion is S2; new grid interconnection, generation and secure hardware supply are S2–S3; sovereign frontier compute with a fully diversified semiconductor and power chain is S3–S4. Cloud access does not substitute for legal authority to transfer chips, model weights or controlled technical support.

4. What the comparison changes

The strongest Chinese choke points

  • rare-earth separation, heavy-rare-earth processing and magnet scale;
  • graphite purification/anodes, LFP-linked materials and battery-cell scale;
  • solar wafers/cells and cost-setting manufacturing scale;
  • commercial shipbuilding, containers and maritime industrial depth;
  • drone and electronics lower tiers that are difficult to see from the prime contractor.

The strongest U.S. or U.S.-plus-allies choke points

  • frontier chips, EDA and semiconductor manufacturing equipment;
  • high-end accelerators, hyperscale cloud/model ecosystems and related permissions;
  • commercial-aircraft engines, avionics, parts, certification and service;
  • access to the U.S. market, dollar finance and regulatory approvals, which are legal permissions rather than physical inputs.

The most overstated dependencies

  • “China owns rare earths.” It dominates key processing and magnet stages; it does not own all deposits.
  • “China makes our medicines.” Exposure is molecule- and step-specific, with India and third countries central to many chains.
  • “The U.S. can cut China off from all chips.” Controls are product-, destination-, end-use-, end-user- and knowledge-specific, and allied/foreign-produced items require their own jurisdictional test.
  • “China can stop buying U.S. agriculture and the crop disappears.” The revenue shock can be severe, but bulk commodities can move through other buyers and prices.
  • “China depends on U.S. energy.” China's larger vulnerability is the global seaborne system; U.S. clean-tech exposure to Chinese manufacturing is more durable than China's dependence on U.S. fuel.
  • “Domestic final assembly means the supply chain is domestic.” It may leave the controlled middle untouched.

5. Cross-sector return gates

  1. Product-level bills of material and tier-two/tier-three ownership for drones, defense electronics and data-center equipment.
  2. Qualified inventory and lead-time data, not only capacity announcements, for magnets, graphite, battery materials, solar cells and transformers.
  3. Current customer qualification and recurring-delivery evidence for new U.S. magnet, graphite/anode, battery, wafer and cell facilities.
  4. Molecule-level API, key-starting-material and alternate-site maps for the critical-drug list.
  5. A public Board of Trade product docket showing whether reciprocal tariff treatment changes any of these chains.
  6. License application, approval, shipment and end-use receipts under the 2026 advanced-chip policy.
  7. COMAC aircraft/component content and certified alternate-engine/avionics progress, separated from purchase announcements.
  8. U.S. shipyard delivery, workforce, dry-dock and supplier receipts after the temporary suspension of the Section 301 response.
  9. Actual non-China drone-component qualification at scale, not only approved-platform listings.
  10. Price and delivered-output tests: whether new non-China capacity operates when China-origin product remains cheaper.

Governing boundary

This is an orientation-level dependency map. It completes Priority 3 at the cross-sector level but does not replace the controlling technical packages or a product-specific bill-of-material audit. Every claim must still be tested through:

function -> exact stage -> owner/control -> legal permission -> inventory -> qualified alternate -> finance/build -> recurring delivery

Country share, plant nameplate, award, construction start and first sample are not interchangeable with delivered, customer-qualified output.
