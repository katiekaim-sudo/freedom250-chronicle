# Reading the Charter — International Organizations Immunities Act

Source text: `1945 - International Organizations Immunities Act (verbatim).md` in this folder. Every claim below cites the exact current U.S. Code section (and, where relevant, the original 1945 act section number in parentheses).

## 1. Immunities & liability shields

This is the whole point of the Act, and it's broader than the IMF's own Article IX class — §288a(b): international organizations, their property, and their assets, "wherever located, and by whomsoever held," get "the same immunity from suit and every form of judicial process as is enjoyed by foreign governments" — full sovereign-immunity-equivalent status, not a narrower carve-out, and it attaches automatically to EVERY organization the President designates, not just the IMF/World Bank. §288a(c) adds search and confiscation immunity plus one absolute clause with no foreign-government analogy needed: "The archives of international organizations shall be inviolable" — full stop, no qualifier. §288d(b) extends personal immunity from suit to the individual officers, employees, and foreign-government representatives themselves, for official acts — this is what shields staff of the IMF, World Bank, WHO, etc. personally, not just the institutions.

## 2. Tax exemptions

§288a(d): customs duties, internal-revenue import taxes, foreign-agent registration, and official-communications treatment are all set at whatever level foreign governments get "under similar circumstances." §288c: flat exemption from "all property taxes imposed by, or under the authority of, any Act of Congress" — including D.C. and territorial property taxes specifically named. §288b: officers'/employees' personal baggage and effects come in duty-free and tax-free on arrival, administered by Customs and Border Protection under Treasury-approved regulations.

## 3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — the control map

This is the load-bearing structural fact of the whole Act: §288 gives the PRESIDENT, alone, by executive order, the power to (a) designate any qualifying organization as entitled to these immunities, (b) "withhold or withdraw" any privilege from any organization at his discretion "in the light of the functions performed," and (c) "revoke the designation of any international organization... at any time" if he judges there's been abuse "or for any other reason" — that last clause is doing enormous work: no cause requirement, no standard, no judicial review contemplated. This is a single-person on/off switch for the immunity status of dozens of international bodies, including the IMF, World Bank, WHO, WTO, and the UN itself. On personnel: §288e gives the SECRETARY OF STATE the parallel gatekeeper role over individual humans — no one gets immunity benefits without State Department notification/acceptance (§288e(a)), and State can strip an individual's benefits and order them to leave "should the Secretary of State determine that the continued presence... is not desirable" (§288e(b)) — no cause standard there either, just Secretary's determination.

## 4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations

Not addressed in this Act at all — it is purely a status/immunity/privilege statute, silent on funding mechanics. Each designated organization's money story lives in its own founding instrument (see the IMF, IBRD, WHO, etc. charter folders).

## 5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

Nothing explicit — no GAO exemption or FOIA carve-out language appears anywhere in this subchapter. The practical audit-avoidance is downstream of §288a(c)'s "archives... shall be inviolable" clause: if the archives are inviolable, ordinary U.S. investigative or audit process can't reach them regardless of what any other statute says, without a waiver.

## 6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

No withdrawal mechanism (an organization doesn't "withdraw" — it's the President's designation that's revocable, under §288, unilaterally, as noted above). Amendment: this Act has been amended by add-on section after add-on section since 1945 — every organization named in §§288f-1 through 288l required its OWN separate Act of Congress or Act-provision to be swept into the immunity regime (European Space Agency 1966, African Union 1973/2004, ICRC 1987, IUCN 1994, European Central Bank 2002, Global Fund 2004, OHR Bosnia/ICO Kosovo 2010). The base machine (§§288-288f) has needed almost no amendment since 1945 — it's the LIST of who plugs into it that keeps growing, not the mechanism itself.

## 7. Emergency powers

The closest analog is §288's "abuse... or for any other reason" revocation trigger — an emergency-adjacent, discretionary kill switch rather than a defined emergency-powers section. There's no "unusual and exigent circumstances" language anywhere in this Act (contrast the Federal Reserve Act's §13(3)) — the President's power here is not conditioned on any emergency at all; it's just a standing, unconditional grant.

## 8. The definitions section — quietly load-bearing

§288's definition of "international organization" is doing the entire Act's scoping work: it requires (1) U.S. participation via treaty or Act of Congress, AND (2) presidential designation by executive order. Both prongs are needed — an organization the U.S. joins by treaty gets NOTHING under this Act until the President separately designates it. This means Congress joining an org and the President extending it immunity are two SEPARATE decisions requiring two separate acts of government — a quiet built-in check, even though the designation decision itself (once the treaty/statute prong is satisfied) is entirely the President's to make or withhold. §288e(c) is a second quietly load-bearing definitional move: "No person shall... be considered as receiving diplomatic status" — meaning everything granted under this Act is legally NOT diplomatic immunity, just a parallel, statute-created immunity regime that happens to mirror diplomatic treatment in most particulars. That distinction matters for how courts analyze claims under this Act versus the Vienna Convention.

## 9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

No general dissolution clause for the Act itself (it's a standing statute, not an entity). One section explicitly contemplates an entity's dissolution and immunity SURVIVING it: §288f-7 (OHR Bosnia/ICO Kosovo) — "Any such extension may provide for the provisions of this subchapter to continue to extend... after that Office has been dissolved." That is genuinely strange for an immunity regime: Congress explicitly authorized immunity protections to outlive the organization they were granted to, presumably to protect against post-dissolution liability claims against former officers/archives.

## 10. Anything strange — the clause you'd never expect

Two things jump out. First, the sheer promiscuity of the designation list under §288 — INTERPOL (with "limited privileges" flagged specifically), the Multinational Force and Observers (a peacekeeping body in the Sinai), the Israel-United States Binational Industrial Research and Development Foundation, and the ITER International Fusion Energy Organization all sit on the exact same legal footing as the IMF and the UN — one 1945 statute has become the universal adapter plug for immunity to virtually any international body Washington wants to host or join, regardless of size or mission. Second, the revocation list is itself a small graveyard of Cold War history: the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO) and the Organization of African Unity both got their immunity designations formally revoked decades after the organizations themselves had effectively dissolved or been superseded — the statute's bookkeeping outlives the politics by years or decades.

## Layer map

- **1945 — International Organizations Immunities Act (act of Dec. 29, 1945, ch. 652, title I, 59 Stat. 669).** Creates the core machine: presidential designation power (§1/§288), the immunity/status package (§2/§288a), customs/property-tax exemptions (§§3, 6/§288b-c), personal immunities for representatives/officers/employees and their families (§7/§288d), Secretary of State's gatekeeper and deportation power over individual beneficiaries (§8/§288e), and the reciprocity-override plus State Department withdrawal power (§9/§288f). Same Congress, same year as the Bretton Woods Agreements Act (July 1945) — this Act generalizes to every future international body the narrower immunity grant BWAA §11 gave specifically to the IMF and World Bank five months earlier.
- **→ Organization of American States extension (act of July 10, 1952).** First "bolt-on" — extends diplomatic-level (not IOIA-level) privileges to OAS member-state representatives, a different and higher tier of protection than the base Act (§288g).
- **→ Pub. L. 89-353 (1966).** Adds §288f-1, the first of the org-specific extension sections (European Space Agency), establishing the template every later addition follows.
- **→ Pub. L. 93-161 (1973), amended 2004/2007.** Adds §288f-2, extending to the Organization of African Unity/African Union — later amended as OAU dissolved into the AU, showing the statute tracking a real-world institutional replacement.
- **→ Pub. L. 100-204 (1987).** Adds §288f-3, sweeping in the International Committee of the Red Cross — notable because the ICRC is not a treaty-based intergovernmental body in the ordinary sense, showing how flexible the "international organization" gate can be stretched.
- **→ Pub. L. 111-177 (2010).** Adds §288f-7, the most recent core-subchapter addition, and the one explicit post-dissolution-survival clause (OHR Bosnia/ICO Kosovo) — the newest wrinkle in an 80-year-old statute.

## Entity Theory read

The International Organizations Immunities Act is not itself a creature with a board or a balance sheet — it's a light switch panel, and the President's hand is the only one on it. Every organization on the list in §288's executive-order notes — from the IMF down to the ITER fusion project — is running on borrowed sovereign-immunity status that Congress pre-authorized once, in 1945, and then handed entirely to the executive branch to flip on and off, org by org, forever, with a "for any other reason" standard that amounts to no standard at all. Who really holds the leash: the President over institutional designation (§288), the Secretary of State over which individual humans get to claim the benefit (§288e) — Congress's only remaining lever is the one-time act of adding a NEW organization to the eligible universe (which is why every fresh addition since 1966 needed its own statute), but once an org is IN, its day-to-day immunity status is a pure executive-branch dial. Does it survive a change of control? Trivially yes for the machine itself — it's outlived eleven administrations of both parties without a single substantive rewrite of its core sections — but for any GIVEN organization on the list, survival is only as durable as the current President's willingness to leave the designation alone, which is precisely the kind of lever that gets pointed at in disputes over an international body's standing when a new administration wants leverage over it.
