# Reading the Charter — FDIC

The FDIC is not a deposit insurer that happens to have police powers. It is a receivership machine with a marketing department. The insurance is the part depositors see; the part that actually runs the American banking system is subsection (d) — twenty paragraphs of "the Corporation may" that turn the FDIC into a bank's new owner, court-proof, the instant that bank fails. Read the two verbatim files in this folder side by side: the 1933 original (sliding-scale haircuts, three-man board, appropriated capital) and the current codified text (full guarantee framing, five-member board, receivership superpowers) are not the same machine wearing a new coat of paint. They are two different designs that happen to share a name.

## 1. Immunities & liability shields

- **Court-stripping on receivership actions.** 12 U.S.C. §1821(j): "Except as provided in this section, no court may take any action, except at the request of the Board of Directors by regulation or order, to restrain or affect the exercise of powers or functions of the Corporation as a conservator or a receiver." One sentence, total effect: the FDIC, once it appoints itself (or is appointed) receiver of a failed bank, cannot be enjoined by any court unless the FDIC's own Board asks for that to happen. The safety valve on the FDIC's receivership power is the FDIC.
- **No attachment or execution against receivership assets.** §1821(d)(13)(C): "No attachment or execution may issue by any court upon assets in the possession of the receiver." A judgment creditor can win in court and still get nothing — the assets sit behind glass the court cannot break.
- **Same shield again, broader, when acting as receiver generally.** §1825(b)(2): "No property of the Corporation shall be subject to levy, attachment, garnishment, foreclosure, or sale without the consent of the Corporation, nor shall any involuntary lien attach to the property of the Corporation."
- **Exemption from criminal prosecution for the failed bank's own pre-receivership conduct.** §1825(b)(4): the Corporation "shall be exempt from all prosecution by the United States or any State, county, municipality, or local authority for any criminal offense... which was allegedly committed by the institution, or persons acting on behalf of the institution, prior to the appointment of the Corporation as receiver." The FDIC inherits the bank's assets and liabilities but explicitly not its criminal exposure.
- **Individual employees shielded from Securities Act liability on asset-disposition deals.** §1812(f)(1): a director, member, officer, or employee of the Corporation "has no liability under the Securities Act of 1933... with respect to any claim arising out of or resulting from any act or omission... in connection with any transaction involving the disposition of assets... by the Corporation" — carved back only for criminal acts, willful/malicious misconduct, or private gain.
- **No bond, no filing fees, ever.** §1819(b)(4): the Corporation "shall not be required to post any bond to pursue any appeal and shall not be subject to payments of any filing fees in United States district courts or courts of appeal."

## 2. Tax exemptions

- **Corporate self-exemption, both as insurer and as receiver.** §1825(a): the Corporation itself — "its franchise, its capital, reserves, and surplus, and its income" — is exempt from all federal, territorial, state, county, municipal, and local taxation, real property carved back in. §1825(b)(1) restates the same exemption specifically for whenever the Corporation is acting as receiver, with an added twist: even a taxpayer's *failure* to challenge a state property-tax assessment doesn't waive the Corporation's right to dispute the valuation later.
- **Obligations exempt too, but with a catch on the resale/gain side.** §1825(a): notes, debentures, bonds issued by the Corporation are tax-exempt as to principal and interest (except estate/inheritance tax) — but "interest upon or any income from any such obligations and gain from the sale or other disposition of such obligations shall not have any exemption, as such." The wrapper is exempt; profit made trading the wrapper is not.
- **No penalties, no fines, no filing fees — even for a bank's own missed tax deadlines.** §1825(b)(3): the Corporation "shall not be liable for any amounts in the nature of penalties or fines" arising from a failure to pay real property, personal property, probate, or recording taxes when due.

## 3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — the control map

- **Five-member Board today, three-member Board in 1933.** Current §1812(a)(1): Comptroller of the Currency (ex officio), Director of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (ex officio, added by Dodd-Frank), and three Senate-confirmed presidential appointees (one must have State bank supervisory experience). No more than three members of the same political party after Feb. 28, 1993 (§1812(a)(2)). Compare the 1933 original (Banking Act of 1933 §12B(b), verbatim file): Comptroller of the Currency plus **two** presidential appointees, $10,000/year salary, "not more than two... members of the same political party."
- **Six-year terms, staggered, holdover until replaced.** §1812(c): appointed members serve six years; a member filling a vacancy serves only the remainder of the term; all appointed members "may continue to serve after the expiration of the term... until a successor has been appointed and qualified" — meaning a President who stalls on nominations doesn't get to empty the Board by attrition.
- **Two-year post-employment cooling-off, waived only for those who serve a full term.** §1812(e)(1): no Board member may hold any office, position, or employment at any insured depository institution or holding company for two years after leaving the Board — unless they served their full appointed term, in which case the restriction doesn't apply at all. A perverse incentive: serve the full six years and walk straight into a bank job; leave early and you're frozen out for two years.
- **No stock, no directorships, sworn certification required.** §1812(e)(2)-(3): Board members cannot be officers or directors of, or hold stock in, any insured depository institution, holding company, Federal Reserve bank, or Federal Home Loan Bank — and must certify compliance under oath on taking office.
- **Removal-for-cause is not stated anywhere in what's captured here.** Neither the 1933 text nor the current §1812 spells out a removal standard for the appointed Board members — a real gap, and a live constitutional question elsewhere in federal law (for-cause removal protections for multi-member independent agency boards have been litigated up to the Supreme Court in adjacent contexts). Flagging rather than guessing: §1812 does not answer "removable by the President for any reason, or only for cause" — that would need to be checked against Humphrey's Executor-line case law, not the charter text itself.

## 4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations

- **The Deposit Insurance Fund is the FDIC's own balance sheet, not congressional appropriations, and the statute puts a hard ceiling on how much the Corporation can owe against it.** §1825(c)(5): the Corporation "may not issue or incur any obligation, if... the aggregate amount of obligations of the Deposit Insurance Fund[] outstanding would exceed the sum of" (A) cash on hand, (B) 90% of the estimated fair market value of the Fund's other assets, and (C) whatever's authorized to be borrowed from Treasury under §1824(a). Self-funding with a borrowing backstop, not appropriations in the ordinary course.
- **But every dollar spent resolving a bank is capped by the "least-cost resolution" test — unless the systemic-risk escape hatch fires (see item 7).** §1823(c)(4)(A): the Corporation "may not exercise any authority under this subsection... unless... the total amount of the expenditures... is the least costly to the Deposit Insurance Fund of all possible methods." This is the ordinary-times fiscal discipline on the fund — and it is the exact rule that gets suspended in a systemic-risk declaration.
- **Any obligation issued after FIRREA carries an explicit federal guarantee.** §1825(d): "The full faith and credit of the United States is pledged to the payment of any obligation issued after August 9, 1989, by the Corporation" — so while the Fund itself is meant to run on premiums/assessments (the exact assessment mechanics of §1817 were not captured this session — see Gaps below), the paper it issues carries the same guarantee language as Treasury debt.

## 6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

- **Perpetual by statute, dissolvable only by a new Act of Congress.** §1819(a), Second: the Corporation shall "have succession until dissolved by an Act of Congress" — identical language appears in the 1933 original (Banking Act of 1933 §12B(j), Second, verbatim file). No sunset, no shareholder vote, no regulator wind-up power.

## 7. Emergency powers — what unlocks in "unusual and exigent circumstances" and who decides

- **The systemic-risk exception is the single most important clause in this charter.** §1823(c)(4)(G)(i): notwithstanding the ordinary least-cost and no-uninsured-depositor-protection rules, if the FDIC's own Board (two-thirds vote), the Federal Reserve's Board of Governors (two-thirds vote), and the Secretary of the Treasury (in consultation with the President) all determine that following the ordinary rules "would have serious adverse effects on economic conditions or financial stability," the Corporation may take "other action or assistance" to wind up a failed bank "as necessary to avoid or mitigate such effects" — i.e., protect uninsured depositors and creditors beyond what the ordinary rules allow, when the failure is judged too disruptive to let play out normally.
- **The bill gets paid by the industry after the fact, not by Congress.** §1823(c)(4)(G)(ii): losses from a systemic-risk action are recovered through special assessments on insured depository institutions and/or their holding companies — a self-funded bailout mechanism, structurally similar to how the ordinary Fund works, just triggered under emergency conditions.
- **Oversight is after-the-fact and thin.** Treasury must document the determination and notify two congressional committees within three days (§1823(c)(4)(G)(v)) — notice, not pre-approval. GAO reviews and reports afterward (§1823(c)(4)(G)(iv)). No court, no Congress, and no depositor gets a vote before the decision is made — only a two-thirds vote of two federal boards plus one Cabinet secretary consulting the President.
- **No one can create a workaround by implication.** §1823(c)(4)(H): "No provision of law shall be construed as permitting the Corporation to take any action prohibited by paragraph (4) unless such provision expressly provides, by direct reference to this paragraph, that this paragraph shall not apply." This is a statutory lock against accidental repeal — any future law trying to loosen the least-cost rule has to say so by name.

## 9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

- Covered in item 6: perpetual existence, Congress-only dissolution, unchanged in substance from the 1933 original to today (Banking Act of 1933 §12B(j) Second → current §1819(a) Second).
- **Recordkeeping has an expiration date, though the Corporation doesn't.** §1821(d)(15)(D): after a 6-year period following appointment as receiver, the FDIC may destroy records of the failed institution it "determines to be unnecessary" — and may destroy records already 10+ years old immediately, without waiting out the 6-year clock. The Corporation is immortal; the paper trail of any given failure is not.

## 10. Anything strange — the clause you'd never expect

- **The FDIC can claw back a bank insider's asset transfers made up to five years before a receivership, and its clawback right beats a bankruptcy trustee's.** §1821(d)(17): the Corporation, as receiver, may avoid any transfer made within five years of its appointment if made "with the intent to hinder, delay, or defraud" the institution, the Corporation, or a banking regulator — and (D): these rights are "superior to any rights of a trustee or any other party (other than any party which is a Federal agency) under title 11" — i.e., beats ordinary bankruptcy-law fraudulent-transfer clawbacks, which typically look back only 1-4 years depending on the theory.
- **The FDIC can get a freeze-the-assets court order without proving irreparable harm — a lower bar than any other litigant gets.** §1821(d)(18)-(19): the Corporation can ask any court for a Rule 65 injunction/asset-freeze order, and that request "shall apply... without regard to the requirement of such rule that the applicant show that the injury, loss, or damage is irreparable and immediate." Ordinary plaintiffs have to prove they'll be irreparably hurt without an injunction. The FDIC doesn't.
- **"Incidental powers" is written as a blank check.** §1821(d)(2)(J): the Corporation may "take any action authorized by this chapter" that it determines "is in the best interests of the depository institution, its depositors, or the Corporation" — the Corporation is both the actor and the judge of whether its own action is justified.
- **The receiver can bind a bank's directors and officers to gross-negligence liability under state common law, retroactively assigned via lawsuits the FDIC itself purchases.** §1821(k): directors/officers can be held "personally liable for monetary damages" in suits brought by, or assigned to, the Corporation, for gross negligence "as such terms are defined and determined under applicable State law" — the federal receiver borrowing fifty different state liability standards depending on where the failed bank sat.
- **The 1933 original was a sliding-scale haircut scheme, not a guarantee.** Per the verbatim file's own annotation: the original 1933 §12B(l) insured 100% of the first $10,000, 75% of the next tier to $50,000, and 50% above that — the opposite of today's "your first $250,000 is fully safe" framing. Deposit insurance was invented to make big depositors eat a loss, not to protect them fully.

## Layer map — organic act through today

1. **1933 — Banking Act of 1933 (Glass-Steagall Act), §8, adding §12B to the Federal Reserve Act.** Created the FDIC as a temporary insurance-fund vehicle: three-member Board (Comptroller + two Senate-confirmed appointees), $150,000,000 appropriated U.S. capital stock plus mandatory Class A (member-bank) and Class B (Federal Reserve Bank) stock subscriptions, sliding-scale coverage (100%/75%/50% by tier), effective July 1, 1934.
2. **1935 — Banking Act of 1935.** Replaced the 1933 temporary plan with a permanent insurance fund (not separately captured verbatim in this folder — noted in the 1933 file's own capture note as the point where the original text was "substantially rewritten").
3. **1950 — Federal Deposit Insurance Act (act of Sept. 21, 1950, ch. 967, 64 Stat. 873).** Withdrew §12B from the Federal Reserve Act entirely and re-enacted it as its own freestanding statute — the FDIC's charter is no longer a subsection of someone else's law. This is the current codification base, 12 U.S.C. Chapter 16, §1811 ff.
4. **1989 — FIRREA (Pub. L. 101-73).** Overhauled corporate powers and litigation posture: rewrote §1819's sue-and-be-sued clause (added "by and through its own attorneys," removed the old blanket no-attachment/no-execution language from that specific paragraph and relocated equivalent protection into the receivership subsection), expanded §1821(d)'s conservator/receiver powers substantially, added the current §1825(c)/(d) borrowing-limit and full-faith-and-credit provisions, added §1812(f)'s Securities Act immunity for Corporation personnel.
5. **1991 — FDICIA (FDIC Improvement Act, Pub. L. 102-242).** Added the least-cost-resolution requirement and, as its most consequential piece, the systemic-risk exception now at §1823(c)(4) — Congress's direct legislative response to the late-1980s pattern of regulators treating large banks as too big to let fail without a formal legal mechanism to say so out loud.
6. **2010 — Dodd-Frank Act (Pub. L. 111-203), Title I and Title II.** Title I expanded the Board to five members by adding the CFPB Director (current §1812(a)(1)(B)). **Title II created an entirely separate resolution authority — Orderly Liquidation Authority (OLA), 12 U.S.C. Chapter 53 — NOT part of Chapter 16 and NOT captured in this file.** OLA extends FDIC-style receivership mechanics to systemically important non-bank financial companies (the entities the FDIC's ordinary bank-receivership powers under §1821 don't reach). Worth flagging as the modern legislative descendant of the systemic-risk exception: where §1823(c)(4)(G) is an emergency escape hatch bolted onto bank-specific receivership, Dodd-Frank Title II is a whole second receivership regime built for the non-bank case Lehman Brothers exposed in 2008.

## Entity Theory read

The FDIC is what happens when a government builds a fire department and then writes into its charter that the fire department cannot be sued while putting out a fire, cannot be stopped by a judge except at its own request, and gets to decide for itself whether the fire counts as "systemic" enough to skip the usual rulebook. It answers to a five-person board that is majority-independent-appointee but includes two sitting federal officials by name (Comptroller, CFPB Director) — so it never fully leaves the executive branch's orbit even while insisting it runs on premiums, not appropriations. Who really holds the leash: in ordinary times, the Board itself, bound by its own least-cost rule; in a real crisis, that leash is handed to the Treasury Secretary and the Federal Reserve's Board of Governors jointly, with the President consulted but Congress only informed after the fact. Does it survive a change of control: absolutely — perpetual by statute since 1933, un-killable except by a future Congress, its personnel protected from removal-for-cause questions the charter itself never quite answers. This is not a passive insurance company. It is the entity a bank becomes the instant it dies, wearing the government's own credit rating, immune from the courts that would otherwise referee the estate.
