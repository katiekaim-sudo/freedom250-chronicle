Oil and gas tightness is selective, and many deals are not yet flows

The Middle East war has made Hormuz, the Red Sea and bypass routes the main supply limit. Diesel is truly tight, but U.S. crude is not, and many announced deals (like the Alaska LNG-Korea plan) have not yet become real shipments.

What we found

  • In June 2026 the U.S. exported 4.735 million barrels a day of crude and 7.805 million of petroleum products; first-half LNG exports averaged 17.4 Bcf a day. The U.S. is a major exporter with or without Venezuela.
  • Ten U.S. diesel export lanes made up about 0.953 million barrels a day in June, 66.5% of distillate exports, led by Mexico, Brazil and the Netherlands. Most go out from the Gulf Coast.
  • Venezuela produces about 1.15-1.20 million barrels a day, and the latest early U.S. receipt figure was about 0.60 million. OFAC permissions, U.S.-controlled payment accounts and Chevron and Eni operating agreements now exist.
  • The roughly $54 billion Alaska LNG-Korea announcement is at project-selection stage only. Korea said participation was not final, and there is no final investment decision, construction or gas flow.
  • Lifting Russia sanctions would first cut shipping and payment friction and reroute crude already selling. It would not repair damaged Russian refineries, and the production-to-capacity gap is about 0.94 million barrels a day.

Why it matters

It shows that energy policy changes travel through permits, licenses, payment accounts and contracts before oil actually moves. Announcements and permissions are steps in that machinery, not delivered supply.

What to watch

  • pending · Korea's formal decision on Alaska LNG participation, financing and a final investment decision.
  • pending · Named PetroChina-Sabine Pass LNG cargos tied to the China trade framework.
  • pending · Recovery of Gulf crude and product trade toward pre-war levels in IEA and EIA data.

Research summary

A multi-wave physical, commercial, identity, permission, payment and observation system for the Iran/Middle East war shock, Russian crude and product corridors, Venezuelan deal execution, U.S. production-processing-trading hub, global replacement supply and LNG. The package includes a validated working globe projecting governed routes without treating forecasts, permissions or targets as observed movement. The September 30 Alaska LNG update adds the federally authorized North Slope-to-Nikiski project and the future Korea export lane, but keeps the new roughly $54 billion political announcement at project-selection state: Korea said participation was not finalized, and no final investment instrument, FID, construction start or operating flow was established. Diesel has a dedicated network: ten material June 2026 U.S. destination lanes represent about 0.953 million barrels per day, while Russian rerouting and Northwest Europe replacement routes are retained only as historical baselines. A new supplier test finds direct Gulf replacement exposure in the United Kingdom and Netherlands, combined Russia/Gulf displacement in Brazil and mostly indirect competition for U.S. barrels across the selected Latin American buyers. The June U.S. network is overwhelmingly supplied from PADD 3/Gulf Coast, with PADD 5 support to Mexico, Chile and Peru. The current shortage is selective: global inventories and diesel/distillate are genuinely tight, while U.S. commercial crude is nearly flat year over year and jet, propane and forecast natural-gas storage do not show a national shortage. The United States already exports at globally material scale with or without Venezuela. Removing Russia sanctions would first lower shipping and payment friction and reroute crude already reaching the market; the public arithmetic production-to-capacity gap is about 0.94 million barrels per day and refinery damage still constrains the product most urgently needed.
