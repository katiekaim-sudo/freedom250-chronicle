Banks and Credit Unions — Federal and Four-State Legal Architecture

Research ID: bank-credit-union-legal-architecture
Artifact role: foundational orientation / comparative legal architecture
Evidence cutoff: September 15, 2026
Jurisdictions: United States; Florida; Wyoming; New York; Texas
Authority boundary: Workbench research only. This is an institutional map, not legal advice. It does not decide the status of a particular institution, account, transaction or tax return and does not create a Chronicle watch or authorize publication.

Question

What legally distinguishes a bank from a credit union, which rules come from the federal government, which choices remain with the states, and what do Florida, Wyoming, New York and Texas reveal about possible banking-transition paths?

Bottom line

The United States does not have one bank regime and one credit-union regime. It has two overlapping dual-charter systems plus federal consumer, payments, anti-money-laundering and insurance overlays.

  • A bank may be nationally chartered by the OCC or state-chartered. A state bank may be a Federal Reserve member or a nonmember bank; that choice changes its primary federal supervisor. Most deposit-taking banks are FDIC-insured, but charter, insurer and supervisor are separate facts.
  • A credit union may be federally chartered by NCUA or chartered under a state credit-union law. NCUA insures all federal credit unions and the overwhelming majority of state-chartered credit unions; some state credit unions use private share insurance. Again, charter, insurer and supervisor are separate facts.
  • The deepest legal difference is governance. A stock bank is owned and controlled through equity; a credit union is a nonprofit mutual cooperative in which qualifying members ordinarily receive one vote each. But “bank equals shareholder-owned” is not universal: mutual savings banks are depositor-owned.
  • A bank generally serves the public subject to its business and compliance rules. A credit union serves a legally defined field of membership, although public-unit and other nonmember shares may be permitted. Membership, account eligibility and lending eligibility are related but not identical questions.
  • A bank deposit and a credit-union share account are economically similar funding claims, but they arise from different legal forms, are insured by different federal funds, and enter different failure-resolution systems.
  • Federal law supplies important common floors—including consumer-credit, fair-lending, privacy, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, payments and safety-and-soundness rules—but it does not erase state choices about charter powers, fields of membership, branching, public deposits, taxation, state supervision or some digital-asset activity.

The four states show four different institutional configurations:

  1. Florida: active state-bank and state-credit-union charters; mandatory federal share insurance for Florida credit unions; credit unions admitted to the qualified-public-depository system in 2024; explicit 2026 digital-asset savings clauses, but insured banks and credit unions are excluded from the separate state-qualified stablecoin-issuer lane.
  2. Wyoming: an active state-bank and special-purpose-depository-institution system but no current state credit-union law. Its credit unions are federally chartered, yet state law now allows qualifying credit unions to receive public deposits. Wyoming's most explicit digital-asset powers sit in the state-bank/SPDI statutes, not in a Wyoming credit-union charter.
  3. New York: active DFS-supervised state-bank and state-credit-union systems; a broad credit-union field-of-membership statute; but the ordinary local-public-deposit law still names banks and trust companies rather than credit unions. Repeated bills to change that remain proposals.
  4. Texas: separate state bank and credit-union regulators; an active state credit-union charter; state and local public-deposit routes for credit unions; a specific franchise-tax exemption; and bank-specific virtual-currency custody guidance. No equally specific Texas Credit Union Department authorization for direct digital-asset custody was identified by the cutoff.

A quick decision tree

  1. Who issued the charter? If OCC, it is a national bank or federal savings association; if NCUA, an FCU; otherwise identify the state statute and regulator.
  2. Who owns and votes? Stockholders, mutual depositors, members, a parent company or government?
  3. Who may open the account and borrow? The public, a defined field, a public unit or only another institution?
  4. What exactly is insured? A deposit or share account at the named insured institution—not a token, security, mutual fund, wallet balance or affiliate product merely sold through it.
  5. Which agency examines which problem? Charter powers, prudential risk, consumer law, holding-company risk and insurance exposure may belong to different agencies.
  6. What has actually happened? Authority is not an application; an application is not approval; approval is not opening; public-depository eligibility is not a deposit; a deposit is not a resulting loan; digital permission is not production use.

Laws that commonly govern both

Federal functional rules often attach to an activity, product or size—not to the marketing distinction between “bank” and “credit union.” Examples include:

  • Equal Credit Opportunity Act/Regulation B. The CFPB states it applies to all credit without regard to the creditor's type, subject to statutory exclusions.
  • Truth in Lending Act/Regulation Z for covered consumer credit.
  • Electronic Fund Transfer Act/Regulation E, Fair Credit Reporting Act/Regulation V, privacy and data-security duties, and mortgage-servicing rules where their scope tests are met.
  • Bank Secrecy Act/anti-money-laundering, customer-identification, sanctions and suspicious-activity regimes. A 2025 interagency order describes the CIP duty for both banks and credit unions.
  • CFPB supervision for banks, thrifts and credit unions with assets over $10 billion and their affiliates. Smaller institutions remain subject to federal consumer law, but routine examination generally stays with the prudential regulator.
  • Federal Reserve payments eligibility and access review. Banks and credit unions may be legally eligible for master accounts/services, but eligibility, application, approval, restrictions and live service are separate clocks.

This common layer explains why consumer experience can look similar even when the charter constitution is different.

Federal distinctions that matter in transition research

Field of membership

Federal CU membership may be based on a single common bond, multiple common bonds or a well-defined local community, neighborhood or rural district. A qualifying person must subscribe to at least one share. A broad geographic charter expands the eligible class; it does not create state ownership or dissolve the membership gate.

Business lending and investment

For most federally insured credit unions, the statutory member-business-loan ceiling is the lesser of 1.75 times actual net worth or 12.25 percent of total assets, with statutory exceptions. FCUs also face charter-specific investment, maturity and interest-rate limits. NCUA has extended the general FCU loan-rate ceiling of 18 percent through September 10, 2027; the statutory default is 15 percent unless the Board acts. These are not bank-wide ceilings.

Tax

Federal credit unions receive the express exemption in 12 U.S.C. §1768, subject to the statute's real- and tangible-property exceptions. The IRS places FCUs under §501(c)(1) and qualifying state-chartered credit unions without capital stock, operated mutually and without profit, under §501(c)(14). This structural subsidy is real, but it does not itself prove lower rates, broader access or a public mission outcome at a particular institution.

Public-unit and nonmember accounts

Federal law permits government deposits in FCUs, and NCUA insurance can cover permitted nonmember deposits. State public-deposit law still decides whether a state or locality may select the institution and what collateral, concentration, application or location rules apply. Public-depository eligibility does not make the CU state-owned.

CRA

The federal Community Reinvestment Act examination regime applies to covered banks and savings associations, not credit unions. That difference should be recorded alongside—not confused with—the CU's membership, nonprofit and service obligations.

Conversion and acquisition

NCUA states that an insured CU may purchase bank assets and assume liabilities, but cannot acquire the bank charter. Bank equity is paid to shareholders rather than carried into the CU as shareholder capital. A headline saying “credit union buys bank” therefore describes a regulated asset/liability transaction, not the CU becoming owner of an operating bank charter.

Florida

Institutional form

Florida maintains separate OFR bank and credit-union supervisory bureaus. Chapter 658 governs state banks and trust companies; Chapter 657 defines a credit union as a cooperative society. A Florida CU is organized by at least five people representing a limited field of membership, is controlled through member governance and must maintain federal share insurance.

Chapter 658's ordinary bank form uses capital stock and stockholder-elected directors. That makes stock ownership the normal Florida bank comparison, while federal and out-of-state mutual-bank exceptions remain possible.

Accounts, lending and failure

Section 657.033 permits member shares and deposits and tightly limits nonmember accounts; §657.038 centers credit on members. Florida therefore retains the member nexus at both sides of the balance sheet. OFR can impose conservatorship and may appoint NCUA; involuntary liquidation has its own statutory route. For state banks, Florida law separately provides for FDIC receivership.

Public funds

Since July 1, 2024, state- and federally chartered credit unions may seek qualified-public-depository status. Section 280.042 requires a public depositor's statement of intent and caps all CU QPDs at 7 percent of state-treasury funds, 7 percent of a state university or college's public deposits, and one CU's public deposits at 10 percent of its total assets. Designation, collateral and selection still must occur.

Tax and digital assets

Florida §213.12 gives state CUs the same state/local tax immunity as FCUs; banks operate under a separate net-income franchise-tax regime. The 2026 stablecoin statute preserves otherwise-permissible tokenized-share, distributed-ledger and custody activities for insured depositories, but excludes insured banks and CUs from the distinct “state-qualified issuer” lane. The government stablecoin pilot ordinarily converts receipts to dollars and credits the ordinary QPD destination. None of these provisions names a CU operator or establishes deployment.

Between the lines: Florida is building a controlled competitive bridge into public deposits while keeping insured depositories, state-qualified stablecoin issuers and the government's payment/conversion function legally separate.

Wyoming

The absence is structural

Wyoming Title 13 and the Division of Banking's current charter materials cover state banks, trust companies, nonbank licensees and special-purpose depository institutions. The IRS's current internal guidance lists Wyoming among jurisdictions without a state credit-union law. The Division's complaint page directs credit-union matters to NCUA and describes the institutions as FCUs. The June 2026 NCUA institution list likewise showed Wyoming-headquartered CUs as federal, not FISCUs.

This supports a stronger but carefully bounded finding than “none happened to be found”: Wyoming has no current state-credit-union charter lane. A future enabling act could change that; legacy statutory references to a “state-chartered credit union” do not themselves supply a current charter code.

Banks, SPDIs and digital assets

Wyoming state banks have broad deposit, loan, securities, trust and related powers. Chapter 12 creates the SPDI form, and §13-1-206 addresses custody/fiduciary treatment for digital assets, stablecoin reserves and segregated customer property. Those are state-bank/SPDI powers. They do not transfer to an FCU merely because the FCU operates in Wyoming.

Public funds and failure

SF 143, effective July 1, 2025, allowed qualifying federal or state CUs doing business in Wyoming to become public depositories. The State Treasurer's June 4, 2026 approved list named ten CUs. Because Wyoming lacks a state CU charter in practice, the operative entrants are federal cooperatives admitted by state public-funds law. Their charter, insurance, prudential supervision and failure resolution remain with NCUA.

Between the lines: Wyoming is not using a state CU charter to innovate. It is combining federally chartered retail cooperatives with state-controlled public-deposit selection, while reserving its bespoke digital-custody architecture to state banks/SPDIs.

New York

Institutional form

DFS charters and annually examines New York state CUs; NCUA examines them periodically as insurer. Banking Law Article 11 permits seven or more qualifying New York residents or employees to organize a CU and allows occupational, associational and geographic membership categories with superintendent approval. Members elect directors; Article 11 governs shares, lending, reserves, stations, conversion and reporting.

New York banking law separately includes banks, trust companies, stock-form savings banks and non-stock/mutual forms. It is therefore the clearest reminder in this four-state set that “bank” is a legal family, not one ownership form.

Public funds

General Municipal Law §10 currently directs ordinary local-government deposits to banks or trust companies. It does not name credit unions. S6050 and S3066 would create CU access under specified limits and community-investment conditions, but both remained in Senate Banks Committee at the cutoff. A durable packet must record that as a proposal clock, not an enacted power.

Tax, failure and digital assets

Banking Law §479 treats Article 11 CUs as institutions for savings and excludes corporate/share/accumulation taxes unless a law specifically names them. Voluntary liquidation and the superintendent's broader possession/liquidation powers remain state-law processes, while NCUA protects federally insured shares and may coordinate resolution.

DFS requires prior approval before a covered institution—including a New York-chartered CU, bank or trust company—begins or materially changes virtual-currency activity. That common approval gate does not make every institution a BitLicensee or limited-purpose trust company, and it does not prove approval was sought or granted.

Between the lines: New York is permissive about chartering and supervising member cooperatives but conservative about placing ordinary municipal cash in them. Its digital gate is regulator-wide prior approval, whereas public deposits remain institution-type specific.

Texas

Institutional form

Texas has intentionally separate supervisors. The Department of Banking supervises state banks; the independent Credit Union Department and Credit Union Commission administer the state CU code and rules. Chapter 121 describes the CU purpose as encouraging thrift, providing fair and reasonable credit and allowing member control. Chapter 122 uses a definable community of interest for membership.

Texas state banks may organize as banking associations or limited banking associations and hold broad deposit, credit, securities, fiduciary and advisory powers. Texas CUs remain mutual nonprofits with one-member/one-vote governance. Texas CUD correctly describes stock ownership as the usual bank form, but the national mutual-bank exception should remain in view.

Insurance, tax and failure

Texas requires share-insurance protection. CUD says most Texas CUs use NCUSIF and some have private insurance; private coverage is not backed by the United States. Tax Code §171.077 expressly exempts a Texas-chartered CU from the franchise tax. Chapter 126 authorizes the CU commissioner to appoint a conservator or liquidating agent; banks follow a separate commissioner/FDIC resolution route.

Public funds

Government Code §404.021 allows a state or national bank, savings and loan association or credit union doing business through a Texas office or branch to apply as a state depository. Federal insurance, physical presence, Comptroller approval and collateral duties are separate gates. Finance Code Chapter 279 adds development-district routes for banking and CU district depositories; it is not the sole source of CU eligibility.

Digital assets

The Department of Banking's 2021 notice concludes that Texas state banks may provide virtual-currency custody when the service fits their existing authority and risk controls. No comparably specific current CUD instrument authorizing direct crypto custody by a Texas state CU was identified. CUD materials instead emphasize third-party due diligence, clear separation of uninsured investment products and existing electronic-services powers. This is an unresolved authority/approval question, not a finding that Texas CUs are prohibited from every crypto-related service.

Between the lines: Texas exposes why regulator identity matters. A digital-custody conclusion issued to state banks does not silently extend to CUs, even when both can receive public funds and offer similar retail accounts.

The high-value transition question is not “does the state like crypto?” It is: which legal entity has permission to issue, hold reserves, custody keys, convert, distribute, redeem and settle, and which balance is insured at each step?

An app failure, token loss, vendor insolvency and depository failure are different events. Insurance follows the legal account at the insured institution; it does not automatically follow value through an affiliate or technology layer.

What the four-state sample teaches

  1. Federal law is a floor and overlay, not one national charter. The same federal consumer rule can cover four institutions whose powers, governance and public-funds eligibility differ.
  2. State charter and state ownership are unrelated. Florida and Texas state CUs are member-owned; Wyoming's approved CU public depositories are federal; none becomes a public bank through deposit eligibility.
  3. Public funds are a state competition choice. Florida, Wyoming and Texas opened lanes; New York has not enacted the same choice.
  4. Tax treatment is constitutional architecture, not proof of performance. CU exemptions alter the cost structure, but institution-level pricing, access and service outcomes require data.
  5. Digital authority attaches to the legal entity. Wyoming SPDI authority, Texas bank custody guidance, Florida issuer exclusions and New York prior approval cannot be generalized across charters.
  6. Ownership drives control and transition mechanics. A stock bank can be acquired through equity/control approvals. A CU's member democracy, FOM and no-common-stock structure make consolidation and conversion legally different.
  7. Insurance similarity can hide legal difference. The $250,000 headline is similar, but FDIC and NCUSIF are separate funds with separate statutory actors and resolution processes.

Claims this packet authorizes—and those it does not

Supported

  • Banks and credit unions operate within overlapping federal/state dual-charter systems.
  • CUs are member-owned mutual cooperatives; stock banks use equity control; mutual banks are the key bank-side exception.
  • Federal consumer and AML laws can govern both even when prudential regulators differ.
  • Florida, Wyoming and Texas allow credit unions into defined public-deposit systems; New York's general local-deposit law does not yet do so.
  • Wyoming lacks a current state CU charter system, while Florida, New York and Texas maintain one.
  • Digital-asset permission must be traced to the named charter, regulator, activity and current approval clock.

Not supported without additional evidence

  • A “state credit union” is state-owned.
  • Public-depository eligibility means public money was deposited.
  • A public deposit caused a lower loan rate or expanded credit.
  • Tax exemption proves better member outcomes.
  • A bank/CU's crypto press release proves charter authority, approval, custody segregation, insurance or operating use.
  • One state's bank authority applies to that state's CUs or to a federal charter.
  • FDIC or NCUA insurance covers stablecoins, crypto assets, mutual funds or affiliate balances.

Durable research use

For any future “bank versus credit union” claim, start with the identity table, then answer in this order:

charter → owner/voter → regulator → insurer → eligible account holder → power → state permission → approval → operating evidence → outcome

That sequence prevents the most common category errors: state-chartered becoming state-owned, public-deposit eligibility becoming funding, custody becoming insurance, a regulator's bank letter becoming CU authority, or a proposal becoming a working institution.
