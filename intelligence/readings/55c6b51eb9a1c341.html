CISA 2026 Election Infrastructure Security Plan Return — October 5, 2026
In plain English
Question: Did CISA publish the election-infrastructure plan promised for
approximately August 16, what did it change, and where does the evidence stop?
Answer: Yes, on September 24, roughly five and a half weeks late. The bundle
is best classified as an agency-owned predicate record and implementation
blueprint, not proof that the described operating change has already happened.
CISA has adopted the July threat reports, supplied the vocabulary and findings
that can be quoted downstream, and laid out a future delivery route through
Regional Directors, state-owned fusion centers, federal intelligence partners,
CISA Central and a near-real-time platform modeled on the 2026 World Cup.
That is consequential because this is the reference stack a later proposal,
manual change, grant term, procurement, MOU or operations plan can cite. But the
plan itself is not any of those downstream instruments. It creates no federal
power to run an election, change a voter record, certify a machine, install a
patch or count a ballot. It also does not identify the platform, its operator,
its records owner, its data rules, its security authorization or any election
jurisdiction already using it.
Scope and exclusions
This return compares the attached 13-page plan with the July election release,
CISA's 2024 public operating model, current EAC certification rules, fusion-
center law and official records about the World Cup information-sharing model.
It does not infer platform identity from similar wording, treat offered services
as delivered services, or treat network visibility as control of voting.
Analytical correction: predicate record, not implemented change
Katie's structural read changes the clock classification. These reports do the
work that often comes before an operative proposal: they define the problem,
select the evidence, normalize terms, identify preferred controls and give a
later author an agency-owned source to quote. That makes the bundle more
important as administrative groundwork, while requiring stricter language
about what has already changed.
The correct chain is:
incident/source report -> agency adoption -> plan and stated recommendation ->
downstream proposal or instrument -> legal/procedural adoption -> funding and
agreement -> deployment -> operation -> measured effect
The September bundle reaches agency adoption, plan and stated
recommendation. Historical CISA services also pre-existed it. The bounded
October 5 check located no EAC notice, Federal Register object, Grants.gov
object or SAM.gov object quoting the plan's exact title. The next decisive
receipt is the first actual downstream instrument that cites or operationalizes
this predicate record.
Likely instrument classes, without assuming any already exists, are:
an EAC manual, notice, engineering-change or certification proposal;
a CISA/DHS operations plan, directive, implementation memo or regional order;
a platform solicitation, award, authority-to-operate, privacy/records object
or user agreement;
a grant NOFO, award condition or cooperative agreement;
a signed federal-state/fusion-center MOU; or
a state rule, procurement, certification action or implementation plan.
1. Publication and bundle
DHS announced on July 17 that CISA would release an updated plan within 30
days, producing an approximate August 16 clock. No qualifying plan appeared in
the August 27 recheck. CISA and DHS published 2026 Election Infrastructure
Security Plan: Securing the Next 250 on September 24, 2026.
The release is a three-object bundle, not one isolated plan:
the new 13-page plan;
the CISA Election Report, now on CISA's own publication page; and
Recognizing and Addressing Threats to Statewide Voter Registration
Databases, also now on CISA's own publication page.
The second file is byte-identical to the July White House copy
(3c6db961...c61485c22). The September voter-database PDF has a new file hash,
but its extracted text is exactly the same length and content as the July copy,
and all 11 pages render pixel-identically at 96 dpi. The observed change is PDF
packaging/metadata, not a substantive revision. The important provenance change
is that CISA has adopted both July products into its current agency resource
library and uses them as the factual premise for the September plan.

3. The proposed operating room is the key next object
On page 12, the plan says all fusion centers and state and local election
officials will have no-cost access to an information-sharing platform for the
2026 cycle. It says the platform supports near-real-time communication and that
federal partners, federal agencies, fusion centers and CISA Central will be
actively engaged in day-to-day communication. It says the model was deployed
during the 2026 FIFA World Cup.
That is the deepest institutional design in the document. If implemented, it
would place election officials inside a general public-safety and intelligence
coordination pattern, not merely on an election-sector mailing list. The plan
does not itself prove enrollment, account creation, platform operation or use in
an election.
The public evidence does not identify the election platform. A July 2
San Mateo County account
says the Northern California Regional Intelligence Center helped build a
Microsoft Teams-based World Cup platform used by more than 4,000 public-safety
officials in the United States, Canada and Mexico, with role-based access,
incident mapping and document/photo sharing. That is a strong candidate for the
model CISA referenced, but the plan does not say the election platform is the
same Teams environment, tenant or system.
DHS separately operates the Homeland Security Information Network (HSIN), its
official system for sharing Sensitive But Unclassified information. A September
4 DHS acquisition forecast
describes a $5 million-$10 million FY2027 follow-on for HSIN stakeholder,
product and governance support. The plan does not name HSIN either.
The controlling open questions are therefore concrete:
Is the election tool Microsoft Teams, HSIN, another fusion-center platform,
or an integration across them?
Who owns the tenant, contract, records, logs and incident response?
What data classes are permitted: threat indicators, contact information,
incident reports, vulnerability findings, network telemetry, voter data or
something narrower?
What are the retention, audit, access-review, correction, disclosure and
breach-notification rules?
Which election offices actually enrolled, and when did the first live
election use occur?
Nothing in the plan says voter files, ballot data or tabulation data will be
uploaded. “All officials have access” is not “all election data is centralized.”
4. Fusion centers create an overlay, not a command chain
The plan accurately notes that fusion centers are state-owned and state-
operated. Federal participation is nevertheless substantial: CISA, DHS I&A,
FBI and other federal partners can supply systems, personnel, threat reporting
and classified access.
6 U.S.C. § 124h
authorizes the DHS fusion-center initiative and requires privacy/civil-liberties
policies, training and security measures. It also expressly contemplates DHS
personnel and the use of HSIN/HSDN or successor systems. Those safeguards and
authorities explain how the overlay can exist; they do not answer the election
platform's exact data, records and audit rules.
This matches the adjacent CISA-NCSWIC finding in
Research Packages/Sovereign Communications/NCSWIC_GOVERNANCE_AND_OPERATING_MAP_2026-09-29.md:
CISA can administratively support, technically assist and coordinate a state-
operated network without acquiring the states' underlying legal authority or
owning their systems. The same boundary applies here.
5. The service catalog can expand visibility, not legal control
Pages 9-11 offer a broad CISA service stack:
continuous internet-facing vulnerability scanning with weekly reports and
urgent alerts;
monthly web-application scans and on-demand assessments;
on-site Risk and Vulnerability Assessments and penetration testing;
continuous CISA Attack Surface Testing;
decoy systems and canary tokens jointly monitored by CISA and the participant;
cloud-security assessments, CPG reviews and KEV prioritization; and
cyber, physical, exercise, insider-threat and incident-response support.
These services can give CISA recurring visibility into a participating office's
external attack surface, findings, alerts and remediation state. Canary systems
can also create a jointly observed detection surface inside the participant's
environment. That is materially more operational than publishing advice.
But the gates remain separate:
service offered -> jurisdiction requests -> scope/terms agreed -> access
granted -> assessment runs -> finding issued -> local/vendor remediation ->
certification approval -> deployment -> independent retest -> production use
The plan proves only the offer and the intended coordination model. It gives no
jurisdiction list, enrollment count, engagement schedule, finding ledger or
remediation result for the 2026 election cycle.
6. The patch/certification sentence is a policy bomb, not a rule
Page 6 recommends harmonizing patch-management and certification requirements
so cybersecurity updates can move in real time without unnecessarily affecting
voting-system certification.
Current EAC materials show why this is difficult. The
Testing and Certification Program
is voluntary at the federal level, but many states incorporate it into law or
regulation. Under the current
Engineering Change Order process,
even a minimal change needs voting-system test-lab review and endorsement plus
EAC approval; a non-minimal change becomes a modification subject to testing
and review.
CISA therefore identified a real security bottleneck but did not solve the
authority problem. CISA cannot, by publishing this plan, waive EAC approval,
state certification law, vendor testing, local acceptance testing or chain-of-
custody requirements. The decisive next object is a changed EAC manual,
approved ECO/modification route, state rule, or named emergency-patch procedure.
7. The threat premise was elevated, not newly proved
The plan carries the July voter-database report's assertion that attackers
probed all 50 states and succeeded in at least 20. CISA's September landing page
also says China breached multiple state voter-registration systems before the
2020 election and criticizes past minimization of the risk.
Publication on CISA.gov and incorporation into the plan move the statement from
a White House release bundle into current agency doctrine. They do not fill the
evidence gap already identified in the July audit: the report does not enumerate
the 20 states, attach incident-level forensics or show that stolen data was used
to obtain ballots or alter results. Its historical examples mix probes, public-
site access, stolen registration data, exposed records, vendor compromise and
modeled future exploitation.
The correct split remains:
supported: voter-registration systems and the PII used to verify voters
are attractive targets; multiple real breaches and exposures occurred;
supported as agency assessment: CISA now publicly carries the “all 50 / at
least 20” count and the China-breach framing;
not publicly reproducible: the complete state list and incident chain
behind the 20-state count; and
not established: use of those breaches to cast, accept or count an
ineligible ballot or change a certified result.
