Reading the Charter — FHLBank System

The FHLBank System is eleven regional banks wearing a trench coat marked "one entity." Each bank is its own corporation with its own members and its own balance sheet — but the moment any of them borrows money in the capital markets through the system's shared Office of Finance, all eleven become liable for that debt together, joint and several, whether or not they signed for it. That single design choice — captured verbatim below — is the entire reason the System's debt trades like it has an implicit federal guarantee without ever needing one written down: the market isn't betting on Washington, it's betting on ten other regional banks' balance sheets standing behind the eleventh.

1. Immunities & liability shields — not captured this session

  • The corporate-powers section that would normally carry sue-and-be-sued language, litigation immunities, and asset-seizure protection is §1432 ("Incorporation of banks; corporate powers; housing project loans") — visible as the "next" cross-reference on the §1431 page fetched this session, but not itself fetched. Flagged as an open gap rather than guessed at (see Gaps below). What this file substitutes instead is the joint-and-several liability structure below — the opposite of an immunity, expanded liability by design.

2. Tax exemptions

  • The marquee clause. 12 U.S.C. §1433, in full: "Any and all notes, debentures, bonds, and other such obligations issued by any bank, and consolidated Federal Home Loan Bank bonds and debentures, shall be exempt both as to principal and interest from all taxation (except surtaxes, estate, inheritance, and gift taxes) now or hereafter imposed by the United States, by any Territory, dependency, or possession thereof, or by any State, county, municipality, or local taxing authority. The bank, including its franchise, its capital, reserves, and surplus, its advances, and its income, shall be exempt from all taxation now or hereafter imposed by the United States... except that... any real property of the bank shall be subject to State, Territorial, county, municipal, or local taxation to the same extent according to its value as other real property is taxed."
  • Read closely, this is a double exemption: the paper (bonds/debentures) is tax-exempt, AND the bank itself — franchise, capital, reserves, surplus, advances, income — is tax-exempt, federal and state/local both, with only real property carved back in. This is a broader exemption than Fannie Mae gets today (state/local only, post-1968) and closer to what Ginnie Mae or the FDIC get (see the FDIC and Fannie Mae extraction notes in this folder for the comparison) — a fingerprint of the FHLBanks never having gone through Fannie's 1968 privatization-and-tax-loss moment. The FHLBanks have stayed cooperatively member-owned GSEs, not shareholder corporations, and their tax treatment shows it.
  • One more wrinkle: the notes/debentures/bonds themselves, with unearned coupons attached, must be accepted at par by the issuing bank as payment against a home-owner debtor's obligation. Same sentence, same section — a built-in acceptance mechanism that isn't really a tax provision at all, just bolted onto the same clause since 1932.

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — thin in what's captured

  • The only appointment/removal-adjacent fact captured this session is structural: the Director — defined at §1422(11) as "the Director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency" — holds pervasive rulemaking and adjustment authority throughout what's captured (e.g., §1431(a): banks borrow "subject to rules and regulations prescribed by the Director"; §1431(d): the Director "shall have full power to require any Federal Home Loan Bank to deposit additional collateral or to make substitutions of collateral or to adjust equities between the Federal Home Loan Banks"). That is real control — the Director can force one bank to bail out another's collateral position by fiat — but the appointment/removal mechanics for the Director's own office live in FHFA's charter (12 U.S.C. Chapter 46), not this Act, and the FHLBanks' own individual bank-level board-of-directors structure lives in §1427 (not captured — see Gaps).

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations — the joint-and-several core

  • §1431(a): each Bank may borrow, give security, pay interest, and issue debentures, bonds, or other obligations, "subject to rules and regulations prescribed by the Director... to do all things necessary for carrying out the provisions of this chapter." This is a capital-markets funding model, not an appropriations model, from the founding text forward.
  • §1431(b) — the joint-and-several clause, verbatim: the Office of Finance, "as agent for the Banks, may issue consolidated Federal Home Loan Bank debentures which shall be the joint and several obligations of all Federal Home Loan Banks organized and existing under this chapter." Every one of the eleven banks is on the hook for every other bank's share of the consolidated debt. The same section caps total outstanding debentures at "five times the total paid-in capital of all the Federal Home Loan Banks" — a system-wide leverage ceiling, not a per-bank one.
  • §1431(c) — bonds get the identical treatment: consolidated Federal Home Loan Bank bonds "shall be the joint and several obligations of all the Federal Home Loan Banks." Two funding instruments (debentures and bonds), same liability structure both times.
  • §1431(k) — the strange cross-entity twist (see item 10): the FHLBanks are statutorily empowered to lend money to the FDIC, not the other way around.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs — partially visible, not captured verbatim

  • Not fetched this session, but visible by cross-reference in the material that was: §1431(j) ("Audits," per the section headers seen while fetching §1431) apparently limits GAO audit scope and disapplies certain federal-corporation-audit provisions to the FHLBanks — flagged as a real, specific carve-out worth a dedicated follow-up fetch, not fabricated here since the operative sentence itself wasn't captured verbatim this session.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses — not captured this session

  • No perpetuity/dissolution or member-withdrawal clause was fetched this session; that language likely sits in §1426 or §1427 (bank organization/capital stock — see Gaps).

7. Emergency powers — mostly lives in a different entity's charter

  • What IS captured: §1431(l) gives the Secretary of the Treasury temporary authority to purchase FHLBank obligations on an emergency basis, conditioned on the Secretary determining such action is necessary to "provide stability to the financial markets," "prevent disruptions in the availability of mortgage finance," and "protect the taxpayer" — nearly identical trigger language to Fannie Mae's §1719(g) emergency-purchase authority (see the Fannie Mae extraction note in this folder for the direct textual parallel; both were bolted onto their respective charters during the 2008 crisis window).
  • What is NOT in this charter at all: any conservatorship/receivership trigger for a Federal Home Loan Bank itself. Exactly as with Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, that power belongs to the regulator's own statute — FHFA's charter, 12 U.S.C. Chapter 46 (see the FHFA extraction note already in this Charter Library) — not to the Federal Home Loan Bank Act. The pattern repeats across every HERA-era GSE: the entity's own charter funds it and empowers it, but the kill-switch is parked one folder over.

8. The definitions section — §1422, captured in full

  • "Community financial institution" is the scope-expanding definition to watch. §1422(10): a member bank "the deposits of which are insured under the Federal Deposit Insurance Act" and that has "less than $1,000,000,000 in average total assets" (three-year average), a threshold the Director must adjust annually for CPI inflation. Crossing or staying under this line changes what collateral a member bank can pledge for advances elsewhere in the Act (not itself captured, but the definitional gate is here) — a classic case of a number buried in the definitions section quietly deciding who gets favorable treatment.
  • "Insured depository institution" is defined by cross-reference, not self-contained — §1422(9) points out to the Federal Deposit Insurance Act's own §3 definition (12 U.S.C. §1813, which this Library's FDIC folder flags as NOT YET captured there either — a definitional dependency chain with a gap at both ends).
  • "Home mortgage" is defined broadly enough to include 50-99 year leaseholds, not just fee-simple ownership (§1422(5)) — an old (1932-1962-era) definition still on the books, expanded once in 1962 from "not more than four families" language to open-ended "one or more homes or other dwelling units... defined by the Director."
  • "Director" and "Agency" both point to the Federal Housing Finance Agency (§1422(11)-(12)) — added by HERA in 2008, replacing the pre-2008 "Finance Board"/"Board" definitions that this same section carried before (visible in the editorial amendment notes: the 2008 amendment "struck out former par. (1)... 'The terms Finance Board and Board mean the Federal Housing Finance Board'").

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution — not captured this session

  • No dissolution or perpetuity clause was fetched; likely candidate section is §1432 (see item 1) or §1427. Flagged as an open gap.

10. Anything strange — the clause you'd never expect

  • The Federal Home Loan Banks are legally empowered to lend money to the FDIC's Deposit Insurance Fund. §1431(k)(1): "Subject to paragraph (3), the Federal Home Loan Banks may, upon the request of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, make loans to such Corporation for the use of the Deposit Insurance Fund." Paragraph (2): "Any loan by a Federal Home Loan Bank pursuant to paragraph (1) shall be a direct liability of the Deposit Insurance Fund" — meaning if the FDIC's own insurance fund runs short, one GSE (the housing-finance cooperative) can be tapped to fund another (the bank-deposit insurer), on the FDIC's own request, at not-less-than-market interest and adequately secured (§1431(k)(3)). Two entities most people assume are entirely separate turn out to have a standing statutory lending relationship built directly into the FHLBank Act.
  • A prior pass on this file speculated, based on unverified web search, that 12 U.S.C. §1441 carried the joint-and-several liability clause. This pass fetched §1441 directly and found it is something else entirely: "Financing Corporation" (FICO), the FIRREA-era vehicle that issued bonds to recapitalize the collapsed FSLIC insurance fund in 1987 — administratively parked in the same U.S. Code chapter as the FHLBank Act, funded partly by FHLBank contributions, but a distinct corporate entity. The actual joint-and-several clause was hiding in plain sight two sections earlier, at §1431(b)-(c). Worth remembering as a lesson in this Library's own methodology: don't cite a section number pulled from search-engine inference without opening the actual text.
  • The five-times-paid-in-capital leverage cap (§1431(b)) is a hard, simple number — not a risk-weighted formula, not a regulator-set ratio, just "five times" — sitting in a 1932-era sentence that has apparently never been rewritten into modern capital-adequacy language the way bank capital rules elsewhere have been.

Layer map — organic act through today

  1. 1932 — Federal Home Loan Bank Act (July 22, 1932, ch. 522, 47 Stat. 725). Created the regional Federal Home Loan Bank System — modeled structurally on the Federal Reserve System (regional banks, member-owned, a central oversight board) but for savings institutions and home-mortgage lending rather than commercial banking. Established the short title (§1421), the definitions anchor (§1422), the borrowing/joint-and-several framework (§1431), and the tax exemption (§1433) — all four captured in this folder.
  2. 1989 — FIRREA (Pub. L. 101-73). Abolished the original Federal Home Loan Bank Board (which had doubled as both the FHLBanks' regulator and the regulator of federal savings-and-loan associations — the same agency whose S&L-side collapse triggered FIRREA in the first place) and replaced it with the Federal Housing Finance Board, a dedicated regulator for the FHLBank System alone. Also chartered the Financing Corporation (FICO, §1441 — confirmed this session to be a distinct entity, not the joint-and-several clause) to recapitalize the failed FSLIC fund, partly through FHLBank contributions.
  3. 1999 — Federal Home Loan Bank System Modernization Act (Pub. L. 106-102, title VI). Overhauled membership rules, capital structure, and the definitions section — added the "community financial institution" asset-threshold definition now at §1422(10) and made Federal Home Loan Bank membership voluntary rather than tied to specific charter types, among other changes visible in this session's §1422 editorial-amendment notes.
  4. 2008 — Housing and Economic Recovery Act (HERA, Pub. L. 110-289, div. A, title II). Abolished the Federal Housing Finance Board and folded FHLBank oversight into the newly created Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) — the same regulator HERA gave conservatorship power over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Renamed "Board"/"Finance Board" to "Director" throughout §1422's definitions (visible directly in this session's fetched editorial notes) and raised the community-financial-institution asset threshold from $500 million to $1 billion (§1422(10)(A)(ii), also visible in the 2008 amendment notes captured this session).

Entity Theory read

The FHLBank System is a mutual-aid pact wearing a government charter: eleven regional banks that agree, by federal statute rather than private contract, to stand behind each other's debt dollar-for-dollar. That joint-and-several clause is the whole trick — it lets the System borrow at near-Treasury rates without a single line of text promising a federal bailout, because the market prices in ten other balance sheets instead. Who really holds the leash: the FHFA Director, a single federal appointee who can force one bank to post collateral for another's benefit (§1431(d)) and who inherited, in 2008, the same oversight seat that used to be a dedicated Home-Loan-Bank-only regulator. Does it survive a change of control: everything captured this session says yes and by design — the cooperative structure, the tax exemption, and the joint-and-several liability all predate FHFA and would outlast any single bank's failure, since the other ten banks are statutorily obligated to absorb it. The strangest thing this pass turned up wasn't in this Act at all: the discovery that the System can lend money to the FDIC's own insurance fund on request — two supposedly separate federal safety nets, wired together by one paragraph almost nobody reads.
