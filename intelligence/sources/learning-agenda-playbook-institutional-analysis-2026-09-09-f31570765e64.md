# Learning Agenda Playbook: institutional capacity and the Education overhaul

Evidence cutoff: September 9, 2026. Official Research; bounded release analysis. Published ambition, demonstrated pilot activity, legal authority and future outcomes are distinguished below.

## Finding

The Department of Education's new Learning Agenda Playbook supplies a practical method for state education agencies to connect priorities, evidence, decisions and resources. Its institutional significance is that it addresses the receiving side of the federal overhaul: how states will make and revise consequential choices as the administration seeks to move responsibility toward them.

The strongest mechanism is the explicit connection between learning and action. Findings may justify continuing, scaling, adjusting or stopping programs, with named decision owners, timing, expected student outcomes and subsequent monitoring. This is potentially consequential for budgets and institutional survival, although the release itself neither reallocates a particular appropriation nor establishes improved student outcomes.

The September 9 [ED announcement](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-launches-learning-agenda-playbook-support-state-education-leaders-and-improve-student-outcomes) attributes development to OESE, IES and the National Comprehensive Center working with eight states: Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Kansas, New Hampshire, Tennessee, Utah and Wisconsin. The [fact sheet](https://www.ed.gov/media/document/fact-sheet-learning-agenda-playbook-114561.pdf) explicitly places the release within the administration's ambition to return education to states and close the federal department. That political framing is the administration's claim; the document is a voluntary assistance product, not a legal instrument accomplishing closure.

## What the operating method changes

The [official playbook](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook) includes a facilitator guide, editable toolbox, learning-agenda template and capacity continuum. It puts existing strategic goals into action rather than replacing those goals with a new national curriculum or strategic plan.

| Step | Practical institutional change |
|---|---|
| 1. Align priorities | Build a cross-functional team including executive decision authority; choose a manageable set of priorities anchored in the state's existing goals and obligations. |
| 2. Prepare communication and constituent engagement | Define whose knowledge is needed and what decisions their input can affect; use engagement throughout the process. |
| 3. Identify knowledge gaps | Make the program's causal assumptions explicit through a logic model; inventory existing evidence before commissioning more. |
| 4. Develop learning questions | Connect each question to a concrete decision, the person who will use the answer and the time when it is needed. |
| 5. Implement an action plan | Specify evidence activities, resources, responsibilities, timing and decision points. |
| 6. Accelerate learning into action | Document findings and decisions; change programs or resource allocations where justified; monitor results and revise questions. |

Sources: [Step 1](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/step-1-align-priorities), [Step 2](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/step-2-prepare-communication-and-constituent-engagement), [Step 3](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/step-3-identify-key-knowledge-gaps), [Step 4](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/step-4-develop-learning-questions-to-drive-decisions), [Step 5](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/step-5-implement-an-action-plan), [Step 6](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/step-6-accelerate-learning-into-action).

Step 4 asks what would happen if evidence contradicts expectations. That creates an opportunity to test the institution's preferred solution rather than using research to justify a predetermined answer. Step 6 makes the answer consequential: officials are asked to record a decision and its rationale, the resulting changes, expected effects and how those effects will be checked. Evidence can also support continuing an existing program or investigating further; discontinuation is one possible action, not a preset objective.

The guide's Exhibit 11, printed page 44 (physical PDF page 48), makes this visible in a single table: learning question → finding → evidence use → action → expected outcome → outcome measure → owner → timing. Its literacy numbers are illustrative examples, not measured results from the pilots.

[Getting started](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/getting-started) anticipates roughly three to six months to establish an initial agenda, with recurring team sessions. The longer process is iterative and extends across multiple years. Publication on September 9 therefore should not be confused with statewide implementation or completed evaluation on that date.

## What the pilots actually show

**Georgia: challenging the proposed remedy.** The team narrowed a broad human-capital effort toward special education teacher retention. Before adding proposed supports, it surveyed more than 2,600 educators to understand their needs and revised its assumptions and questions. This is evidence that inquiry changed the planning process. The case does not demonstrate a subsequent retention gain caused by the playbook. [Georgia case](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/case-studies/georgia).

**Kansas: repairing fragmentation.** The state used a shared logic model to connect accreditation, federal school identification, at-risk accountability and needs assessments. Its four fundamentals concern standards, instruction, balanced assessment and structured literacy. The demonstrated gain is a clearer shared framework across divisions; a causal improvement in student outcomes is not established. [Kansas case](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/case-studies/kansas).

**Tennessee: institutional memory through leadership change.** The case emphasizes maintaining knowledge and continuity across staff and administration transitions. It also develops questions about literacy and workforce pathways, including credentials, advising, transitions and work-based learning. Earlier literacy improvements cited as context must not be attributed to this newly released playbook. [Tennessee case](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/case-studies/tennessee).

**Utah: assisted drafting with human decisions.** Facilitators used Claude to generate alternative question wording, which people refined and selected. This demonstrates a bounded drafting use, not delegation of education policy authority to AI or a requirement to use a named product. [Utah case](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/case-studies/utah).

The other cases describe Arkansas constituent engagement, Idaho prioritization and initiative inventory, New Hampshire literacy planning and proposed legislative assistance, and Wisconsin early-childhood evidence coordination. These are varied development experiences rather than eight completed impact evaluations. New Hampshire's narrative includes prospective language about the July 2026–June 2027 period, indicating that publication date is not the date of every activity described. [Case index](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/case-studies).

## Who holds authority, and who supplies capacity?

State leadership supplies the priorities and decision authority. The method asks for an internal lead and executive participation, linking inquiry to officials who can act. Yet the guide favors external facilitation where available, including Regional Comprehensive Centers, Regional Educational Laboratories or other partners. This creates a real institutional question: can shared assistance build durable state expertise, or will crucial methodological knowledge remain with outside facilitators?

The [acknowledgments](https://compcenternetwork.org/resource/362/learning-agenda-playbook/acknowledgments) show a federally supported network involving NCC, regional centers and laboratories, state participants and outside experts. The resource is published through the NCC/Westat infrastructure. This is evidence of federal support for state-led decision capacity; it does not establish that Washington has disappeared from the operating system or that an outside organization controls state decisions. No specific award amount was established in this pass.

The [Capacity Continuum](https://compcenternetwork.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026-09/learning-agenda-capacity-continuum-20260908.pdf) provides nine dimensions and four developmental categories, from Emerging through Sustaining. It supports team reflection at several stages. It explicitly is not a high-stakes evaluation, and there is no minimum capacity rating required to proceed. It must not be described as a new federal state-ranking, grant-eligibility or compliance gate.

## Connection to the existing institutional-overhaul research

Our saved Education work distinguishes the department's legal existence from administrative service arrangements and payment operations. The Chronicle's August 15 Consummation Map remains the dated legal baseline for that earlier analysis. The September 2 PRWORA Education interlock separately tracks ED authority, DOL administration and grant/payment systems. Neither older file was comprehensively refreshed by this release review.

This playbook adds a complementary question: after administrative responsibilities move, what capability does the receiving institution possess to decide what works and change course? That joins the existing storyline through decision authority, evidence quality, resource allocation and continuity. It does not itself amend statutes, move grant ownership, authorize a new data-sharing regime, or change payment eligibility.

**Analytical inference:** the release is most significant as an attempted reform of institutional judgment. Counting funded activities is insufficient if institutions cannot determine whether those activities help students, identify contradictory evidence, act on findings and retain the knowledge after personnel change. The Georgia, Kansas and Tennessee cases give concrete examples of those problems and initial responses.

That interpretation remains conditional on use. A completed template can become another administrative ritual. Stronger evidence of transformation would be a published learning question tied to a real decision, a documented change in resources or program design, and subsequent outcome evidence that survives a leadership transition.

## Material next evidence

- A state publishes an adopted agenda with named decision owners, evidence activities and dates.
- Findings produce a documented budget, staffing, program-design or discontinuation decision.
- Measures distinguish improved student outcomes from counts of participants, grants or meetings.
- A state retains and uses the process across personnel or administration change.
- Contracts, grant awards or staffing records clarify the cost and durability of external facilitation and internal capacity.

These are research return criteria, not scheduled monitoring or new canonical watches.
