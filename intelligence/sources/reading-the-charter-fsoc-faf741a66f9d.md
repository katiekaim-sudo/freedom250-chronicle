# Reading the Charter — FSOC

## The 10-point hunt

**1. Immunities & liability shields.** None in this material — FSOC is a coordinating council of existing regulators, not a corporate entity that needs a suit-immunity clause of its own.

**2. Tax exemptions.** None.

**3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause.** §5321(b) is the tell: FSOC has almost no independent appointments of its own. Nine of ten voting seats are held *ex officio* by the sitting heads of other agencies (Treasury Secretary as Chairperson, Fed Chair, Comptroller of the Currency, CFPB Director, SEC Chair, FDIC Chairperson, CFTC Chairperson, FHFA Director, NCUA Chairman) — whoever runs those agencies automatically sits on the Council. The only Senate-confirmed, independently-termed seat is the "independent member... having insurance expertise" (§5321(b)(1)(J)), serving a 6-year term with an 18-month holdover provision (§5321(c)(4)) that lets a lapsed appointee keep the seat warm if the President is slow to renominate. §5321(c)(3): if any *member agency's* own head slot is vacant, the acting head fills the FSOC seat automatically — the Council's membership is designed to never have an empty chair, because it's borrowed, not owned.

**4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations.** Nothing in this material — FSOC has no funding section of its own in the captured sections; §5321(h) shows it runs on borrowed staff and services ("any department or agency... may provide to the Council... such services, funds, facilities, staff... as the Council may determine advisable") and detailed federal employees serve "without reimbursement" (§5321(j)). FSOC is a committee wearing other agencies' budgets, not a funded entity in its own right — contrast sharply with the CFPB and OFR, which both have dedicated funding mechanisms captured elsewhere in this library.

**5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs.** §5321(g): the Federal Advisory Committee Act (recodified as chapter 10 of title 5) explicitly does NOT apply to the Council or its advisory committees — a specific FACA exemption — except that if an advisory committee includes non-government members, the Council must publish their names. §5322(d)(5)(C) confirms FOIA (5 U.S.C. §552) DOES apply to data submitted to the Council, but with (5)(A)-(B) confidentiality and privilege-non-waiver protections layered on top — so the Council can receive confidential supervisory data from members without blowing anyone's privilege, while remaining nominally FOIA-reachable.

**6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses.** N/A — statutory creature amended by ordinary legislation (2017, 2018, 2022 amendments already show this).

**7. Emergency powers.** §5323(f): the "Emergency exception" is the sharpest tool in this material — the Council can waive the normal notice-and-hearing process (§5323(e)) for designating a nonbank financial company as systemically important, by a ⅔ supermajority including the Chairperson's affirmative vote, if it decides the normal process would let a threat mature further. Notice to the affected company can come as late as 24 hours *after* the waiver is already granted (§5323(f)(2)) — due process delivered after the fact, on the Council's own emergency finding.

**8. Definitions section quietly load-bearing.** Not captured directly in this pass, but the whole designation power in §5323 hinges on the undefined-here-but-elsewhere-defined term "nonbank financial company" — a term broad enough that its exact contours (not captured) determine how far the Board of Governors' reach can extend into non-bank America.

**9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution.** Not addressed — FSOC has no corporate life span; it exists as long as the statute says it does.

**10. Anything strange.** §5323(h) — the judicial review standard. If FSOC designates a company as systemically important and subject to Fed supervision, and the company sues, the court's review is expressly limited by statute to whether the Council's decision "was arbitrary and capricious" — Congress wrote the deferential administrative-law standard directly into the statute itself, rather than leaving it to background APA doctrine, making it explicit up front that a challenged company faces the highest bar available in American administrative law, not a fresh look at the merits. Combined with the ⅔-supermajority-plus-Chairperson voting rule that runs through nearly every consequential FSOC power (§5323(a)(1), (b)(1), (c)(1), (d)(2), (f)(1)) — the same structural fingerprint appears again and again: big decisions require a genuine supermajority AND the Treasury Secretary's personal sign-off, never a simple majority alone.

## Layer map

- **2010 — Dodd-Frank Title I, Subtitle A (Pub. L. 111-203):** creates the Council, its ten-agency-head membership, its coordinating/monitoring duties, its ⅔-supermajority nonbank-designation power, and its enhanced-prudential-standards recommendation authority.
- **2017 — Pub. L. 115-61:** adds the independent member's holdover provision (§5321(c)(4)) — a technical fix ensuring the insurance-expert seat never sits empty during a nomination gap.
- **2018 — Pub. L. 115-174 (EGRRCPA):** raises the automatic-enhanced-supervision asset threshold above the original $50 billion line (§5325(a)(2)(B)), shrinking the population of bank holding companies swept automatically into heightened prudential standards.
- **2022 — Pub. L. 117-286:** technical recodification substituting "chapter 10 of title 5" for "the Federal Advisory Committee Act" language in §5321(g) — no substantive change, a cross-reference cleanup after FACA's own recodification.

## Entity Theory read

FSOC isn't really its own creature — it's a committee-shaped hole where nine other regulators' heads sit down together, wearing their day jobs' authority, with only one truly independent seat (the insurance member) and no budget of its own. Its bite comes entirely from one power: the ⅔-supermajority-plus-Treasury-Secretary vote to declare a nonbank financial company systemically important and hand it to the Federal Reserve for supervision (§5323) — essentially a legislative-branch-granted authority to draft private companies into bank-style oversight without their consent, reviewable only under the most deferential standard courts have. Whoever holds the Treasury Secretary's chair holds the one vote every designation and every emergency waiver requires; control the Treasury Secretary and you control whether FSOC's teeth ever bite anyone.
