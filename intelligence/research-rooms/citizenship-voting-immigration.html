Postal workers helped run a Census test; no citizenship database shown

The 2026 Census test used postal workers as door-to-door counters in two cities and ran an online form that asks citizenship. The public record does not show postal workers asking citizenship or any joined enforcement database, and results are not yet out.

What we found

  • Census cut the test from six sites to Huntsville and Spartanburg on Feb 2, 2026; GAO says 10 of 19 planned activities were removed or reduced. Online response began May 1, door-to-door follow-up June 1, and Census says the test ended Aug 31.
  • The test covered about 154,600 housing units with about 25 postal and 25 regular Census takers per site. In Huntsville postal workers took a separate Census job after hours at $19.75 an hour; in Spartanburg they counted on their own delivery routes during postal work, on postal pay.
  • The online form uses American Community Survey content, including citizenship. The shorter in-person form (D6-QE-IFE) is not public, so it is not shown that postal workers ask about citizenship.
  • Census and USPS signed an interagency agreement that has not been published; a 2018 postal pilot was canceled because the agencies could not settle the legal conflicts. The governing records notice allows no routine uses, so it gives no path to share responses with DHS, ICE or election offices.
  • The approved cost estimate is $5 million, not counting IT and headquarters. The Commerce Secretary's Aug 11 claim of billions in savings has no numbers behind it in the public record.

Why it matters

It shows a possible change to the 2030 count being tested through normal procedure inside the existing Census system. So far the record supports a new workforce channel, not a merged citizenship-enforcement system.

What to watch

  • pending · Published test results on response, cost, quality, productivity and mail disruption.
  • summer 2027 · Census Operational Plan Baseline 2, the first 2030 design decision after the test.
  • 2028-03-30 · Proposed census questions due to Congress, which will show what the 2030 form asks.

Justice Department opinion widens which state offices must report people unlawfully present

A September 1, 2026 Justice Department legal opinion reads a 1996 welfare-law reporting duty to cover all of a participating state's agencies, not just the welfare office. It also drops the old rule that a final removal order was needed before the state 'knew'. No new procedure or actual report has followed yet.

What we found

  • The 1996 welfare law (PRWORA) requires states that get TANF funds to report, at least four times a year and on request, people they know are unlawfully present. A 1998 opinion and a 2000 notice had limited this to the benefit agency and required a formal finding backed by immigration authorities.
  • The new opinion, requested by HHS on June 16, 2026, withdraws the 1998 opinion. It says 'State' means the whole state and its agencies, and that 'knowledge' can come from a DHS notice, a person's admission, expired status records or plainly decisive documents. Suspicion alone is not enough.
  • It applies going forward only. Agencies may not reopen past agreements or claw back old funds. It says noncompliance could put program money at risk, but no withholding has been announced.
  • The study places it in a string of 2025–2026 actions it treats as one campaign: EO 14218 (Feb 19, 2025), the HHS benefit notice (Jul 10, 2025, partly blocked by courts), Justice opinions in Nov 2025, Dec 2025 and Feb 2026, a HUD proposed rule (Feb 20, 2026) and a Treasury/IRS proposed tax-credit rule (Aug 20, 2026).
  • No new reporting procedure, data format, state rollout, DHS intake or court ruling had been found by September 2. The SAVE system is legally barred from being used for noncriminal immigration enforcement, which leaves open how any reports would flow.

Why it matters

This is the thesis in close-up: a 30-year-old law is given new reach through a legal reinterpretation, using identity systems that already exist, without Congress passing anything new. The study notes that everything after the opinion, including any reports, enforcement or court test, is still ahead.

What to watch

  • pending · A replacement HHS, SSA or DHS reporting procedure, or the first state directive or expanded report.
  • pending · Court challenges on whether a funding condition run by one office can reach unrelated state agencies.
  • pending · Whether the HUD and Treasury/IRS proposed rules become final rules.

White House election files show real risks, not proof of changed results

The July 16, 2026 White House release (58 PDFs, 269 pages) documents four separate things: foreign collection of voter data, internal intelligence disputes, weak state and local election networks, and questionable voter registrations. As released, none of it proves an ineligible or altered vote changed an outcome.

What we found

  • Voter data: reporting describes a 204,822,241-record U.S. voter dataset and Chinese analysis involving at least 18 states. Some later state data was bought or downloaded from commercial sites; the rest of the sourcing is partly redacted.
  • Intelligence: internal emails show a real dispute over how China reporting was described, and also show FBI analysts challenging the minority view's sourcing.
  • Election systems: CISA found serious patching, login and remote-access weaknesses, and authorized testers took full control of some state and local networks within hours or days. No record shows a foreign actor changed cast votes or certified totals.
  • Registration: Michigan records show possibly fake registration applications and a multi-year investigation. DHS match figures on noncitizens and deceased registrants have no published method; reported maxima in DHS letters total 256,463.
  • On June 22, 2026 a D.C. federal court vacated the 2025 DHS and SSA records notices and the modified SAVE system, and denied a stay on July 8; the appeal (No. 26-5243) is pending. The 'SAVE America Act' is not law; the live vehicle is the House amendment to S. 1383, passed February 11, 2026.

Why it matters

Voter-eligibility checks are being pushed through agency databases, grant terms, court fights and bills rather than one sweeping change. Keeping what is documented apart from what is claimed shows how far that push actually reaches.

What to watch

  • pending · D.C. Circuit ruling in No. 26-5243 on the SAVE system and any replacement records notices.
  • pending · Senate action on the House amendment to S. 1383.
  • pending · DHS's matching method, data dates and state-by-state outcomes behind its registration figures.

Current law bars a third Trump election; any 2028 push would be a fight over process

The 22nd Amendment bars anyone from being elected President more than twice, and Trump was elected in 2016 and 2024. The study maps how a 2028 push could still press on filing, ballot, elector and vote-counting steps, but that pressure would not change the law.

What we found

  • At a July 24, 2026 dinner speech Trump spoke of his "intention to run for a fourth term," said "I won three times," called 2020 rigged and said "we do have to tighten up these elections." The speech created no candidacy or legal status.
  • No 2028 FEC candidate filing was found. His 2024 campaign committee became a leadership PAC, NEVER SURRENDER, INC. "Trump 2028" merchandise is a product, not a legal filing.
  • H.J.Res.29 (Rep. Andy Ogles, January 23, 2025) would allow up to three elections but not after two consecutive terms. It has zero cosponsors and no action, and it only helps Trump if 2020 stays counted as Biden's win.
  • The FEC does not rule on constitutional eligibility. In Hassan v. Colorado and Lindsay v. Bowen, courts let states keep clearly ineligible candidates off ballots. No court has decided a 22nd Amendment case.
  • The speech came after a week of election moves: White House election-integrity releases (July 16), White House/DHS letters to states on SAVE voter matching (July 17), and a July 23 multistate lawsuit over election conditions and a 20% holdback on FY2026 homeland security grants.

Why it matters

The study finds the live action is less about 2028 and more about control of the rules, data, grants and systems for the 2026 and 2028 elections, pursued through agencies and grant conditions. It stresses the 2028 strategy is its own constructed argument, not one any official has adopted.

What to watch

  • January 3, 2027 · H.J.Res.29 dies if Congress does not act by the end of the 119th Congress.
  • pending · The multistate lawsuit over election grant conditions (D.R.I. No. 1:26-cv-00485).
  • November 7, 2028 – January 20, 2029 · Election day, elector votes (December 19), the count in Congress (January 6) and inauguration.

Court requires notice before deportations to third countries; DHS asks Supreme Court to pause it

A federal appeals court on September 18, 2026 largely upheld a ruling that DHS's guidance for deporting people to countries not named in their removal orders was unlawful, because it did not give fair notice and a chance to raise fear claims. DHS asked the Supreme Court for a stay on September 24.

What we found

  • The fight began with a February 18, 2025 ICE directive and DHS guidance of March 30, 2025 that allowed some removals without further process when the U.S. had diplomatic assurances.
  • A district court set the guidance aside on February 25, 2026 under the Administrative Procedure Act. The First Circuit affirmed on September 18, 2026, except for two rulings about the order in which DHS must try countries, which it threw out for lack of standing.
  • On September 23 the First Circuit lifted its March 16 stay, so the judgment took effect. On September 24 DHS filed Supreme Court application No. 26A406; Justice Jackson asked for a response by 4 p.m. EDT on September 28.
  • DHS's filing says it canceled a flight carrying 70 people, pulled nearly 150 people from flights and faced added cost and diplomatic strain. These are claims in a court filing, not checked against agency records.
  • DHS still has the legal power to make third-country removals. What changed is the procedure it must follow: effective notice of the destination and a real chance to raise persecution or torture fears.

Why it matters

It shows how a major enforcement policy is fought out through the system's own machinery: agency guidance, the Administrative Procedure Act, appeals and emergency applications. The power itself stays; the courts are reshaping the process around it.

What to watch

  • 2026-09-28 · The response filed in No. 26A406 and any government reply.
  • pending · Whether the Supreme Court grants an administrative stay or a stay pending certiorari.
  • pending · Any new DHS guidance setting out notice, screening and hearing steps, and records of resumed or canceled flights.
