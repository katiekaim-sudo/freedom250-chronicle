SR 26-6: Verifiable Digital Credentials Inside the Bank Identity Gate

SR 26-6: Verifiable Digital Credentials Inside the Bank Identity Gate

Evidence cutoff: September 8, 2026, 21:30 EDT
Geographic scope: United States
Research state: Current Workbench answer; primary-source deep dive
Authority boundary: This report records public law, regulation, guidance, technical standards infrastructure and demonstrated architecture. It does not treat a proposed rule, draft NIST practice guide, reference implementation, industry request, technical capability or Workbench interpretation as an implemented legal mandate.
Promotion state: Registered as Workbench Research Desk supporting evidence. Not promoted to the Chronicle vault or rebuilt/shipped into the wrapper or installed Observatory app.

Bottom line

SR 26-6 is a narrow but consequential identity-infrastructure change. On September 8, 2026, the Federal Reserve Board transmitted joint interagency FAQs that say an unexpired, qualifying government-issued verifiable digital credential (VDC), including a state mobile driver's license (mDL), may count as the government-issued identification used in the documentary branch of a bank or credit union's existing Customer Identification Program (CIP). The same release also updates a 2005 FAQ to say an electronic credential, including a VDC, may be used as one non-documentary verification method when the institution's CIP permits it.1

The agencies did not create a digital-ID mandate, a right to have an mDL accepted, a new account type, a new payment rail or a new Bank Secrecy Act obligation. They did something more specific: they removed an interpretive barrier that could keep a digitally signed state credential outside the regulation's familiar category of an unexpired government-issued identity document. The 2003 rule, the bank's written risk-based CIP, the required identity fields, the reasonable-belief standard, fraud escalation, recordkeeping and examination remain in place.2

The practical effect is therefore permission plus a supervisory pathway, not automatic implementation. A financial institution must choose to accept the credential, write that method into its CIP, deploy technology capable of extracting and validating the relevant information, decide what additional checks are needed, preserve the required records and defend the result to its regulator. The institution retains the compliance risk even when a state, wallet provider, verification vendor or trust-list service supplies part of the technical chain.

For the working Fed-castration theory, the result cuts in two directions. It moves a high-volume identity operation toward state issuers, private wallets, verification software and a non-Federal trust-list utility. But the legal gate remains a Treasury/interagency BSA rule interpreted centrally and distributed through all twelve Reserve Banks to supervised institutions and examiners. This is better described as technical outsourcing inside an incumbent supervisory constitution than as a transfer of monetary, account-access or central-bank authority.

What changed, and what did not

Question September 8 answer Legal or operational effect
What is a VDC? A data structure containing individual information, digitally signed by its issuing source, cryptographically bound to a device and protected by an activation factor. Creates a specific interagency working definition. It is not a definition of every object marketed as a “verifiable credential.”
Can a government-issued VDC be a documentary method? Yes, if unexpired, evidences nationality or residence, bears a photograph or similar safeguard, the institution has appropriate extraction technology and its CIP allows the method. A qualifying mDL can occupy the same regulatory category as a physical driver's license or passport.
Can an electronic credential be a non-documentary method? Yes, as one method allowed by the institution's CIP. Updates the 2005 electronic-credential FAQ and expressly names VDCs.
Who bears responsibility for a non-government third-party credential? The bank or credit union must ensure the third party uses the same level of authentication the institution itself would use. Vendor or issuer involvement does not move the CIP duty off the institution.
Must an institution accept an mDL? No. The rule neither requires nor prohibits reliance. No customer acceptance right and no institution implementation deadline.
Did the agencies amend 31 CFR 1020.220? No. No new final rule, statutory duty or compliance date.
Did the agencies establish a new supervisory expectation? They expressly say no. Examiners receive the clarification, but the underlying risk-based standard remains the examination anchor.
Does the release cover legal entities? The new government-VDC question concerns natural-person customers. Corporate existence and beneficial-owner requirements remain separate.
Does the release create a national credential or identity database? No. It permits reliance on credentials issued elsewhere; it does not create an issuer, wallet, registry of people or federal identity number.
Does it authorize a payment, stablecoin or Federal Reserve account? No. Identity verification is upstream of product eligibility, account approval, sanctions, transaction monitoring and rail access.

The exact legal move

1. The statute supplied the identity duty

Section 326 of the USA PATRIOT Act added 31 U.S.C. 5318(l), directing Treasury and the federal functional regulators to establish minimum procedures for verifying the identity of people seeking to open financial accounts, maintaining verification records and checking designated government lists.3

2. The 2003 rule supplied a technology-neutral structure

The joint final rule was published May 9, 2003, became effective June 9, 2003 and required compliance by October 1, 2003. Now codified for banks at 31 CFR 1020.220, it requires a written CIP appropriate to the institution's size and business and incorporated into its AML program.4

Before opening an account, the institution generally must obtain at least:

  • name;
  • date of birth for an individual;
  • address; and
  • an identification number.

Verification can occur within a reasonable time after account opening. The procedures must be risk-based and must let the institution form a reasonable belief that it knows the customer's true identity. They must specify when the institution uses documents, non-documentary methods or both.4

The documentary branch already referred to “unexpired government-issued identification” evidencing nationality or residence and bearing a photograph or similar safeguard, with a driver's license and passport as examples. The rule did not say the identification had to be plastic, paper or visually inspected by a teller. The September 2026 interpretation uses that technology-neutral language to admit the digital form.1

3. The 2005 FAQ had already admitted electronic credentials—but only as a

non-documentary method

The agencies' April 28, 2005 FAQs said a bank could obtain an electronic credential, such as a digital certificate, as one non-documentary method. SR 26-6 amends that FAQ to use current terminology and expressly include VDCs. The old FAQ survives; it is not superseded wholesale. SR 05-9's page now records both a June 2, 2026 revision removing reputational-risk references and the separate September 8 amendment made by SR 26-6.5

4. SR 26-6 opens a second route

The new FAQ's important language is that a qualifying government-issued VDC “would qualify” as government-issued identification for the documentary method. That is the substantive clarification. A digitally presented state license no longer has to be treated only as an electronic, non-documentary signal simply because it is not a physical card.1

The two routes should not be collapsed:

Route Typical object What the institution records Key residual question
Documentary Qualifying unexpired state mDL or other government VDC Description of the document: type, identification number, place of issuance and, if present, issuance and expiration dates Is it valid, within the CIP and sufficient to support reasonable belief?
Non-documentary Electronic credential or VDC used as one electronic signal, including one issued or maintained by a non-government third party Description of the method and its results Is the source/authentication level adequate, and what corroboration is required?
Combination VDC plus database, customer contact, biometric/liveness or other check Both document description and method/results as applicable Do the joined checks resolve discrepancies and presentation risk?

A government-issued mDL can be the object used in a documentary method. The same general class of digital credential can also participate in a non-documentary method. Classification depends on the issuer, credential and institutional use—not on the marketing label alone.

What a bank must actually build

SR 26-6 does not install a reader or rewrite an institution's CIP. A plausible implementation chain, synthesized from the rule, FAQ and NIST/AAMVA public architecture, is:

  1. Policy gate. The board- or management-approved CIP identifies which government VDCs, channels, customers and account types the institution will accept and whether the method is documentary, non-documentary or combined.
  2. Presentation request. In a branch or remote flow, the bank's reader or verifier requests the identity attributes needed for the account-opening process.
  3. Holder activation. The wallet requires an activation factor such as a PIN, password, face or fingerprint before releasing the credential.
  4. Issuer-signature validation. The verifier checks that the credential and attributes were digitally signed by a trusted issuing authority and have not been altered.
  5. Device-binding/session checks. The system checks proof tied to the credential's device and protects the presentation against replay or session substitution.
  6. Attribute extraction. The system extracts the relevant fields. SR 26-6 conditions acceptance on the institution having the technology or systems to do this.
  7. Fraud and person-to-credential checks. The institution considers fraud signals, discrepancies and whether another method is needed to connect the presenter to the state-issued identity.
  8. Reasonable-belief decision. The institution—not the wallet—decides whether it knows the customer's true identity to the regulatory standard.
  9. Account outcome. Its CIP governs whether to open, restrict, delay or close the account and whether suspicious-activity reporting should be considered.
  10. Record creation and retention. The institution records the required customer data and the description of the document or methods/results.

NIST's financial-sector mDL project demonstrates both cross-device and same-device patterns. In a cross-device flow, a customer may open a bank site on a computer, scan a session QR code with the phone, review the requested attributes, unlock the wallet and present the license. The verifier can obtain trusted issuer keys, validate the signature and send verified attributes to the bank's identity-management system. NIST labels this a reference implementation and its March 2026 SP 1800-42A an initial public draft; it is not the mandated architecture for SR 26-6.6

AAMVA's DTS is a meaningful infrastructure node. It says participating issuer programs must adhere to ISO/IEC 18013-5, AAMVA's implementation guidelines and key-management practices; relying parties download the VICAL into their reader technology before engaging with an mDL. This can remove the need for every bank to negotiate separately with every DMV for public keys.7

But it is not a federal database of credential holders. The public description is a repository and distribution service for issuer public keys. The bank still receives the selected credential data from the presentation path. AAMVA's terms also disclaim warranties and place use risk on relying parties, including limitations on AAMVA and issuer liability. The operational trust hub therefore does not absorb the institution's CIP liability.8

The most important technical distinction: signature validation is not

identity completion

An mDL can improve the answer to “did the state issue this data, and was it altered?” A physical card inspected over video or submitted as a photograph is more vulnerable to image manipulation, counterfeit templates and uncertain security-feature inspection. Public-key verification can make credential authenticity machine-testable.

That does not answer every identity question:

  • Was the DMV record itself obtained fraudulently?
  • Is the license revoked or superseded even if its displayed expiration date has not passed?
  • Was the credential provisioned to the correct wallet and device?
  • Is the phone now controlled by the rightful holder?
  • Did malware or a malicious application intercept the session?
  • Was the user tricked into approving a presentation initiated by an attacker?
  • Does a face/fingerprint unlock prove liveness and rightful use at the bank's required assurance level?
  • Are the presented attributes sufficient to satisfy the institution's CIP and risk profile?

NIST's mDL issuance profile makes this residual work explicit. It recommends comparison of a live facial sample with the DMV system-of-record image, presentation-attack detection, fraud programs, device and velocity checks, red-team testing, and a way to invalidate or status-check attribute bundles. It also warns that status methods should avoid telling the issuer where a specific person used an mDL. These are recommended controls in an emerging profile, not requirements added by SR 26-6.9

The interagency definition itself also matters. Its VDC is digitally signed, device-bound and activation-protected. A generic W3C Verifiable Credential is not automatically an SR 26-6 VDC, and “verifiable credential,” “digital certificate,” “mobile ID” and “mDL” should not be treated as interchangeable without testing those properties.

Data extraction, copies and retention

The phrase “extract the relevant information” does not mean the bank must copy the entire credential. The existing CIP rule requires the bank to retain:

  • the identifying information collected at account opening;
  • a description of any documentary evidence relied on, including document type, identification number, place of issuance and any issuance/expiration dates;
  • a description of non-documentary methods and results; and
  • the resolution of substantive discrepancies.

Identifying information is retained for five years after the account closes (or a credit-card account closes or becomes dormant). Document descriptions, methods/results and discrepancy resolutions are retained for five years after the record is made.4

The FFIEC manual is unusually clear: the CIP rule does not require a copy of the identifying document. An institution may keep one, and risk or another law/purpose may warrant it, but a description can satisfy the CIP record. That distinction survives the move from plastic to digital.10

This creates three separate data decisions that should not be collapsed:

  1. Presentation: which attributes the bank requests from the wallet.
  2. Verification processing: what the verifier temporarily sees while validating the presentation.
  3. Institutional retention: what the bank or its vendors preserve after the identity decision.

NIST's draft reference work shows a privacy-preserving design in which only attributes required for account opening are shared, the verifier deletes mDL attributes after disclosure to the bank's identity system, and issuer keys can be used to verify without contacting the issuer at transaction time. That can reduce overcollection and issuer tracking. It is a demonstrated design choice, not a privacy guarantee in SR 26-6.11

The reverse is also possible. A bank or verifier could request more attributes, retain full presentations, create a shadow profile, join wallet metadata to other activity or call an issuer/status service in a way that reveals use. NIST's privacy analysis names surveillance by verifier and issuer as risks. SR 26-6 does not prescribe minimization, prohibit correlation, specify deletion, create a correction right or identify who receives presentation metadata.

The last distinction is essential: CIP onboarding, account authentication and transaction authorization are different clocks and control decisions. NIST's reference build explores re-presenting an mDL during high-risk transactions, but SR 26-6 speaks to customer identity verification under CIP. It does not make an mDL a universal authenticator or payment authorization instrument.12

The bank still owns the judgment

The FAQ says that if a government VDC shows indications of fraud, the institution must consider that fact in deciding whether it can form the required reasonable belief. The existing rule also requires procedures for when not to open an account, what use is permitted while verification is pending, when to close after failed verification and when a suspicious activity report should be considered.14

The bank may use an agent or service provider to perform pieces of the work, but that is not the same as the rule's tightly conditioned reliance on another regulated financial institution. Formal reliance requires, among other things, reasonable reliance, another federally regulated institution subject to an AML program rule, a contract and annual certification. A wallet or identity vendor does not become such a relying institution merely by verifying a credential.2

For a non-government credential, the amended FAQ adds a direct responsibility: the bank or credit union must ensure the third party uses the same level of authentication the institution itself would use. The FAQ does not specify an assurance-level number, approved-vendor list, certification regime or audit test. Those are important unresolved implementation questions.

A buried citation-lineage problem

The amended FAQ ends with “See also FFIEC guidance titled ‘Authentication in an Electronic Banking Environment’ (Updated: September 2026).” The original FAQ cited a July 30, 2001 document under that title. FFIEC later issued Authentication in an Internet Banking Environment in 2005, supplementary guidance in 2011, and then Authentication and Access to Financial Institution Services and Systems in August 2021. FFIEC says the 2021 guidance replaced the 2005 and 2011 documents.20

The September 2026 parenthetical therefore should not be silently promoted to proof that FFIEC issued a new authentication framework that month. On the public record located at cutoff, it appears in the amended FAQ and may date the FAQ revision rather than a separately published FFIEC instrument. The live FFIEC cybersecurity page still identifies the August 2021 guidance as the current resource. The operative prudent reading is: institutions must join SR 26-6 to the current layered, risk-based authentication framework, but a distinct September 2026 FFIEC authentication release was not located.20

What remains separate

Legal-entity and beneficial-ownership identity

The new FAQ concerns verification of natural-person customers. It does not replace the separate documentary rules for proving a corporation, partnership or trust exists, nor the beneficial-ownership requirements for legal-entity customers. A human's mDL can help verify that human; it does not by itself prove the human owns or controls a company.13

Customer due diligence, sanctions and suspicious-activity monitoring

The rule expressly leaves other BSA obligations unaffected. SR 26-6 does not complete customer-risk profiling, ongoing monitoring, OFAC screening, beneficial-ownership work, fraud investigation or SAR analysis. A valid credential can belong to a sanctioned person, a money launderer or a victim whose account is controlled by someone else.

Stablecoin-issuer CIP

The June 18, 2026 joint proposal for permitted payment stablecoin issuers is a different legal object. It would implement the GENIUS Act's direction to treat those issuers as BSA financial institutions and require effective CIPs. At this cutoff, the located official sources still describe it as a proposed rule; no final rule was located.14

SR 26-6 applies existing bank/credit-union CIP interpretation to digital credentials. It does not finalize the stablecoin proposal, confer stablecoin issuer status, authorize a wallet as a bank, grant Federal Reserve account access or determine which entity is the “customer” in every token arrangement.

A federal digital identity mandate

No such mandate appears in SR 26-6. It is voluntary for the financial institution, contingent on its CIP and technology, and limited to verifying customers under an existing law. H.R. 7270, introduced January 27, 2026, is a separate proposal concerning identity fraud and state digital credentials; its introduced text would bar requiring a person to obtain or use a digital credential and preserve physical credentials. It was not established as law in this review and does not supply SR 26-6's authority.15

Why the change arrived now: established context and bounded inference

The agencies did not publish a detailed purpose statement with the FAQs. The following context is established; the causal synthesis is an inference:

  • FinCEN and FDIC ran a 2022 digital-identity tech sprint in which proposed solutions included W3C credentials and ISO-compliant mDLs.16
  • FinCEN's 2024 Identity Project divided failures into validation, verification and authentication and said mDLs could address impersonation and strengthen AML/CFT compliance amid rising AI threats.17
  • NIST's NCCoE began a financial-services mDL use case in September 2024, published an initial draft practice guide on March 18, 2026 and worked with government, bank, technology, standards and wallet participants.6
  • AAMVA's live DTS supplies a common issuer-key distribution layer for conforming participating jurisdictions.7
  • Industry comments to the FDIC in 2025 reported that compliance teams were reluctant to treat novel mDLs as CIP documents without regulatory clarity. That comment is stakeholder evidence, not an agency finding.18

The bounded inference is that SR 26-6 follows technical maturation plus demand for examiner-safe legal classification. The agencies did not order adoption; they aligned a 2003 technology-neutral rule with an emerging state credential format after the standards, reference architecture and issuer-key layer became concrete enough for institutional use.

Federal Reserve and twelve-Bank role

SR 26-6 is addressed to the officer in charge of supervision at each Federal Reserve Bank. It applies to all Federal Reserve-supervised financial institutions subject to the BSA. The Board asks the Reserve Banks to distribute the letter to supervised domestic and foreign financial institutions and to supervisory and examination staff.19

That language assigns the same operational role to all twelve Banks:

  1. receive the Board's common interagency interpretation;
  2. distribute it through district supervisory channels;
  3. put examination staff on the same interpretive footing; and
  4. field implementation through the Federal Reserve supervisory structure.

The letter does not assign a particular Reserve Bank to operate the VDC technology, maintain a trust list, contract with wallet providers, issue a credential or make a national policy independently. There is no district specialization in this release comparable to New York's market operations, Richmond's National IT concentration or FRFS payment-service assignments.

This is exactly where the Reserve Banks' often-overlooked role matters: the Board and interagency group can formulate one interpretation, while the district Banks are the supervisory delivery and examination network. The technical work may decentralize; the examination lattice does not.

Theory assessment

What strengthens the functional-unbundling reading

  • A core bank-entry fact can arrive as a portable signed state credential rather than through a bank's visual inspection of a physical card.
  • State DMVs remain the authoritative source; wallet and verification providers mediate presentation; AAMVA can centralize issuer-key discovery.
  • Banks can integrate identity proofing into remote, automated customer acquisition and later step-up flows.
  • Selective disclosure and local key verification can reduce direct issuer contact and whole-document copying.
  • The technical identity edge can therefore move outside the Reserve Banks and outside a bank's branch network.

What weakens an annihilation or replacement reading

  • The duty is still rooted in federal statute and 31 CFR 1020.220.
  • FinCEN, the Board, FDIC, NCUA and OCC issued one joint interpretation.
  • Every institution must preserve its own written CIP and reasonable-belief decision.
  • Federal and state supervisors can examine the implementation.
  • All twelve Reserve Banks remain distribution and examination nodes for Federal Reserve-supervised institutions.
  • No monetary-policy, reserve-account, clearing, settlement, liquidity, currency-issuance or emergency-lending power moved.

Controlling ruling

SR 26-6 mildly strengthens the “functional unbundling” leg and modestly strengthens the incumbent-control counterweight at the same time. Identity evidence becomes more portable and more dependent on state/private technical infrastructure, but its legal sufficiency is absorbed into a common federal supervisory framework. The Fed's direct operating role in identity technology does not expand; its Board-and-district role in defining and examining the banking gate remains intact.

The release does not establish “castration of the Fed” as a fact. It is a specific example of the larger mechanism under study: functions can migrate to external technical layers while the incumbent institution retains the rule, risk, examination and ultimate access boundary.

Unresolved records and next watches

These are the high-value records needed to see whether interpretive permission becomes material infrastructure:

  1. Institution adoption: named banks and credit unions publicly accepting mDLs for CIP, with production dates and supported jurisdictions/wallets.
  2. CIP amendments: institution-level descriptions of documentary versus non-documentary treatment, fallback methods and discrepancy handling.
  3. Examiner procedures: any later FFIEC manual, work-program or examination update that turns the FAQ into testable examiner steps.
  4. Authentication citation: a corrected or clarified interagency pointer explaining the FAQ's legacy title, its September 2026 parenthetical and the 2021 FFIEC replacement guidance; no separate September 2026 framework was located.
  5. NIST finalization: final SP 1800-42 volumes, changes from the initial draft and any adopted assurance profile.
  6. Trust-list operations: participating jurisdictions, update frequency, revocation behavior, incident history, audit/certification and reader conformance.
  7. Wallet governance: approved-wallet criteria, platform fees, interoperability, portability and recovery when a device is lost or a platform exits.
  8. Data fields: actual attributes requested and retained by institutions, including whether photographs, device metadata, presentation histories or full credential payloads are stored.
  9. Vendor liability: contracts allocating false-acceptance, false-rejection, key-compromise, breach, uptime, evidence-preservation and correction duties.
  10. Customer correction: routes when DMV data, credential status, bank records or a vendor fraud score is wrong.
  11. Equity and fallback: continued availability of physical and non-smartphone identity methods.
  12. Stablecoin rule: a final rule, effective date and customer/account definition for permitted payment stablecoin issuers.
  13. Scope expansion: later use of VDCs for account recovery, high-risk transactions, payments, government benefits or Federal Reserve account eligibility; none is established by SR 26-6.
  14. Incident clock: a public fraud, trust-list, wallet or issuer-key incident that tests who actually absorbs loss and remediation duties.

Claim audit

Claim Ruling
“Banks can now use a qualifying state mDL as the documentary government ID under CIP.” Established, with conditions.
“Every bank must now accept a digital driver's license.” False. Acceptance is optional and CIP-dependent.
“The regulation changed on September 8.” False. The agencies issued interpretive FAQs under the existing rule.
“A cryptographic signature completes KYC.” False. It authenticates issuer/data integrity; reasonable belief and other BSA controls remain.
“The bank no longer carries identity liability.” False. The institution remains responsible for its CIP and third-party authentication.
“Banks must retain the full mDL or a license image.” Not established; CIP itself does not require a copy. Required identity information and document/method descriptions must be retained.
“Selective disclosure means the bank receives no unnecessary data.” Technically possible, not guaranteed by SR 26-6.
“AAMVA now holds a national database of every mDL user.” Not established. Its public DTS role is issuer-public-key distribution, not a holder registry.
“The Federal Reserve now operates digital identity.” False. The Board interprets and the Reserve Banks distribute/examine; the release assigns no credential operation.
“This is the stablecoin identity rule.” False. The stablecoin-issuer CIP remained a separate proposal at cutoff.
“This creates a national digital ID mandate.” False. No mandate, universal issuer or required consumer use appears.
“This is irrelevant because it is only an FAQ.” Too dismissive. It is not a new rule, but it supplies a common interagency supervisory interpretation and removes a concrete adoption ambiguity.
