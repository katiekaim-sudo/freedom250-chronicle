Fusion power has many separate gates, and none has reached the grid

Getting from fusion strategy to power on the grid takes separate steps: money, a named company and machine, test results, licenses, a site, a grid hookup and measured delivery. At the August 15, 2026 cutoff the U.S. had a final roadmap, funded research and early licensing steps, but no plant making electricity.

What we found

  • DOE's August 15, 2026 'Energy Without Limits' social-media thread announced no new experiment, award, license or power result. It pointed back to the final Fusion Science and Technology Roadmap released June 9, 2026.
  • Money: the FY2026 spending law (P.L. 119-74, January 23) gave Fusion Energy Sciences $805.657 million ($634.973 million research, $170.684 million U.S. ITER construction). The eight-company Milestone Program showed $110.685 million obligated and $28.490 million paid out.
  • Rules: NRC published a proposed fusion-machine rule on February 26, 2026 (comments closed May 27); it is not final. Tennessee's state fusion rules took effect June 9, 2026, and Type One Energy reported filing an initial license application for its Bull Run project on January 29. No license has been issued.
  • Science: the National Ignition Facility has reached ignition repeatedly, such as 8.6 MJ from 2.08 MJ of laser energy on April 7, 2025 (target gain 4.13). That is gain at the target, not net energy for the facility or electricity.
  • DOE's claim of four times more energy per kilogram of fuel compares the reactions themselves, not plant efficiency, grid output or cost.

Why it matters

Fusion is being built through existing machinery: appropriations, a new DOE Office of Fusion, NRC rulemaking and state rules, each on its own clock. The study keeps announcements apart from what has actually been funded, licensed or built.

What to watch

  • 2027-12-31 · Statutory deadline for NRC's fusion-machine framework; watch for a final, effective rule.
  • pending · Tennessee completeness review and any issued license for Type One's Bull Run project.
  • pending · First Fusion BRIDGE awards with cost share, site and construction authority.

AI helps run turbines, but people still make the big calls

Turbines are run by layers of sensors, controls, monitoring centers, analytics and work-order systems. AI does narrow jobs now, such as inspection, spotting problems and small tuning loops, but no fleet is run by AI, and most newly ordered big gas turbines won't run until 2027 or later.

What we found

  • Remote monitoring is at scale: GE Vernova reports more than 1,000 monitored plants and Siemens Energy more than 800 units (vendor figures). GE's Autonomous Tuning adjusts combustion every two seconds, but only inside fixed safety logic.
  • Fully unattended running is rare. Siemens Energy and Volkswagen completed a certified 72-hour unattended plant test in October 2025. In July 2026 DOE released a test environment (Stormbreaker) to study how AI agents fail in power systems.
  • EIA lists 86 GW of planned U.S. additions for 2026, only 6.3 GW of it gas. Of 253 GW of gas in grid-connection queues at end-2025, only 18% had a draft or signed agreement.
  • Order books far exceed near-term builds: GE Vernova reported 116 GW of gas backlog plus reserved slots (July 2026) and Siemens Energy 87 GW of gas commitments, 24 GW tied to data centers. These are global and can't be added up; GE says most recent orders deliver in 2027 or later.
  • Examples: Pin Oak Creek and T.H. Wharton (415 MW) in Texas began commercial operation in April and May 2026; Plant Yates in Georgia targets end-2027; Duke's Anderson County plant in South Carolina is approved, with service targeted for 2031.

Why it matters

The power buildout behind AI is being wired with data systems that could one day trigger loan draws or payments, but only once contracts say so. For now, safety and return-to-service authority stays with engineers, owners and regulators, not software.

What to watch

  • by end of 2026 · Final investment decision on the Kilby, Texas project, with first power targeted for 2028.
  • 2027 · Delivery of most recently ordered gas turbines, and the Homer City and Plant Yates targets.
  • pending · The first loan or project contract that treats a sensor-confirmed event as a trigger for a draw or payment.

Instant payment can't replace the legal steps in running power plants

Programmable payments could change how turbine and power-plant obligations are managed, but they can't fold safety, contract, sanctions, funding, accounting and dispute steps into one instant step. A payment that completes technically is not the same as one that is legally final.

What we found

  • Physical events already create money obligations: a GE service agreement filed by Guernsey Power charges a fee based on gas-turbine fired hours, and FERC Order 825 settles power markets in five-minute intervals.
  • Asset-management software such as IBM Maximo already creates bills, credits and warranty claims, but the study found no proof that it turns turbine data into final cash.
  • Programmable bank money is live: JPMorgan's Kinexys serves Siemens, and Citi's tokenized-deposit service is live, though its Maersk use was a pilot.
  • No official source joins a named working turbine, service contract or asset system to those payment rails. The pieces exist separately.
  • The study's best first uses are narrow, rule-based payments such as fired-hour fees, inspection-milestone releases and parts escrow, with a human path for exceptions.

Why it matters

It tests a core claim of the thesis: new payment machinery can be wired into old systems, but sanctions law, contracts, courts and banks still decide what counts as final. Iran is offered as the case where technical speed and legal finality split.

What to watch

  • pending · A named power site where a turbine event is linked to an actual payment through a programmable rail.
  • pending · A service or power contract that spells out meter authority, sanctions, dispute and correction terms for automated payment.

No federal data-center ban, just scattered permits and leases

No federal law bans data centers or sets rules for their power queues, water or emissions. The July 2025 executive order to speed them up has produced real leases, selections and permit changes, but no finished project or blanket exemption.

What we found

  • EO 14318 (signed July 23, 2025) covers projects over 100 MW of new load or $500 million in capital spending and tells agencies to speed permits and offer federal land. It is not itself a permit, lease, waiver or funding, and it keeps existing law in place.
  • Federal land: DOE confirmed an SB Energy affiliate is leasing an initial 189 acres at Portsmouth, with groundbreaking March 20, 2026. Savannah River (Amentum, July 20, 2026), Paducah (Brookfield, 1.8 GW) and Army sites at Fort Bliss and Dugway are selections or negotiations, not final leases.
  • Permits: the Army's Nationwide Permit 39 names data centers and took effect March 15, 2026, generally capped at half an acre of waters lost. EPA's brownfield reuse guidance and priority chemical-review handling change process, not the underlying rules.
  • Congress: bills for a moratorium, power-queue rules, cost allocation, energy and water reporting, an emissions fee, a federal-land ban and a 1-cent-per-kWh excise tax (introduced Aug. 13, 2026) were all only introduced and referred to committee.
  • The only enacted pieces are two narrow FY2026 NDAA sections: a Navy nuclear pilot due to start by December 18, 2026 that may weigh data-center co-location, and military data-center energy and water estimates due March 1, 2027.

Why it matters

Data-center growth is being pushed by executive order and agency tools (leases, permits, guidance) inside existing law, while Congress has not acted. Ordinary environmental and utility rules still apply project by project.

What to watch

  • 2026-12-18 · Deadline for the Navy to start its nuclear-energy pilot at naval installations.
  • 2027-03-01 · First military data-center energy, water and community-effect estimates due.
  • pending · Kentucky PSC approval of the Paducah power agreement, and executed leases at Savannah River, Fort Bliss and Dugway.

Who loses money if AI demand falls depends on each deal

AI infrastructure is financed through several separate structures (GPU funds, GPU loan vehicles, cloud companies, data-center property and power plants), so who owns the chips, who owes the debt and who takes the loss has to be traced one deal at a time.

What we found

  • NVIDIA announced memoranda with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to raise more than $500 billion of outside capital over time. Final agreements are not signed, and no money is shown as committed or lent.
  • IREN's subsidiary borrowed about $1.5 billion in a term loan plus $2.1 billion in 5.96% notes due 2031, backed by GPUs and a Microsoft GPU-services contract at Childress, Texas. IREN gives only a limited parent guarantee.
  • CoreWeave's March 2026 DDTL 4.0 facility is $8.5 billion, with only $1.26 billion drawn at March 31, 2026. It is nonrecourse: lenders, not the parent, take losses beyond the GPUs, contract and cash. One customer made up 67% of CoreWeave's 2025 revenue.
  • At Stargate Abilene, the property company had a $2.288 billion construction loan, paid by Oracle's 15-year lease. That loan covers buildings and power, not the GPUs.
  • Brookfield and Bloom raised their AI power framework to $25 billion, but only $2 billion was committed capex. The Brookfield-NAVER deal is still a nonbinding term sheet for up to $9 billion.

Why it matters

It shows private capital, not the state, building much of the new compute base through ordinary finance tools like SPVs, leases and secured loans. Where the loss finally lands is unsettled, which matters if public backstops are later asked to step in.

What to watch

  • pending · Signed final agreements under the NVIDIA financing platforms, with named borrowers, collateral and loss order.
  • pending · A definitive Brookfield-NAVER agreement showing who owns the GPUs.
  • by June 30, 2027 · How much of CoreWeave's $8.5 billion DDTL 4.0 commitment is drawn before the draw period ends.

What's really inside the 'trillions coming in' investment claims

The administration's $18–20 trillion investment total is not a pile of construction money. It mixes multi-year company spending plans, foreign-government frameworks, trade and purchase figures and some real projects, and each step from announcement to operation is separate.

What we found

  • The official check is BEA's 2025 new foreign direct investment release: $232.2 billion actually spent to buy, start or expand U.S. businesses, of which $218.4 billion was acquisitions and only $13.8 billion new building or expansion. Planned future spending tied to those deals was $284.5 billion.
  • The study audits the $18T–$20T administration total claim by claim, with 28 dated claim families (13 flagged for source mismatch or category risk) and a 21-row project ledger across chips, computing, energy, grid, critical minerals and defense.
  • It traces six projects from legal borrower through financing and draws to construction, to see who supplies the cash and who carries the credit risk.
  • NVIDIA's six-platform AI-infrastructure announcement is still only a memorandum of understanding.
  • A loan guarantee, tax credit, purchase contract or land lease is not construction cash, and no token or stablecoin counts as funding without a named issuer, instrument, cash source and borrower receipt.

Why it matters

It tests a headline the thesis leans on: the build-out may be real, but it runs through ordinary project finance, guarantees and company budgets, and the political total runs well ahead of money actually spent.

What to watch

  • pending · Signed financing documents or loan draws for named projects, which would move a claim from announced to funded.
  • pending · Any NVIDIA platform moving past the MOU stage to a signed, disclosed financing.

No proof yet that tokens fund real industrial projects

In six real project-finance deals checked, none showed crypto, stablecoins, tokenized debt, tokenized collateral or deposit tokens as a funding source. Wholesale token systems exist next to these projects, but nothing ties them in yet.

What we found

  • Live wholesale systems exist. Broadridge reported $384 billion in average daily repo volume on its blockchain repo platform in December 2025, and J.P. Morgan's Kinexys runs a live intraday repo service. Neither was tied to a named industrial borrower.
  • Other efforts are tests or pilots: the JPMC–Chainlink–Ondo test, the DTCC collateral demo, BIS Project Agorá, and SWIFT's shared ledger, which was getting ready for a live bank pilot in July 2026. On SWIFT, final settlement still runs through existing systems.
  • These systems can cut friction and move collateral faster, but they do not create a lender, an investor or new construction money.
  • To count a token as funding, the study wants the full paper trail: the signed loan or bond, the investor's cash, the borrower's first draw, final settlement and the loss rules. A marketing claim or a blockchain record is not enough.
  • At the July 28, 2026 cutoff of the related GENIUS research, the stablecoin law was enacted but its core rules were still proposed and had not taken general effect.

Why it matters

Banks and market utilities are building the new rails inside regulated wholesale markets first. Whether that reaches the actual build-out of factories and power plants is still unproven, and the study treats it as a test, not a given.

What to watch

  • pending · A named project, borrower or lender showing a token-based draw or settlement with signed documents behind it.
  • pending · GENIUS Act core rules moving from proposed to final, and the law taking general effect.
  • pending · SWIFT's shared ledger moving from pilot preparation to live use by participating banks.

U.S. trade agency pays for early project studies abroad, before any deals

In one week, September 21-25, 2026, the U.S. Trade and Development Agency announced seven projects that pay for early planning work, like studies, legal papers, tender packages and financial models, that could shape later decisions abroad. None has yet led to financing, a contract award, construction or operation.

What we found

  • The seven announcements cover the Marshall Islands, Vietnam, Ecuador, Nigeria, the Lobito corridor, Türkiye and the Buenassa refinery in the DRC.
  • Marshall Islands, Vietnam and Ecuador name the selected U.S. contractor. Nigeria and Lobito have active requests for proposals with detailed task lists.
  • Türkiye and the DRC have signed USTDA funding agreements but no selected contractor in the reviewed record.
  • The funded work includes regulatory roadmaps, legal documents, tender packages, environmental assessments, financial models, procurement criteria, technical designs and demand forecasts. No final study is public for any of the seven.

Why it matters

Paying for the studies, specs and tender papers lets a small U.S. agency shape how foreign projects are designed before money and contracts are decided. It is groundwork, not proof of deals.

What to watch

  • pending · Contractor selections for the Nigeria, Lobito, Türkiye and DRC projects.
  • pending · Final studies made public, then any financing close, equipment award or construction notice.
