Reading the Charter — Federal Financing Bank

The FFB is the bank inside the bank — a body corporate Congress built in 1973, stuffed inside the Treasury, that exists for one job: buying up the debt of every other federal agency and public body off the open market, at Treasury's own borrowing rate, with the transactions swept entirely off the federal budget. It has no branches, no depositors, no public face. Its whole purpose is to let federal credit programs borrow cheap and invisibly. Read below section by section.

1. Immunities & liability shields

§2289(1) — the Bank has ordinary corporate power "to sue and be sued, complain, and defend, in its corporate name." No special jurisdiction-shifting language (contrast Freddie Mac's "deemed to be an agency" federal-removal clause) — the FFB doesn't need it; as "an instrumentality of the United States Government" (§2283) it already sits inside the sovereign's own legal apparatus rather than needing litigation armor bolted on. §2289(4) lets it "conduct its business... and exercise the powers granted by this chapter in any State without regard to any qualification or similar statute in any State" — it never has to register or qualify to do business state by state, the same "operate above state law" pattern seen across these federal financial instrumentalities.

2. Tax exemptions

§2290(a) is a clean, complete exemption: the Bank, "its property, its franchise, capital, reserves, surplus, security holdings, and other funds, and its income shall be exempt from all taxation now or hereafter imposed by the United States or by any State or local taxing authority" — federal AND state AND local, all at once, in one sentence. Two carve-outs only: (1) real/tangible personal property is taxed locally like anyone else's, and (2) FFB's own obligations are subject to federal taxation the same as private corporate bonds are. §2290(b) makes every FFB obligation an "exempted security" under the '33 Act, Trust Indenture Act, and '34 Act securities-registration provisions — market-plumbing treatment identical to a Treasury bond, without FFB ever needing to disclaim government backing on its paper the way Freddie Mac's statute forces it to (there is no "not backed by the United States" disclaimer requirement anywhere in this chapter — because unlike Freddie, the FFB actually IS Treasury money, moving through a corporate shell).

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — the control map

§2284(a) — five-person Board: the Secretary of the Treasury sits as Chairman BY STATUTE (not by appointment — the office itself is wired into the Board), and the President appoints the other four "from among the officers or employees of the Bank or of any Federal agency." There is no independent outside board, no removal-for-cause language, no fixed term of years for the four presidential appointees — because they're required to already be federal officers/employees, they serve at whatever pleasure already governs their underlying federal job. §2283 reinforces this: "The Bank shall be subject to the general supervision and direction of the Secretary of the Treasury" — no Dodd-Frank-style carve-out limiting that supervision (contrast the OCC's §1(b)(1), which bars Treasury from touching rules/enforcement). Here, Treasury's grip is total and undiluted: the Secretary chairs the board that sets the Bank's general policies (§2284(b)), AND separately controls the Bank's financing-plan-approval gate government-wide (§2286), AND is the counterparty who can require or refuse to buy the Bank's own paper (§2288(b)-(c)). This is about as un-independent as a federal financial instrumentality gets — by design; it exists to be an arm of Treasury debt management, not a check on it.

4. Money — self-funding vs. appropriations, off-budget status

This is the loadbearing section of the whole charter.

  • §2287 — Treasury seeded it with up to $100,000,000 in initial capital, appropriated once, "available... without fiscal year limitation."
  • §2288(a) — the Bank can issue up to $15 billion in its own publicly-marketed obligations (or more if Congress authorizes it in appropriations acts) — but in practice the FFB almost never borrows publicly; it borrows almost entirely from Treasury itself.
  • §2288(b)-(c) — the real mechanism. The Bank can issue obligations directly to the Secretary of the Treasury, and the Secretary "may in his discretion purchase" them, funded out of ordinary Treasury public-debt-transaction authority (the same authority Treasury uses to issue T-bonds to the public) — meaning FFB effectively borrows straight from the federal government's own general credit, at Treasury's own rate, with no separate market transaction, no separate credit assessment, no separate investor base at all. And then §2288(c) hands FFB something no ordinary borrower ever gets: the Bank "may REQUIRE the Secretary of the Treasury to purchase" its obligations, up to $5 billion outstanding at any time. Not "the Secretary may agree" — the Bank can compel the purchase. A body corporate holding a statutory PUT option against the U.S. Treasury, up to $5 billion, on demand.
  • §2290(c) — off-budget, twice over. "The receipts and disbursements of the Bank in the discharge of its functions shall not be included in the totals of the budget of the United States Government and shall be exempt from any general limitation imposed by statute on expenditures and net lending (budget outlays) of the United States." Every dollar the FFB lends to every federal credit program it touches — rural electrification, HUD-guaranteed housing, foreign military sales financing, and dozens of other agency programs over the decades — moves through federal hands without ever showing up in the federal budget total or counting against any statutory spending cap. This is the FFB's entire reason for existing: Congress in 1973 was worried that scattered agency borrowing was flooding the market and driving up Treasury's own borrowing costs (§2281's findings), so it built a single off-budget conduit that lets every agency borrow at the government's own rate without the borrowing ever registering as government borrowing in the numbers anyone votes on.
  • §2286 — separately, ALL federal agencies (not just those using the FFB) need Treasury's prior approval for the method, source, timing, and terms of any of their own obligation issuances — with hard deadlines (60 days default, 120-day absolute cap) forcing Treasury to act. This is the coordination mechanism the findings clause describes: nobody in the federal borrowing universe moves without checking with Treasury first, and the FFB is the vehicle that lets them borrow FROM Treasury instead of the open market once approved.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

§2293 applies the Government Corporation Control Act's budget-and-audit regime (now recodified at 31 U.S.C. ch. 91) to the FFB "in the same manner as" wholly-owned federal government corporations — meaning FFB does get GAO-style audit coverage as a government corporation, unlike a shielded GSE. §2292 requires only a bare annual report to the President and Congress "as soon as practicable" — no statutory content requirements, no public-disclosure mandate, no FOIA reference anywhere in the chapter. Note the oddity flagged in the verbatim file: the Act's own internal section numbering jumps from §13 (§2292) to §15 (§2293) with no §14 ever appearing in the current U.S. Code — a gap in the codification with no explanatory repeal note found in the sections retrieved this session (flagged as a research gap, not confirmed content).

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

§2283 — "There is hereby created a body corporate... which shall have succession until dissolved by an Act of Congress." Same perpetuity-killable-only-by-Congress formula seen across this whole family of federal instrumentalities (Freddie Mac, Fannie Mae, etc.) — no self-liquidation trigger, no regulator-initiated wind-down built into this chapter. §2281's statutory notes carry a separability clause (Act §19): if any provision is struck down, the rest survives.

7. Emergency powers

No "unusual and exigent circumstances" emergency trigger exists in this chapter — the FFB's whole design IS the emergency-avoidance mechanism: it exists so federal borrowing demand never has to hit a crisis point in the open market in the first place. The closest thing to an emergency lever is the ordinary discretionary language throughout (Secretary "may in his discretion purchase," §2288(b)) — broad standing authority, not a special-circumstances switch.

8. Definitions section — scope traps

§2282 is short but does real work:

  • "Federal agency" (1) is defined broadly: "an executive department, an independent Federal establishment, or a corporation or other entity established by the Congress which is owned in whole or in part by the United States." That last clause — "owned in WHOLE OR IN PART" — means the FFB's customer base isn't limited to pure federal departments; any congressionally-chartered entity with even partial federal ownership qualifies, which is how GSE-adjacent and mixed-ownership bodies get pulled into FFB's orbit over the decades.
  • "Obligation" (2) excludes Federal Reserve notes and ownership-interest stock — i.e., FFB deals in debt instruments, not currency or equity.
  • "Guarantee" (3) explicitly excludes deposit insurance — carving FDIC/NCUA-style insurance out of FFB's functional reach even though the definition would otherwise be broad enough to catch it.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

Covered under #6 — perpetual existence per §2283, dissolvable only "by an Act of Congress." No wind-down mechanism, no sunset, no shareholder analog (there are no shareholders — it's wholly a government instrumentality with Treasury-advanced capital, not privately capitalized).

10. Anything strange

  • The compelled-purchase clause is the strangest item in the whole file. §2288(c) gives a federal corporation the statutory power to FORCE the U.S. Treasury to buy up to $5 billion of its own debt, on the corporation's own say-so, no separate congressional or presidential sign-off required for that specific $5 billion. Nothing else in this batch of charters gives an entity a compellable claim against the sovereign's own purchasing discretion in that direction — everywhere else (Freddie Mac's $2.25B Treasury line, for instance) the Treasury purchase is discretionary ("may purchase"), never mandatory from the entity's side.
  • The double off-budget trick. §2290(c) doesn't just exempt FFB's own receipts/disbursements from the federal budget totals — it's the mechanism that lets DOZENS of other federal credit programs borrow via FFB specifically so THEIR borrowing also never shows up as agency borrowing in the ordinary sense; it shows up (if at all) as an FFB balance sheet item that itself sits outside the budget totals. It's off-budget squared: the vehicle is off-budget, and everything routed through the vehicle inherits that off-budget status.
  • A congressional finding admitting the problem it's designed to hide. §2281 states as a "finding" that federal and federally-assisted borrowing was growing "faster than the total supply of credit" and wasn't "adequately coordinated" — Congress diagnosing, in the statute's own text, that federal credit expansion was outrunning the private market's ability to absorb it, and then building a mechanism (route it all through one Treasury-controlled conduit, off the books) rather than a mechanism that would actually slow the borrowing down. The "fix" for too much federal borrowing was to make the borrowing less visible, not less large.

Layer map — how we got here

  1. 1973 — Federal Financing Bank Act (Pub. L. 93-224). Full creation in one act: body corporate, Treasury-chaired board, compelled-purchase authority up to $5B, $15B public-issuance ceiling, full tax exemption, off-budget status, GAO-style audit coverage. The core architecture has not been substantively rewritten since.
  2. 1978 — New York City Loan Guarantee Act (Pub. L. 95-339) adds §2285a, a narrow carve-out barring FFB from acquiring obligations guaranteed under that Act's Title I — a one-off restriction bolted onto the FFB's chapter by an unrelated crisis-response statute (the mid-1970s NYC fiscal crisis), not an amendment to the FFB Act itself.
  3. 1981 — Housing and Community Development Amendments / Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (Pub. L. 97-35 §329E) adds §2294a, letting HUD contract for periodic payments to FFB to offset the cost of buying local public housing agency obligations, capped at $400 million aggregate — again, a program-specific rider, not a rewrite of the FFB's own charter.
  4. 1982 — recodification of Title 31 (Pub. L. 97-258) swaps old cross-references (Second Liberty Bond Act → chapter 31 of title 31; Government Corporation Control Act → chapter 91 of title 31) — purely technical, no substantive change.
  5. 2026 (today) — essentially unchanged core. Unlike the FHLBank System, Fannie/Freddie, or the OCC, the FFB's foundational architecture from 1973 has never been the subject of a HERA-style, Dodd-Frank-style, or FIRREA-style overhaul. It is one of the quietest, least-amended, most structurally stable pieces of federal financial architecture in this whole library — because it was built right the first time for exactly one narrow, powerful purpose: off-budget, Treasury-rate financing for the rest of the federal credit system.

Entity Theory read

The FFB isn't a bank in any sense a depositor would recognize — it's a pipe. Treasury built it in 1973 specifically so every other federal lending program (rural power, foreign military sales, HUD housing, and dozens more before and since) could borrow at the government's own rock-bottom rate without that borrowing ever counting as government borrowing in the numbers Congress votes on. The leash here isn't hidden or contested the way it is at the OCC or Freddie Mac — Treasury chairs the board, Treasury sets the yield floor, Treasury approves every agency's financing plan before FFB ever touches it. The only genuinely strange power in the whole file is the reverse leash: FFB can compel Treasury to buy up to $5 billion of its own paper on demand, no questions asked. Change of control doesn't apply here in the usual sense — there are no private shareholders to flip, no President to fire a board that's really just Treasury wearing a corporate hat. As long as Treasury exists, the FFB is Treasury, one layer down, off the books.
