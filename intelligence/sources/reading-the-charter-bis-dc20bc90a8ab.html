Reading the Charter — Bank for International Settlements

Sources captured in full, verbatim, in this folder: - 1930 - Convention Respecting the Bank for International Settlements (verbatim).md - 1930 - Constituent Charter of the Bank for International Settlements (verbatim).md - 1930 - Statutes of the Bank for International Settlements (as amended 2016) (verbatim).md - 1936 - Protocol Regarding the Immunities of the Bank for International Settlements (Brussels Protocol) (verbatim).md

1. Immunities & liability shields

This is the single most extreme immunity stack in the whole Charter Library.

  • Constituent Charter, ¶10: the Bank, its property, its assets, and ALL deposits and funds entrusted to it are immune in time of peace and in time of war from expropriation, requisition, seizure, confiscation, and gold/currency export or import restrictions "and any other similar measures." War doesn't touch it. Nothing in the rest of the Library says "in time of war" this bluntly.
  • Statutes Art. 55(1): the Bank has immunity from jurisdiction — full stop — except (a) where the Chairman, General Manager, or Deputy General Manager personally waives it case-by-case, or (b) civil/commercial suits by contractual counterparties arising from banking transactions (and even those get routed to arbitration if arbitration was agreed). The Bank writes its own waiver.
  • Statutes Art. 55(2)–(3): property and assets of the Bank, "wherever located and by whomsoever held," are immune from seizure, attachment, freeze, or any execution measure — unless a final judgment was won under the narrow carve-outs above. Same blanket immunity extends to every deposit entrusted to the Bank, every claim against it, and every share it issues, again "wherever located and by whomsoever held," and again requiring the Bank's own express prior agreement to touch them.
  • Brussels Protocol (1936) Art. 1: extends the Constituent Charter's asset immunity to third-party property merely held "in the name or for the account of" the BIS — so money doesn't have to be the Bank's own to be shielded; it just has to flow through BIS custody.
  • Disputes about the Bank's own Charter or Statutes don't go to ordinary courts at all — they go to the Hague Agreement's own Arbitral Tribunal (Constituent Charter ¶11; Statutes Art. 54), a private forum the Bank helped create.

2. Tax exemptions

Constituent Charter ¶6–9 is a full-spectrum exemption: stamp/registration duties on incorporation and share issuance, all taxes on capital/reserves/profits (distributed or not), taxes on the German-annuity mobilisation loans, and taxes on salaries paid to non-Swiss staff — plus a catch-all (¶9) barring any tax beyond what ordinary Basle banks pay "de facto and de jure." ¶7 separately immunizes government deposits placed with the Bank under the 1930 Hague Plan from taxation. This is a Swiss-law exemption, not a US one — the US never designated BIS for IOIA tax/immunity treatment (see §7 below).

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause

Control sits with central-bank governors, not elected officials, by explicit design:

  • Statutes Art. 27: the Board is six ex-officio seats (the Governors of the day at the central banks of Belgium, France, Germany, Great Britain, Italy, and the USA — note: a seat reserved for the US central bank concept even though the Federal Reserve doesn't hold BIS shares the way other central banks do), plus one jointly-appointed director, plus up to 11 elected directors.
  • Statutes Art. 30 is the "anything strange" clause for this category: no sitting government official can be a Director unless he is himself a central bank Governor, and no sitting legislator can be a Director unless he is a Governor or former Governor. The Statutes affirmatively wall out politicians. Central bank governors govern the BIS; elected officials are structurally excluded except through the narrow central-bank-governor door.
  • No removal-for-cause provision exists in the Statutes at all — directorships simply expire on their term (3 years, Art. 27) or through the normal vacancy-fill process (Art. 28). There is no mechanism in the Statutes for a government to remove its own Governor from the BIS Board; that would have to happen at the national central-bank level, once removed.

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations

The Bank runs entirely on its own balance sheet — banking operations (Statutes Art. 21: gold trading, discounting, deposits, agency work for central banks) fund it, and profits are swept into Legal Reserve, General Reserve, and Special Dividend Reserve funds by a fixed cascading formula (Art. 51–52). No government appropriates BIS's budget; no Congress or parliament touches its purse. It is capitalized in Special Drawing Rights (Art. 4), not any single nation's currency — an early "denationalized" balance sheet.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

Statutes Art. 50: audited by independent auditors who report to the Board and the General Meeting — not to any government regulator, not to a Congress, not to a parliament. There is no GAO/FOIA-equivalent exposure anywhere in the Statutes; disclosure is exactly what the Bank chooses to publish in its Annual Report and monthly Statement of Account (Art. 49).

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

  • Ordinary Statutes amendments: Board proposes by 2/3 majority, General Meeting adopts by simple majority (Art. 57).
  • The "entrenched" Articles — 2, 3, 8, 14, 19, 24, 27, 44, 51, 54, 57, and 58 itself — need Board 2/3 + General Meeting majority plus a law supplementing the Charter (Art. 58; also Constituent Charter ¶4). Switzerland bound itself in the 1930 Convention (Art. 1) never to unilaterally abrogate or amend the Charter, and never to sanction Statute amendments except in agreement with the other original signatory governments (Germany, Belgium, France, UK, Italy, Japan). So Switzerland hosts the Bank but does not unilaterally control its founding law — a multilateral lock installed at birth.
  • Liquidation requires a 3/4 General Meeting majority (Statutes Art. 53(1)) — deliberately harder to kill than to amend.

7. Emergency powers

Nothing framed as "emergency powers" in the classic Fed §13(3) sense — but note the war-carveout embedded permanently in the peacetime immunity clause (Constituent Charter ¶10, above): the Bank's wartime footing is not an emergency exception, it is baked into ordinary law from day one. There's no "unusual and exigent circumstances" trigger anywhere because the immunity is unconditional in the first place.

8. The definitions section

Statutes Art. 56 quietly does real work: "central bank" is defined to include, in a cross-border system, "the national central banks and the common central banking institution" (i.e., built to accommodate something like the ECB before the ECB existed) — showing the Statutes were amended (2003 tranche, per the amendment list) to anticipate monetary unions holding a BIS seat as a single "country." "Country" itself (Art. 56(d)) is defined as "a sovereign state, a monetary zone within a sovereign state, or a monetary zone extending over more than one sovereign state" — meaning "country" in BIS law does not mean country in the ordinary sense at all; it means whatever currency zone the Board recognizes.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

No fixed lifespan. The Convention (Art. 3) was originally 15 years but was prolonged "for the existence of the Bank" on 10 June 1930 — i.e., made permanent, tied to the Bank's own survival rather than a calendar date. Liquidation needs a 3/4 General Meeting vote (Statutes Art. 53(1)); on liquidation, staff pension obligations get first priority over all other liabilities (Art. 53(2)) — pensioners outrank creditors — and remaining reserve funds are divided among shareholders (Art. 52).

10. Anything strange — the one clause you wouldn't expect

The United States has never given the BIS the immunity treatment it demanded. The Fed, State, and Treasury jointly pushed H.R. 3269 (109th Congress, introduced 13 July 2005) to designate BIS as an "international organization" under the International Organizations Immunities Act (IOIA) — the same US-law status IMF and World Bank enjoy. The bill passed the House and died in the Senate. No Presidential Executive Order has ever designated BIS under the IOIA. So inside the United States, on US soil, the BIS carries none of the Swiss-law/Brussels-Protocol immunity described above — it sits there as an ordinary foreign entity, unlike its Bretton Woods siblings. The world's most immunity-armored bank has a hole in its armor exactly where Washington is standing, by Washington's own choice (or Senate inertia).

  6. Statute amendments 1937–2016 (13 tranches) — recapitalization in SDRs (2003 era), private-shareholder buyout (2001, Art. 18(A)), Board composition tweaks, cross-border central bank language (Art. 56) to fit currency unions.
  7. US side: H.R. 3269 (2005) — attempted, failed IOIA designation. No US EO exists. This is the one thread that never landed.

Entity Theory read

The BIS is the central bankers' own private club, built by central banks, for central banks, answerable to no elected government — a corporation that happens to have the power to move the plumbing of the entire international monetary system. It is chartered by Switzerland but not controlled by Switzerland: Switzerland signed away its own right to touch the Charter without the other founding powers' sign-off, and it locked politicians out of the boardroom by Statute (Art. 30) rather than by custom. It survives any change of national government because no national government sits on its Board — only the office of central-bank Governor does, and Governors rotate independent of elections. The leash, such as it is, is held jointly by the club of member central banks amending their own club rules by supermajority — not by any single sovereign. The one place this creature is NOT armored is the United States, where its own advocates (the Fed, State, Treasury) tried and failed to get it Congressional-strength immunity — meaning on American soil, at least on paper, BIS assets are exposed in a way they are nowhere else in the world.
