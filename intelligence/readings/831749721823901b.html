What the “Petrodollar” Really Is

Executive ruling
The “petrodollar” is not a currency issued by a petroleum institution. It is
not a named payment network, a Treasury account through which every oil sale
passes, or a public treaty that legally requires every producer to sell every
barrel in dollars. It is a useful but dangerously compressed label for a
self-reinforcing system with several separable parts:
major oil benchmarks are quoted in U.S. dollars;
many physical oil contracts therefore set price, invoice and payment in
dollars;
banks fund, hedge and settle those obligations through dollar markets and
correspondent accounts;
exporters receive foreign exchange and decide how to absorb or invest it;
central banks, sovereign funds and private institutions hold some resulting
assets in dollars, including but not limited to U.S. Treasuries;
dollar exchange-rate anchors and reserve policies reinforce the system;
deep U.S. capital markets, global dollar liquidity and U.S.–Gulf strategic
relations make the arrangement durable.
The system is therefore an architecture, not a switch. One non-dollar cargo
can be real and material without ending it. A dollar-priced cargo can occur
without creating a net Treasury purchase. The right research question is not
“does the petrodollar still exist?” but “which layer moved, for which cargo,
through which institution, on which date, and where did the proceeds finally
go?”

after those layers are reopened.
2. The historical record—and the 50-year myth
2.1 Dollar oil pricing did not begin in June 1974
The dollar's rise belongs first to the wider postwar monetary and trading
system. Bretton Woods, the scale of the U.S. economy, the growth of dollar
banking and the depth of U.S. markets predated the U.S.–Saudi Joint Commission.
The official historical record also shows dollar oil terms before the
commission. A 1945 U.S. memorandum described Aramco's option to pay Saudi oil
royalties in sterling or dollars. A 1972 Geneva agreement described posted oil
prices in dollars, royalties payable in dollars or their equivalent, and an
exchange-parity adjustment for dollar devaluation. The monetary unit was
already embedded in oil contracts and the wider system; the commission did not
invent dollar denomination from nothing.
The February 1945 Roosevelt–Ibn Saud meeting aboard the Quincy matters to the
history of the relationship, but the official meeting record reviewed here is
not an oil-for-dollar covenant. It covers Palestine and regional and
agricultural matters. The familiar “oil for security” phrase is therefore a
later strategic summary, not the text of a petrodollar treaty in that record.
2.2 What happened in 1974–75
The United States and Saudi Arabia established the Joint Commission on
Economic Cooperation by joint statement on June 8, 1974, after the embargo and
oil-price shock. Official planning and later GAO review describe a broad
program: Saudi industrialization and development, trade, technical assistance,
science and technology, agriculture, investment and reserve-management
cooperation, closer political ties, and channels through which Saudi oil
surpluses could return to the world economy.
GAO used the phrase “recycling petrodollars,” but its description is not an
exclusive oil-sales clause. It reports that a February 13, 1975 Technical
Cooperation Agreement funded U.S. services through a Saudi dollar trust
account at the U.S. Treasury. That agreement had a five-year term, subject to
revision or extension, and could be terminated on notice. A project-funding
account is not a universal oil-payment account.
Contemporaneous U.S. records do show active efforts to attract Saudi reserves
into U.S. assets. Treasury created a noncompetitive “add-on” facility through
which foreign official institutions could buy Treasury securities; Saudi
Arabia was the first user before access broadened. GAO also reported special
confidentiality treatment around some OPEC holdings. By 1976, an official U.S.
memorandum said more than $8 billion of Saudi placements had been attracted to
U.S. government securities.
That is the real historical core: strategic cooperation plus deliberate
investment and recycling channels. It is substantial. It still does not turn
the public record into a 50-year promise that expired in June 2024.
2.3 The correct claim disposition
The viral claim that “the 50-year U.S.–Saudi petrodollar agreement expired” is
unsupported by the controlling public objects located in this review. The
public Joint Commission record does not contain that exclusive obligation, and
the dated technical agreement described by GAO was five years, not fifty.
This is an evidence ruling, not a claim that every diplomatic understanding was
public. Confidential investment or reporting arrangements may have existed.
Anyone asserting a controlling 50-year oil-pricing covenant should produce the
instrument, parties, operative currency clause, commencement provision,
duration, amendment history and termination notice. A repeated anniversary
calculation is not a legal object.
3. How a dollar oil sale actually works
Consider a simplified physical cargo sold by a Gulf producer to an Asian
refiner:
benchmark assessment/future in USD
↓
physical formula: benchmark ± grade/location differential
↓
seller invoices buyer in USD
↓
buyer funds USD directly or converts local currency through FX market
↓
buyer bank sends payment instruction; correspondent balances move
↓
seller, seller bank or controlled sovereign account receives a USD claim
↓
proceeds are spent, converted, retained, invested or transferred
This walk contains at least four different contracts: the physical sale, any
trade-finance facility, the FX trade, and the bank-payment relationship. It can
also contain futures or swaps, insurance, freight and letter-of-credit
obligations. Those instruments need not all use the same currency.
SWIFT may carry messages between banks, but the message is not the money. Final
discharge occurs by changes to bank and correspondent balances under the
relevant legal and settlement arrangements. A sanctions license may permit a
payment lane without making the payment occur. A blocked or controlled account
may receive funds without giving the producer unrestricted use.
4. Recycling: where the money goes next
The IMF defines petrodollar recycling as the reflow to the rest of the world
from the use oil exporters make of oil receipts. It identifies two main paths.
4.1 Absorption channel
The exporter uses receipts to import goods and services: equipment, food,
construction, technology, defense articles, travel, professional services or
other consumption and investment. Dollars may pay foreign suppliers directly
or be converted first. Economically, this is still recycling because the
foreign exchange returns to non-oil exporters.
4.2 Capital-account channel
Receipts not absorbed through imports may be saved abroad through:
central-bank reserves;
sovereign wealth funds;
bank deposits;
U.S. Treasuries or other government bonds;
corporate bonds and equities;
real estate and direct investment;
loans through private banks;
lending to international institutions such as the IMF.
In the 1970s, commercial banks also intermediated oil surpluses into loans to
oil-importing and emerging economies. This is another reason “recycling” cannot
be reduced to Treasury purchases.
4.3 Gross receipts are not net investable surplus
The amount available for foreign-asset accumulation is not gross oil exports.
A useful identity is:
gross oil export receipts
− imported goods and services
− income and transfer outflows
− domestic/fiscal uses requiring conversion
± non-oil trade and income
± external borrowing and liability issuance
= net external financing position
Saudi Arabia supplies a current example. The IMF reported that its current
account moved from a 2.9 percent-of-GDP surplus in 2023 to a 0.5 percent deficit
in 2024 as lower oil proceeds, higher investment-related imports and remittance
outflows outweighed other receipts. The deficit was financed through external
borrowing and reduced foreign-asset accumulation. SAMA still held about $415
billion of net foreign assets at end-2024, but the flow arithmetic had changed.
Thus:
an oil cargo ≠ an external surplus
an external surplus ≠ a dollar allocation
a dollar allocation ≠ a Treasury purchase
a Treasury holding ≠ a current-period net purchase
5. Why the dollar remains central
Oil reinforces dollar use, but the current dollar system is much larger than
oil. Its resilience comes from a network:
liquid dollar FX markets and low conversion costs;
deep markets for Treasury and private dollar assets;
global banks that fund and lend in dollars;
dollar-denominated trade credit and debt;
benchmark, derivative and collateral conventions;
legal and institutional confidence;
central-bank dollar liquidity backstops;
incumbent invoicing choices: firms prefer the currency suppliers, customers,
banks and hedging markets already use.
The Federal Reserve's 2025 review estimated the dollar at 58 percent of
disclosed official reserves in 2024 and found it dominant in trade invoicing
outside Europe. The IMF's 2026Q1 COFER release put the dollar share at 57.13
percent, while warning that valuation effects accounted for about half the
quarterly increase. The BIS reported that the dollar was on one side of 89.2
percent of April 2025 FX trades; because each FX trade has two currencies,
currency shares sum to 200 percent.
These are different measures. None is “the petrodollar share.” Together they
show why a buyer can leave the dollar for one invoice yet still touch it while
funding, hedging, converting or investing.
Oil benchmarks remain an important junction. ICE Brent futures are specified
in U.S. dollars and cents per barrel. Aramco's 2025 annual report describes the
U.S. dollar as the currency in which crude oil is priced globally. These facts
establish benchmark and company accounting practice, not the private terms of
every cargo.
6. Saudi Arabia's dollar links are separate objects
Saudi Arabia's riyal has been pegged at 3.75 per dollar since 1986. The IMF
calls the peg a credible policy anchor; with an open capital account it links
Saudi monetary conditions to the Federal Reserve. But a domestic exchange-rate
regime is not an oil-sale treaty.
Likewise, Treasury's country table reported $142.5 billion of Saudi holdings of
Treasury securities at June 2026. That is a stock attributed through the TIC
reporting system, not a measure of Saudi oil receipts in that month or proof of
their source. Treasury warns that country attribution can reflect the location
of custodians and broker-dealers rather than the ultimate owner.
SAMA reserves, PIF assets, Aramco cash, government accounts, commercial banks
and private Saudi investors are also different balance sheets. “Saudi Arabia
bought” is too coarse unless the legal holder is identified.

7.2 Current alternative infrastructure
There is real diversification:
The Shanghai International Energy Exchange lists a deliverable medium-sour
crude futures contract quoted in RMB per barrel. This creates RMB price
discovery and hedging capacity.
SAMA and the People's Bank of China signed a three-year bilateral swap in
November 2023 with a maximum size of RMB 50 billion. This establishes
liquidity capacity, not evidence that it was drawn or used for an oil cargo.
Saudi Arabia joined Project mBridge as a full participant when the BIS
announced the platform's minimum viable product stage in 2024. Membership
and real-value capability are not a public production record for Saudi oil.
These changes matter because they lower the cost of bypassing a dollar leg and
create optionality under sanctions or geopolitical stress. They do not, by
themselves, show the contract, invoice, final receipt or retained reserve asset.
7.3 What would constitute structural erosion
A single symbolic sale is weak evidence. Stronger evidence would combine:
recurring material oil volumes invoiced outside dollars;
liquid non-dollar benchmarks and hedging across the curve;
trade credit and bank funding in the same currency;
reliable cross-border settlement with acceptable legal finality;
exporter willingness to retain the proceeds;
deep, safe and liquid assets in which to invest them;
reserve managers reducing dollar assets after adjusting for valuation;
suppliers and third-country traders adopting the new currency without state
compulsion;
performance through a crisis, including liquidity and convertibility;
lower dollar use across several functions, not just the invoice.
Dollar erosion can therefore be asymmetric. The reserve share can decline while
oil benchmarks remain dollar-based. A payment rail can diversify while dollar
funding remains dominant. A producer can accept RMB and immediately convert it
to dollars. Each is a different state.
8. Sanctions, Russia, Iran and Venezuela
Sanctions make the layer separation operationally essential.
Russia and Iran have strong incentives to use non-dollar invoices, non-U.S.
banks, barter, local-currency accounts, intermediaries, or alternative
messaging and settlement systems. These can reduce U.S. jurisdictional touch
points. Yet a trade can avoid dollar settlement while retaining dollar-linked
price formation, and intermediaries may still use dollar FX or stablecoins at
another stage. Avoidance is not the same as monetary replacement.
Venezuela illustrates a different architecture. A U.S. authorization can
permit sale or payment, and proceeds may enter a specified or controlled
account, without establishing unrestricted Venezuelan receipt. Physical
lifting, legal permission, invoice, settlement and beneficiary use must be
mapped separately.
If Russia were unsanctioned, the first-order change would be lower legal,
shipping, insurance and payment friction and some rerouting toward conventional
channels. It would not automatically convert every non-dollar contract back to
dollars or create new oil production. Currency practice would depend on
contract terms, banking access, reserve preferences and politics.
9. What this means for the shipment globe
The globe should not draw one generic “petrodollar” line. Each observable cargo
should be capable of carrying these monetary fields:

confidence + claim_limit + next_receipt
National reserve holdings should be a separate balance-sheet layer, joined to
cargoes only when an authoritative transaction key exists. Otherwise the map
would turn correlation into a false money trail.
The useful visual is a dual projection:
PHYSICAL: origin → terminal → vessel → destination
MONEY: benchmark → invoice → funding/FX → bank settlement → receipt → use
The two chains can diverge geographically. A Middle Eastern cargo delivered to
Asia may be funded by a European bank, settled through New York correspondent
accounts, and reinvested by a sovereign fund through a global custodian. The
physical route and the balance-sheet route are related but not identical.
10. Bottom line
The petrodollar is real as a historical and operating system, but misleading as
a singular agreement. Its durable core is the interaction of dollar oil
pricing, dollar banking and hedging, exporters' external surpluses, investable
dollar assets, currency anchors and geopolitical relationships.
The most important correction is this:
Oil does not mechanically create Treasury demand. It creates foreign-exchange
receipts. What happens next depends on imports, fiscal choices, bank balance
sheets, sovereign funds, reserve policy, FX conversion and available assets.
The most useful monitoring question is correspondingly narrow:
For this cargo, which currency performed each job—and where did the final
claim remain after settlement and conversion?
That is the level at which oil-trade movements can be joined to monetary change
without reproducing either the “50-year pact” myth or an equally misleading
“nothing is changing” dismissal.
