# HHS High-Risk Life Sciences Research Policy — Primary-Object Delta

## Executive call

The July 28 HHS announcements carry one new government-wide policy object:
the White House-hosted *United States Government Policy for Stopping High-Risk
Life Sciences Research*, dated July 20, 2026.

The object changes the federal research-funding control system. It:

- prohibits federal funding for dangerous gain-of-function research in the
  United States and abroad;
- prohibits federally funded life-science research involving defined countries
  or entities of concern;
- allows potential DGOF only after institutional review, a full risk-benefit
  assessment and a recommendation from a single federal independent
  third-party review body;
- permits other risky international research only after a risk-based assessment
  of the threat and the host institution's oversight capacity;
- requires continuing attestations, disclosures, monitoring, reporting and
  enforcement terms across the federal life-science funding ecosystem; and
- keeps the EO 14292 potential-DGOF suspension in place until agencies publish
  implementation guidance and the new review mechanisms exist.

This is a policy and funding-state change. It is not evidence that the review
body, institutional controls, award clauses, public reports or enforcement
system are already operating.

## 2. Scope and covered entities

### DGOF

The policy defines dangerous gain-of-function research by both consequence and
activity. Covered research uses a biological agent, seeks, achieves or has a
substantial risk of achieving one or more specified enhancements, and could
produce significant negative societal consequences. The listed enhancement
families include harmful consequences, immune or immunization evasion,
resistance to interventions or detection, stability or transmissibility,
host-range changes, increased host susceptibility and creation or
reconstitution of an eradicated or extinct agent.

**Federal funding state:** prohibited.

### Potential DGOF

Potential DGOF is research that could potentially produce one or more of the
listed outcomes and could result in significant negative societal
consequences.

**Federal funding state:** permissible only after institutional review and a
recommendation from the independent third-party review body that the project,
with its mitigation plan, does not meet the DGOF definition.

### International research of concern

The policy divides IROC into two lanes:

1. research involving a country or entity of concern; and
2. other international life-science research that could reasonably threaten
   public health, public safety, economic security or national security where
   oversight may be inadequate.

**Federal funding state:** the first lane is prohibited. The second requires a
risk-based assessment of the threat and the institution's ability to provide
adequate oversight.

### Parties in scope

The policy applies to:

- principal investigators who apply for or receive federal life-science funds;
- research institutions receiving federal life-science funds; and
- federal agencies that conduct, fund or support life-science research.

Institutions outside the federal funding ecosystem are strongly encouraged to
adopt equivalent oversight, but the policy does not impose the same direct
funding conditions on them.

## 3. Computational biology and AI boundary

Purely computational or *in silico* work is not prohibited unless it involves
an entity of concern. If the computational work produces a plan to create or
modify a biological agent that would meet the DGOF or potential-DGOF
definition, the policy applies. OSTP must convene an interagency group to
monitor advances at the intersection of biological sciences and artificial
intelligence.

This creates a monitoring and proposal-gate seam. It does not ban biology
models, software or AI research as a class.

## 4. Oversight and operating architecture

| Actor | Required control | State at cutoff |
|---|---|---|
| Principal investigator | Written attestation; key-personnel, foreign-collaborator and nonfederal-support disclosure; continuous risk review; training; mitigation-plan compliance | Policy requirement issued; project-level operation not yet observed |
| PI and institutional review entity | Initial risk-benefit analysis; full assessment and mitigation plan for potential DGOF; two-tier halt and notification duties when risk increases | Required architecture; institution-specific processes pending |
| Research institution | Designate an Institutional Contact for DGOF; establish a review entity of at least five qualified members; certify proposals; monitor compliance; report nonfederal potential-DGOF/DGOF annually | 180-day implementation clock open |
| Federal funding agency | Review all attestations; identify DGOF, potential DGOF and IROC; send relevant material to OMB and the review body; make funding decisions; preserve audit records | Agency guidance and implementation open |
| Independent third-party review body | One U.S. Government body; review potential DGOF; issue recommendations; conduct ordinary and expedited review; publish annual recommendations report; audit up to 25 percent of negative attestations annually | 90-day establishment clock open |
| Interagency leadership | OSTP/EOP coordination; OMB and APNSA oversight; HHS, State, USDA and DNI entity-of-concern list; policy review at least every four years | Coordination and publication products pending |

For funded ITPRB-reviewed work, when research or context changes to increase
potential-DGOF risk, the policy says the research **should** be halted while the
PI, institution and funding agency determine whether the mitigation plan must
change or another review is required. If the work may meet the DGOF definition,
the PI **will** halt immediately and notify the institutional and federal actors
within 24 hours.

Standard ITPRB review is expected within 90 calendar days after receipt of
review materials. Declared public-health or agricultural emergencies and
national-security needs may receive expedited review. The policy requires a
30-day expedited procedure specifically for time-sensitive national-security
objectives; it does not state the same deadline for the emergency lane.
Expedited approval does not displace other applicable law.

## 5. Replaced and preserved frameworks

The policy replaces the 2024 *United States Government Policy for Oversight of
Dual Use Research of Concern and Pathogens with Enhanced Pandemic Potential*,
including the oversight described in:

- the 2012 *United States Government Policy for Oversight of Life Sciences Dual
  Use Research of Concern*;
- the 2014 *United States Government Policy for Institutional Oversight of Life
  Sciences Dual Use Research of Concern*; and
- the 2017 *Recommended Policy Guidance for Potential Pandemic Pathogen Care
  and Oversight*.

It does not supersede or modify existing statutory and regulatory authorities,
including the Federal Select Agent Program and export-control regulations.
Replacement of the policy framework is therefore not repeal of the surrounding
law.

## 7. Enforcement state

The policy calls for life-science research award terms that make compliance
material to federal payment decisions and permit violations to be attributed
to the recipient institution. Failure to recognize and report unanticipated
DGOF may lead to revocation of ongoing federal funding, additional penalties
under existing law and up to five years of ineligibility for federal
life-science funds.

These are available or required consequences. No July 28 source establishes a
specific award termination, false-claim case, debarment or five-year
ineligibility decision under the new policy.

## 8. Private-funding and authority gap

The policy's page-one footnote is controlling on the most important limit:
additional statutory action is required to stop all DGOF research and DGOF
funding by all U.S. entities. It says an interagency working group will explore
regulatory, legislative or other actions, including privately funded research.

Federally funded institutions must monitor and report certain nonfederal DGOF
activity, and noncompliance can affect future federal eligibility. That creates
substantial reach through the funding ecosystem. It is still not a blanket
domestic prohibition on every privately funded institution or researcher.

## 9. Claim and overclaim audit

| Claim | Ruling | Safe formulation |
|---|---|---|
| “The United States banned gain-of-function research.” | Overbroad | The policy prohibits **federal support** for DGOF and uses federal-funding conditions to extend oversight and reporting. |
| “All high-risk research is prohibited.” | False | Potential DGOF and some international research may proceed after specified review and mitigation. |
| “The policy bans vaccine and countermeasure research.” | False | It expressly preserves essential medical products and countermeasures under safeguards. |
| “The private-funding gap is closed.” | False | The policy says further authority and action are required. |
| “The new review system is operational.” | Not yet established | The policy creates 90/120/180-day implementation clocks; operating evidence is still required. |
| “HHS, NIH and the joint statement are three policy actions.” | Duplicate | They are announcement, signaling and implementation-intent carriers for one controlling policy. |
| “This proves a COVID-origin claim.” | Unsupported | The policy is prospective oversight. It neither adjudicates SARS-CoV-2's origin nor validates every prior GOF allegation. |
