## The question

Who can change the IMF, by which legal route, with what threshold and effect;
what can the Fund actually change in a member country; and which parts of that
answer were already present—but scattered—across the research library?

## Short answer

The IMF is a treaty-based international organization whose powers are split
among its members, Board of Governors, Executive Board, Managing Director and
staff. It can interpret and administer its charter, make policy within that
charter, allocate resources, condition access to financing, conduct
surveillance, approve specified exchange restrictions, operate the SDR system,
and provide requested legal and technical assistance. It cannot enact a
member's budget, rewrite domestic statutes, compel creditors to exchange bonds,
set most daily exchange rates, issue a national currency, or silently enlarge
its own treaty mandate.

The strongest practical leverage often comes from a financing gate: the member
chooses whether to request and implement a program, while the Executive Board
decides whether Fund resources are made available and whether reviews or
waivers are approved. The deepest formal change requires collective member
action: an amendment normally needs acceptance by three-fifths of the members
holding 85 percent of total voting power.

## Evidence boundary

The map describes legal authority and documented institutional practice as of
September 21, 2026. Authority is not proof that it was used in a particular
case. A Board discussion is not a decision; a decision is not implementation;
a member commitment is not domestic enactment; financing availability is not a
disbursement; and technical assistance is not sovereign lawmaking.
