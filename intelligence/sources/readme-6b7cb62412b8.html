Donald J. Trump Criminal Indictments — Primary-Source Map, 2023–2026

In plain English

Question: What did the four criminal indictments against Donald J. Trump actually charge, how did the count structures differ, what later rulings changed them, and what was the procedural state of each case by October 1, 2026?

Answer: The famous 91-count total was a real peak snapshot, but only New York reached a verdict; the other three cases ended before a jury resolved the allegations.

Bottom line

The official record is materially different from the single headline story, but not in the direction of a simple vindication or a simple aggregate prosecution.

Donald J. Trump faced four separate criminal prosecutions brought by four separate sovereign offices. The widely repeated 91 felony counts was an accurate peak snapshot after the July 27, 2023 superseding classified-documents indictment:

Case Peak Trump counts What the number actually represented Final state at cutoff
New York business records 34 One offense repeated across 11 invoices, 12 ledger entries and 11 checks or check stubs in a reimbursement sequence Jury conviction on all 34; unconditional discharge; perfected appeal with no merits decision located
Federal classified documents 40 32 document-specific national-defense-information counts plus eight obstruction, concealment and false-statement counts charged against Trump Indictment dismissed pretrial on Special Counsel appointment grounds; appeal as to Trump dismissed; no merits trial
Federal 2020 election 4 Four conspiracy or obstruction theories arising from the post-election effort to affect collection, counting and certification Narrowed after presidential-immunity and obstruction rulings; dismissed without prejudice under DOJ's sitting-President rule; no merits trial
Georgia 2020 election 13 One RICO count plus 12 solicitation, false-writing, forgery or filing-conspiracy counts tied to the Georgia and multistate theory Five Trump counts quashed before trial; remaining eight nol-prossed after prosecutor reassessment; no merits trial

The same arithmetic becomes misleading when treated as 91 independent schemes, 91 proven crimes or the present count of live charges. The legal record contains one adjudicated conviction, three cases that ended before a merits verdict, five Georgia counts that were judicially quashed, two federal indictments dismissed for constitutional or institutional reasons, and no acquittal in any of the four cases.

The four cases were not one story

1. New York: 34 records, one repeated felony theory

Case: People v. Donald J. Trump, Ind. No. 71543/23, Supreme Court, New York County.

The indictment charged the same offense 34 times: Falsifying Business Records in the First Degree under New York Penal Law § 175.10. Each count corresponds to a distinct record, not a distinct payoff or a separate election plot:

  • 11 Michael Cohen invoices;
  • 12 general-ledger entries; and
  • 11 checks or check stubs.

The bare indictment said that the alleged intent to defraud included an intent to commit, aid or conceal “another crime,” but it did not identify that crime. The operative theory becomes clear in the court's jury instructions. The People alleged that the other crime was a New York Election Law § 17-152 conspiracy to promote or prevent an election by unlawful means. Jurors had to agree unanimously that such a conspiracy existed, but they did not have to agree which of three asserted unlawful means supplied it: a Federal Election Campaign Act violation, falsification of other business records, or a tax-law violation. The court also instructed that the People did not have to prove the other crime was completed; they had to prove the charged intent beyond a reasonable doubt.

That distinction corrects two opposite simplifications. This was not merely “34 hush-money payments”: the records were the charged objects and the felony element depended on an election-conspiracy predicate. It also was not 34 separate election conspiracies: the 34 counts tracked separate business entries within one alleged reimbursement-and-concealment sequence.

The instructions treated Michael Cohen as an accomplice as a matter of law and required corroborating evidence. They also told jurors that Cohen's federal guilty plea, the FEC investigation, and AMI's agreements were not evidence of Trump's guilt. The jury returned guilty verdicts on all 34 counts on May 30, 2024. On January 10, 2025, the court entered judgment and imposed an unconditional discharge—no incarceration, probation or fine. The First Department later recorded that Trump's appeal from that judgment was perfected. Official orders through May 19, 2026 concern amicus participation; no merits decision was located by the cutoff.

State boundary: this is a conviction and judgment, not an allegation-only case. It is also not appellate-final.

2. Federal classified documents: 40 Trump counts, dismissed on appointment—not evidence—grounds

Case: United States v. Trump, Nauta, and De Oliveira, No. 9:23-cr-80101-AMC, Southern District of Florida.

The June 2023 indictment charged Trump and Walt Nauta. The July 27 superseding indictment added Carlos De Oliveira and new video-surveillance allegations. Of its 42 total counts across all defendants, Trump was charged in 40:

  • Counts 1–32: willful retention of national defense information, one count per described document;
  • Counts 33–38: conspiracy, withholding, concealment and false-statement theories concerning the subpoena and document search; and
  • Counts 40–41: alleged efforts to delete security-camera footage.

Count 39 charged Nauta alone and Count 42 charged De Oliveira alone. Thus “42-count indictment” and “40 charges against Trump” are both correct, but they answer different questions.

Judge Aileen Cannon dismissed the superseding indictment on July 15, 2024. Her order held that the Attorney General lacked statutory authority to appoint Jack Smith as an inferior officer with the powers he exercised, violating the Appointments Clause. It also concluded that the office's use of the permanent indefinite appropriation violated the Appropriations Clause, while declining to choose a separate funding remedy. The order twice confined itself to this proceeding.

The dismissal was a legal ruling against the prosecution's authority, not a factual determination about retention, obstruction or declassification. The Government appealed, then dismissed the appeal as to Trump after the 2024 election, expressly leaving the district-court dismissal in place. The remaining appeal as to Nauta and De Oliveira was later dismissed with prejudice at the Government's request. No jury assessed the allegations, and the district-court ruling was not reviewed on the merits by the Eleventh Circuit.

Volume II of the Special Counsel's final report, addressing the classified-documents investigation, was not public by the cutoff. Official DOJ summaries of 2025 FOIA decisions record that courts treated Judge Cannon's injunction as continuing to bar DOJ release and rejected collateral attempts to obtain the volume. That is a publication restriction, not a merits adjudication of the charged conduct.

State boundary: dismissed before trial; not acquitted; appointment ruling left standing without appellate merits review.

3. Federal election case: four charges survived in form, but the factual route changed

Case: United States v. Donald J. Trump, No. 1:23-cr-257-TSC, District of Columbia.

Both the original and superseding indictments charged the same four offenses:

  1. conspiracy to defraud the United States, 18 U.S.C. § 371;
  2. conspiracy to obstruct an official proceeding, 18 U.S.C. § 1512(k);
  3. obstruction of and attempt to obstruct an official proceeding, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1512(c)(2), 2; and
  4. conspiracy against rights, 18 U.S.C. § 241.

The indictment did not charge insurrection, seditious conspiracy or incitement. It expressly acknowledged Trump's right to speak publicly—even to make false election claims—and to pursue lawful recounts, audits and lawsuits. Its criminal theory was that knowingly false claims were used as instruments in conspiracies to impair the federal collection, counting and certification function, organize elector certificates, pressure state officials and the Vice President, and exploit the January 6 disruption.

Two Supreme Court decisions changed the route:

  • Fischer v. United States narrowed § 1512(c)(2) to conduct impairing the availability or integrity of records, documents, objects or other things used in an official proceeding. The superseding indictment retained Counts 2 and 3 and continued to allege use of fraudulent elector certificates; that theory was never tried.
  • Trump v. United States held that a former President has absolute immunity for conduct within exclusive constitutional authority, at least presumptive immunity for other official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts. It held the alleged Justice Department discussions absolutely immune and required further act-by-act analysis for Vice-Presidential, state, private and public conduct.

The August 27, 2024 superseding indictment responded by reducing the pleading from 45 pages and 130 numbered paragraphs to 36 pages and 106 paragraphs. It removed the Justice Department official as Co-Conspirator 4 and the Justice Department pressure theory, described the remaining co-conspirators as private actors, emphasized Trump's candidate and campaign capacities, and characterized the Ellipse event as a privately funded campaign rally. The four statutory counts stayed the same; the alleged conduct supporting them did not.

On November 25, 2024, DOJ moved to dismiss without prejudice. Its stated reason was its binding Office of Legal Counsel position that the Constitution categorically forbids federal indictment and prosecution of a sitting President. The motion expressly said the result did not turn on the gravity of the charges, strength of proof or merits, which the Government said it continued to support. The court granted dismissal. The case closed without trial.

State boundary: dismissed without prejudice for a temporary office-based constitutional rule; not acquitted and not adjudicated on evidentiary sufficiency.

4. Georgia: the broadest theory, then the sharpest official reassessment

Case: State of Georgia v. Donald John Trump et al., No. 23SC188947, Fulton County Superior Court.

The 98-page indictment charged 19 defendants in 41 counts and alleged 161 overt acts. Trump appeared in 13 counts: 1, 5, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19, 27–29, and 38–39. Those counts covered a Georgia RICO conspiracy, solicitation of oath violations, conspiracies involving elector documents, filing a federal complaint alleged to contain false statements, and statements or solicitations directed to Georgia officials.

The RICO count matters because its overt acts were not all separately charged crimes. The indictment used speeches, tweets, calls, meetings, filings and actions by many people to allege participation in one enterprise. Treating every overt act as an independently charged felony overstates the document.

The indictment contracted before it ended:

  • On March 13, 2024, Judge Scott McAfee quashed Counts 2, 5, 6, 23, 28 and 38 for insufficient pleading detail. For Trump, that removed Counts 5, 28 and 38. The order allowed possible reindictment and left related overt acts within Count 1.
  • On September 12, 2024, the court quashed Counts 14, 15 and 27 because federal law preempted state prosecution of perjury or false filings in a federal court. For Trump, that removed Counts 15 and 27. The order did not decide presidential immunity.

Eight Trump counts therefore remained: 1, 9, 11, 13, 17, 19, 29 and 39.

The Georgia Court of Appeals disqualified District Attorney Fani Willis and her office in December 2024. The Georgia Supreme Court denied review on September 16, 2025. After the Prosecuting Attorneys' Council could not recruit another prosecutor, Executive Director Peter Skandalakis appointed himself and filed a motion to nolle prosequi all remaining defendants and counts on November 26, 2025.

His 22-page public memorandum is the most substantial official reassessment in the four-case record. It did not say nothing troubling happened. It said, among other things, that the Republican elector nominees lacked provable criminal intent and relied on lawyers; that public-speech and legislative-testimony theories raised constitutional and precedent concerns; that the Coffee County proof and witnesses had narrowed after misdemeanor pleas; that Jeffrey Clark's draft letter was predecisional, unsent and legally weak as an attempt; that evidence and federal-record access were insufficient for Mark Meadows; that the Ruby Freeman conduct belonged in Cobb County absent a viable RICO bridge; and that the Raffensperger call reasonably admitted competing interpretations of criminal intent.

As to the overall case, Skandalakis concluded that the nationwide Georgia RICO theory was not a viable basis for prosecution, that many alleged overt acts were insufficient without fuller context, that federal court was the proper venue for the core January 6 obstruction theory, and that immunity litigation, presidential tenure, delay, speedy-trial rights and state resources made further prosecution unproductive. He still described the plan to use elector certificates to disrupt certification as the core serious conduct and called Jack Smith's federal case the strongest prosecutable theory.

That memorandum is a prosecutor's exercise of discretion, not a jury finding or an appellate holding. It is unusually probative of why the state abandoned the case, but it is neither an acquittal nor proof that every allegation was false.

State boundary: five Trump counts were judicially quashed; eight remaining counts were abandoned before trial after prosecutor reassessment; no merits verdict.

What the primary record corrects

“Trump faced 91 criminal counts”

Accurate only as a peak-count snapshot after July 27, 2023. It combines different sovereigns, statutes, units of prosecution and procedural clocks. It is not a present count and never meant 91 separate criminal schemes.

“All four cases were election-interference prosecutions”

Incorrect. The Florida case concerned retention of national-defense documents and alleged obstruction. New York used a business-record offense with an election-conspiracy predicate. The District of Columbia and Georgia cases both concerned the 2020 transfer of power, but used different sovereign authority and materially different charging structures.

“The federal election indictment criminalized Trump saying the election was stolen”

Incomplete. The indictment expressly protected public speech and lawful challenges, even false claims. It alleged that knowingly false claims were used to execute conspiracies affecting official processes. Whether the remaining conduct was unofficial, proved beyond a reasonable doubt, and sufficient after Fischer was never adjudicated.

“The classified-documents case proved the Special Counsel was unconstitutional”

Too broad. One district judge so held in this case, expressly confined the order to the proceeding, and dismissed the indictment. The appeal was abandoned rather than decided, so no appellate court adopted or rejected that reasoning in this prosecution.

“The Georgia case collapsed only because Trump won the election”

Incomplete. The sitting presidency and delay were major factors, but the replacement prosecutor's filed memorandum also rejected or doubted important parts of the RICO, elector-intent, speech, witness, venue, attempt and evidence theories. Before that reassessment, courts had already removed five of Trump's 13 counts and disqualified the original prosecutor's office.

“The dismissals exonerated Trump”

Incorrect as a legal description. The federal and Georgia cases ended without merits verdicts. The Florida order concerned prosecutorial appointment and funding; the D.C. dismissal invoked the sitting-President rule; and Georgia ended through quashal plus nolle prosequi. None was an acquittal. Conversely, allegations in those indictments were never proven at trial and should not be reported as findings.

Evidence limits

  • An indictment proves that a grand jury found probable cause; it does not prove guilt.
  • A jury verdict is a merits adjudication, but the New York judgment remains under appellate review.
  • A pretrial dismissal may establish a legal defect without resolving the alleged conduct. The Florida order did that.
  • A prosecutor's dismissal motion explains that prosecutor's institutional or evidentiary judgment; it is not an acquittal. This matters especially in D.C. and Georgia.
  • The Georgia September 2024 filed order was preserved from a reliable public docket mirror because the clerk's direct route was not locatable in this pass. The document bears the Fulton County electronic filing stamp.
  • The Florida and D.C. dismissal filings were preserved from CourtListener's RECAP copies because the district courts' dockets are served through PACER.
  • New York's court server blocked direct local download of the official jury-instruction and verdict PDFs. Identical public copies were preserved from DocumentCloud and Wikimedia; the official New York court URLs remain the controlling source routes in SOURCE_LEDGER.csv.
