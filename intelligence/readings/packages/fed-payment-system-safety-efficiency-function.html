# Federal Reserve payment-system mandate map — what “safe and efficient” means

**Question:** What does the Federal Reserve System's responsibility to foster or promote a safe and efficient payment system actually mean in law, institutional practice and transaction-level operation?

**Scope:** United States; Federal Reserve payment, clearing, settlement and recording functions; primary official sources checked through September 20, 2026 at 18:00 EDT. This joins the existing Fed/clearing/Treasury package rather than opening a new research family.

**Exclusions:** This is not a compliance opinion on a named institution, a complete rule-by-rule examination of every private network, or proof that any proposed account, token, rail or operating-hours change is live. It does not refresh the separate UN research, the Chronicle, the canonical watch calendar or any installed app.

## Bottom line

The familiar wording is slightly compressed. The Federal Reserve calls the function **“fostering payment and settlement system safety and efficiency”** and says it works to promote a system for U.S. dollar transactions that is **safe, efficient and accessible**. That is a central Federal Reserve function, but it is **not a third statutory mandate parallel to the monetary-policy objectives in Federal Reserve Act §2A**. Congress did not create one all-purpose payment czar or one operative sentence that gives the Board complete authority over every U.S. payment.

Instead, the function is assembled from several legal powers, institutional roles and policies:

1. **Operate:** the Reserve Banks provide central-bank accounts and operate currency services, check services, FedACH, Fedwire Funds, Fedwire Securities, the National Settlement Service and FedNow.
2. **Set access and service terms:** eligible institutions obtain accounts and services under statutes, Board policies, Reserve Bank agreements and risk controls; access to one service does not imply access to all services.
3. **Supply settlement liquidity:** Reserve Banks provide intraday credit under the Payment System Risk policy so payments can settle smoothly while controlling Reserve Bank credit exposure.
4. **Regulate:** the Board issues rules for specified subjects, including Reserve Bank transfers and checks, funds availability, netting, unlawful-internet-gambling payment controls, designated financial market utilities and debit-card routing/interchange.
5. **Supervise and oversee:** the Board and Reserve Banks examine institutions and infrastructures within their jurisdiction, oversee the Reserve Banks' own payment operations, and cooperate with the SEC, CFTC, banking agencies, FSOC and foreign authorities.
6. **Coordinate and catalyze:** the Fed researches system weaknesses, convenes industry participants, promotes standards and sometimes builds a public service when its public-benefit and cost-recovery tests are met.

The job therefore is not merely “keep Fedwire running.” It is to shape the conditions under which dollar claims can be authenticated, cleared, funded, settled with legal finality, recorded, recovered after failure and made broadly reachable—without allowing speed, low price or innovation to externalize unacceptable credit, liquidity, operational, legal, fraud or systemic risk.

## 1. First correction: mandate, function and power are not the same object

| Object | Controlling language | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Monetary-policy mandate** | Federal Reserve Act §2A instructs the Board and FOMC to pursue maximum employment, stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates. | Supplies Congress's monetary-policy objectives. [Federal Reserve Act §2A](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/section-2a-monetary-policy-objectives.htm) |
| **Payment-system function** | The Fed's current institutional description is “fostering payment and settlement system safety and efficiency”; the public explanation adds accessibility. | Names a major system responsibility and organizes many separate authorities. [Fed Explained](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/fedexplained/payment-systems.htm) |
| **Specific legal powers and duties** | Federal Reserve Act provisions, the Monetary Control Act, EFAA/Check 21, FDICIA netting law, Title VIII, the Durbin amendment and other statutes. | Authorize particular services, prices, accounts, rules, supervision, liquidity and oversight. |
| **Board policy** | The Federal Reserve in the Payments System policy and Payment System Risk policy. | States how the Board will use existing authority; policy does not create unlimited new jurisdiction. |
| **Operator rules and contracts** | Operating circulars, service schedules, account agreements and private FMI rulebooks. | Bind participants and determine daily processing, liability, timing and finality inside a particular service. |

“Promote” or “foster” is important. It describes a direction of travel and a public-interest standard, not a guarantee that no payment will fail, no fraud will occur, every person will have a direct Fed account, or every rail will be instant and free.

The Fed itself says its regulatory authority over the payment system is limited and that it influences the system heavily through its operator role. The system is a distributed public–private constitution, not a single Federal Reserve machine. [FedNow additional questions and answers](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fednow-additional-questions-and-answers.htm)

## 2. The legal constitution

| Authority | Payment-system contribution | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Federal Reserve Act §§11, 13 and 16** | Board oversight powers; Reserve Bank deposits, checks, exchange and collection; transfers of funds; note issuance and Reserve Bank clearing/collection activity. | Powers are allocated between the Board and Reserve Banks; they are not a general charter over every private payment company. [Federal Reserve Act](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/federal-reserve-act.htm) |
| **Monetary Control Act of 1980 / FRA §11A, 12 USC §248a** | Requires explicit pricing for listed Reserve Bank services, equal availability to nonmember depository institutions at the same fee schedule, long-run recovery of direct, indirect and imputed private-firm costs, and due regard for competition and adequate nationwide service. Covered services include currency and coin, check clearing, wire transfer, ACH, settlement, securities safekeeping, float and new services. | The statute disciplines public operation; it does not require every proposed service to be launched. [12 USC §248a](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/12/248a) |
| **Expedited Funds Availability Act and Regulation CC** | Governs funds availability, disclosures, check return and same-day-settlement rules. | Consumer rulemaking is shared with the CFPB for specified EFAA subjects; it is not a universal electronic-payment code. [Regulation CC overview](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/regcc-about.htm) |
| **Check Clearing for the 21st Century Act** | Makes a qualifying substitute check the legal equivalent of the original and supplies warranties, indemnities and expedited recredit. | Enables check truncation; it does not convert a check into an instant, irrevocable payment. [Check 21 Act](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/check-clearing-for-the-21st-century-act.htm) |
| **FDIC Improvement Act payment-system-risk provisions** | Protect enforceability of qualifying netting contracts and related settlement rules, reducing uncertainty if a participant fails. | Legal enforceability of netting does not remove credit or liquidity risk. [12 USC Chapter 45](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/12/4401) |
| **Dodd–Frank Title VIII, 12 USC §§5461–5472** | Makes enhanced supervision of systemically important financial market utilities and payment, clearing and settlement activities a federal objective; authorizes uniform risk standards, material-change review and a Board role in liquidity and accounts. | Applies through designation and agency jurisdiction, not to every payment provider. [Title VIII purposes](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/12/5461) |
| **Regulation HH, 12 CFR Part 234** | Implements risk-management standards for designated FMUs supervised by the Board, material-change notice and account/service provisions. | SEC-registered clearing agencies and CFTC-registered DCOs generally sit under their primary agencies' comparable regimes rather than Board risk standards. [Regulation HH](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/regulation-hh-designated-financial-market-utilities.htm) |
| **Electronic Fund Transfer Act / Regulation E** | Consumer disclosures, unauthorized-EFT limits, error resolution and remittance protections. | Most rulemaking authority transferred to the CFPB in 2011; Federal Reserve payment-system safety is not a substitute for individual consumer remedies. [CFPB Regulation E](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/) |
| **Durbin amendment / Regulation II** | Debit-interchange standards, fraud-prevention adjustment, two unaffiliated network choices and merchant routing rights. | Enforcement is divided among prudential regulators, NCUA and FTC; card networks remain private systems. [Regulation II overview](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/regii-about.htm) |

The important constitutional result is **fragmentation with joins**. Congress defines powers and public objectives; the Board writes policy and rules within jurisdiction; twelve Reserve Banks hold accounts and deliver services; private operators set network rules; prudential regulators supervise participants; the SEC and CFTC govern much of securities and derivatives clearing; FSOC designates systemically important utilities; and the CFPB supplies many consumer-facing remedies.

## 3. Institutional control map

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    C[Congress<br/>statutes and public objectives] --> B[Board of Governors<br/>policy, rules, oversight]
    C --> F[FSOC and primary regulators<br/>designation and sector jurisdiction]
    B --> R[12 Reserve Banks<br/>accounts, services, credit, supervision]
    B --> P[Payment System Risk policy<br/>risk standards and intraday credit]
    B --> H[Regulations J, CC, EE, GG, HH and II]
    R --> S[FRFS and service operators<br/>Fedwire, FedACH, FedNow, NSS, checks, cash]
    R --> A[Master accounts and settlement balances]
    R --> L[Intraday liquidity<br/>collateral, caps and fees]
    F --> M[Private FMIs and networks<br/>CHIPS, CLS, DTC, NSCC, FICC, CCPs, cards]
    M --> A
    S --> A
    D[Depository institutions] --> S
    D --> M
    U[Households, firms and governments] --> D
    P --> S
    P --> M
```

The map has no single top-to-bottom operator. The Board does not itself process every transfer; the Reserve Banks do not write every governing statute; and a private network may clear transactions whose ultimate settlement occurs through Reserve Bank accounts.

## 4. The seven operational verbs

| Verb | Actual Federal Reserve work | Main control surface |
|---|---|---|
| **Issue and maintain** | Issue Federal Reserve notes; forecast demand; distribute, authenticate and destroy currency through the Reserve Banks. | Physical integrity, availability, counterfeiting controls and public confidence. [Currency and coin services](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/coin_about.htm) |
| **Operate** | Process checks and ACH entries; run Fedwire Funds, Fedwire Securities, NSS and FedNow; maintain business continuity. | Processing rules, availability, capacity, security, finality, settlement asset and participant agreements. [Payment services](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems.htm) |
| **Keep accounts and gate access** | Maintain master accounts for eligible institutions and decide service access under law and policy. | Eligibility, due diligence, supervision, balances, restrictions, transparency and consistent treatment. [Section 11C database](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section11c.htm) |
| **Fund settlement** | Permit controlled daylight overdrafts and other intraday credit to eligible institutions. | Collateral, net debit caps, fees, monitoring and loss protection. [Intraday credit policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/ii-federal-reserve-intraday-credit-policies.htm) |
| **Regulate** | Write payment-specific rules where Congress supplied authority. | Legal rights, timing, disclosure, routing, netting, risk standards and enforcement allocation. |
| **Supervise and oversee** | Examine supervised institutions and designated utilities; oversee Reserve Bank services against standards comparable to or stronger than those applied to private systems. | Governance, credit, liquidity, operations, cyber resilience, change control, recovery and incident reporting. [Reserve Bank system oversight](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/over_rbsystems.htm) |
| **Catalyze and coordinate** | Research, convene, set strategy, promote standards and directories, and test whether public action can solve coordination failures. | Public benefit, market failure, ubiquity, interoperability, competition and innovation. [2015 payment-improvement strategy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20150126a.htm) |

The Reserve Banks also act as fiscal agents for Treasury. That work can use the same operating capabilities, but a Treasury fiscal-agency payment is not identical to a private payment-service transaction or an FOMC monetary-policy operation. [Fiscal agency services](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fisagy_about.htm)

## 5. Transaction lifecycle: where safety and efficiency attach

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    I[1. Initiate<br/>payer and payee intent] --> V[2. Validate<br/>identity, authority, funds]
    V --> X[3. Exchange<br/>message and instructions]
    X --> C[4. Clear<br/>match, calculate, net]
    C --> Q[5. Fund<br/>liquidity and collateral]
    Q --> T[6. Settle<br/>change claims in settlement asset]
    T --> F[7. Finality<br/>irrevocable legal discharge]
    F --> R[8. Record and reconcile<br/>books, statements, exceptions]
    R --> E[9. Error, fraud and failure response<br/>return, remedy, recovery]
```

Every stage can be fast while the end-to-end payment remains slow. A message can arrive instantly while settlement is deferred. A transfer can settle finally between banks while the customer still has an error claim. A netting arrangement can save liquidity while concentrating losses at the clearing utility. The analysis must therefore name **which claim changed, on whose books, at what time, under which rule and with what remedy**.

## 6. What “safe” means

Safety is not one cybersecurity score. The Federal Reserve's Payment System Risk policy expressly focuses on credit, liquidity, operational and legal risk and on the possibility that a participant or infrastructure failure can transmit disruption across the financial system. [Payment System Risk policy introduction](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/introduction-payment-system-risk.htm)

| Safety dimension | Failure being controlled | Typical controls or evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal basis** | A transfer, lien, netting rule or default action is unenforceable or uncertain. | Statute, regulation, governing law, operating circular, participant agreement and legal opinion. |
| **Settlement finality** | Parties do not know when payment becomes irrevocable; an insolvency later unwinds it. | Defined finality point, real-time gross settlement, protected netting and exception rules. |
| **Credit risk** | A participant owes money or securities and cannot perform. | Admission standards, limits, collateral, loss allocation, guarantees and default funds. |
| **Liquidity risk** | A participant has value but cannot deliver cash or securities when due. | Prefunding, committed resources, intraday credit, collateral, throughput rules and liquidity stress tests. |
| **Settlement-asset risk** | The asset used to discharge obligations fails or loses value. | Central-bank money where practical; controlled commercial-bank exposures where it is not. |
| **Operational resilience** | Hardware, software, people, facilities or vendors fail. | Redundancy, capacity, recovery-time objectives, change management, exercises and incident reporting. |
| **Cyber and fraud integrity** | Unauthorized instructions, account takeover, counterfeit data or malicious disruption. | Authentication, authorization, encryption, monitoring, anomaly controls and recovery; consumer reimbursement depends on separate law and contract. |
| **Record integrity and reconciliation** | Operator, participant and customer books disagree. | Unique identifiers, acknowledgments, balanced control totals, exception queues, audit trails and timely reconciliation. |
| **Custody and collateral integrity** | Securities or collateral cannot be located, valued or mobilized. | Segregation, perfected interests, valuation, haircuts, substitution and delivery-versus-payment. |
| **Default management and recovery** | A participant or operator fails and losses cascade. | Default waterfall, porting, replenishment, recovery tools, wind-down plan and authority coordination. |
| **Access and concentration resilience** | Exclusion or dependence on one operator, bank or vendor creates systemic fragility. | Objective participation rules, alternate routes, interoperability, geographic and vendor resilience. |
| **Transparency and governance** | Hidden rules or conflicted incentives prevent participants and authorities from pricing risk. | Public disclosures, accountable boards, material-change notice, examinations and quantitative reporting. |

The international PFMI framework supplies 24 principles covering these areas and five responsibilities for public authorities. It is an international standard, not self-executing U.S. law; the Board incorporates it through its Payment System Risk policy and Regulation HH where its jurisdiction reaches. [PFMI overview](https://www.bis.org/committees/cpmi/pfmi/overview.htm) and [Federal Reserve Part I risk policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/i-risk-management-for-financial-market-infrastructures.htm).

## 7. What “efficient” means

Efficiency is not synonymous with “fastest” or “cheapest.” It means using resources well while meeting the service, safety, access and resilience requirements of the actual use case.

| Efficiency dimension | The useful question | Common trap |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | How long from customer initiation to usable, final funds—not merely message delivery? | Calling authorization or notification “settlement.” |
| **Availability** | On what days and hours can instructions, funding and settlement occur? | Advertising 24/7 at one layer while funding or exception handling remains on business-day clocks. |
| **Cost** | What are direct fees, indirect compliance/integration expense, float and failure cost? | Looking only at the visible transaction fee. |
| **Liquidity** | How much cash or collateral must participants immobilize and for how long? | Treating netting's liquidity savings as free of concentration risk. |
| **Capacity** | Can the rail process expected and stressed volumes within its service level? | Using average volume to infer peak resilience. |
| **Reach and access** | Can institutions of different sizes and locations participate on fair, lawful terms? | Confusing direct Fed access with universal end-user access. |
| **Interoperability** | Can messages, identities, directories, rules and settlement windows connect without unsafe manual conversion? | Treating a common message format as common money or common finality. |
| **Competition and choice** | Do public and private alternatives discipline price and service without unfair public advantage? | Assuming public provision or private provision is inherently competitive. |
| **Straight-through processing** | How much manual repair, rekeying and reconciliation remains? | Measuring the happy path while ignoring exception labor. |
| **Innovation and adaptability** | Can the system add useful services without destabilizing incumbents or locking users into one vendor? | Treating novelty or adoption as proof of public benefit. |

The Monetary Control Act makes several of these ideas unusually concrete: explicit prices, comparable access for nonmember depository institutions, long-run cost recovery including imputed private-sector costs, competitive factors and adequate nationwide service. Board policy adds a three-part test for a new Federal Reserve service: a reasonable prospect of long-run cost recovery, a clear public benefit, and a reason private providers alone cannot reasonably deliver that benefit with the necessary effectiveness, scope or equity. [Evaluation criteria](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/criteria-for-evaluating-proposed-payments-system-changes.htm)

## 8. Safety and efficiency are joined—and sometimes conflict

| Proposed improvement | Efficiency gain | Safety cost or question | Design response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faster or instant settlement | Less delay and counterparty exposure; quicker funds availability | Less time to detect fraud or recall an error; continuous funding need | Stronger pre-validation, confirmation-of-payee tools, limits, fraud controls and clear customer remedies |
| Netting | Far less gross liquidity needed | Concentrates obligations and makes default management critical | Enforceable netting, margin, liquidity resources, default waterfall and stress testing |
| Wider direct access | More competition and reach | New operational, supervisory, liquidity and resolution risk | Risk-tiered due diligence, service limits, monitoring and transparent decisions |
| Longer hours / 24×7 | Better user availability and cross-border overlap | Staffing, cyber, maintenance and liquidity operations must also become continuous | Coordinated calendars, prefunding/intraday facilities, resilient operations and tested exception handling |
| Richer data and standard messages | Automation, reconciliation and compliance gains | Privacy, data quality and common-mode software/vendor risk | Data minimization, validation, governance, fallback and interoperability testing |
| Centralization | Scale, common standards and lower unit cost | Single point of failure and concentrated control | Redundancy, alternate routes, strict oversight and credible recovery |
| Multiple competing rails | Innovation and bargaining choice | Fragmentation, inconsistent rules and weak network reach | Common standards, directories, gateways and clear allocation of liability |

The governing standard is therefore **risk-adjusted efficiency**. A design is not efficient in the public-interest sense if it produces cheap ordinary transactions by transferring catastrophic or opaque losses to participants, the central bank, customers or taxpayers.

## 9. Rail-by-rail map

| Rail or object | Who operates the core | What the Fed actually controls or influences | Finality / boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Currency** | Board and twelve Reserve Banks with Treasury/BEP and Secret Service roles | Issuance, supply, distribution, authentication and fitness | Physical bearer settlement; coin is Treasury-issued and distributed by Reserve Banks. |
| **Checks** | Reserve Banks and private processors; paying and depositary banks | Reserve Bank collection, Reg J, Reg CC, Check 21 and settlement accounts | Availability, presentment, return, final settlement and consumer recredit are different clocks. |
| **ACH** | FedACH and private EPN are the two U.S. operators | FedACH operation, Reserve Bank settlement, rules applicable to Fed processing, bank supervision | Batch clearing and scheduled settlement; same-day ACH is not continuous instant settlement. [FedACH](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fedach_about.htm) |
| **Fedwire Funds** | Reserve Banks | Direct operation, account access, intraday credit, operating circular, business continuity and oversight | Real-time gross transfers are final and irrevocable when processed to master accounts. [Fedwire Funds](https://www.frbservices.org/financial-services/wires) |
| **FedNow** | Reserve Banks | Direct operation, participation terms, central-bank settlement and 24×7×365 service | Near-real-time interbank clearing and settlement; customers access it through participating institutions, not retail Fed accounts. [FedNow](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fednow_about.htm) |
| **National Settlement Service** | Reserve Banks | Final multilateral settlement for eligible private clearing arrangements through master accounts | Settles the private arrangement's net positions; does not replace that arrangement's own rules or customer obligations. [NSS](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/natl_about.htm) |
| **Fedwire Securities** | Reserve Banks as fiscal agents and service operator | Book-entry transfer and delivery-versus-payment for eligible Treasury, agency and other securities | Final securities transfer linked to funds; custody, beneficial ownership and trading remain separate layers. [Fedwire Securities](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fedsecs_about.htm) |
| **Cards** | Private networks, issuers, acquirers and processors | Reg II, bank supervision, settlement accounts and broad fraud/cyber cooperation | Authorization is not clearing; clearing is not interbank settlement; consumer dispute rights arise under other law and contract. |
| **CHIPS and other private payment systems** | Private operators | Participant supervision, Title VIII/other oversight where applicable, central-bank accounts/services and cooperative oversight | A private rulebook can create finality and netting while settlement ultimately uses central-bank money. |
| **DTC, NSCC, FICC, OCC, CME, ICE Clear Credit and CLS** | Separate private utilities | FSOC designation architecture; Board, SEC or CFTC standards depending on entity; account/service and material-change roles | Each utility has its own legal person, asset, rulebook, margin, default and settlement design. FSOC currently lists eight designated FMUs. [FSOC designations](https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/financial-markets-financial-institutions-and-fiscal-service/fsoc/designations) |
| **Correspondent, closed-loop, wallet and stablecoin arrangements** | Banks and private providers | Bank supervision, account and settlement interfaces, limited activity-specific rules and coordination | A message, token or private claim is not automatically central-bank money, a Fed liability or final Fed settlement. |

## 10. Who decides what

| Actor | Decides or performs | Does not decide alone |
|---|---|---|
| **Congress** | Statutory eligibility, powers, objectives, consumer rights, regulator jurisdiction and reporting. | Daily service operations and individual transactions. |
| **Board of Governors** | Federal Reserve policy, regulations, Reserve Bank oversight, specified FMU standards, intraday-credit framework and many national access principles. | Every Reserve Bank account decision, every private network rule or every consumer claim. |
| **Twelve Reserve Banks** | Hold eligible accounts; deliver services; apply operating agreements; make institution-specific decisions within law and Board policy; supervise assigned institutions. | Unilateral national law or the legal duties of another regulator. |
| **Federal Reserve Financial Services / product offices** | Systemwide product operation, service design and implementation within Federal Reserve governance. | Independent statutory policy. |
| **FSOC** | Designates systemically important FMUs and activities under Title VIII. | Day-to-day primary supervision of every designated utility. |
| **SEC and CFTC** | Securities-clearing-agency and derivatives-clearing rules and supervision. | Federal Reserve account eligibility or Reserve Bank operations. |
| **OCC, FDIC, NCUA, state authorities and the Fed** | Supervise their respective participating institutions. | The entire end-to-end network when jurisdiction is elsewhere. |
| **CFPB** | Most federal consumer-financial rulemaking and enforcement within its statutory perimeter. | Wholesale settlement operations or prudential liquidity policy. |
| **Private operators** | Network membership, technical rules, prices, loss allocation and services subject to law and oversight. | Public law, central-bank account policy or public backstop terms. |

The eight Title VIII FMUs illustrate the split. FSOC designates; the Board is the supervisory agency for CLS and CHIPS/The Clearing House Payments Company; the SEC is primary for DTC, FICC, NSCC and OCC; and the CFTC is primary for CME and ICE Clear Credit. The Board can still have consultation, examination, material-change or account/service roles outside its primary-supervisor lane.

## 11. How to measure performance without being fooled

There is no single official “safe and efficient” score. A useful evidence board would keep at least these measures separate.

### Safety measures

- service availability, outage duration and recovery time by rail;
- unauthorized-payment, fraud, counterfeit and error losses with denominator and loss bearer;
- late, rejected, returned and unreconciled transactions;
- finality disputes, legal challenges and settlement failures;
- daylight overdrafts, collateral coverage, liquidity shortfalls and peak funding use;
- participant defaults, near misses, default-fund use and replenishment;
- cyber or operational incidents, material-change notices and remediation;
- concentration in participants, settlement banks, vendors and common software;
- recovery and wind-down tests, not merely written plans.

### Efficiency and access measures

- end-to-end time to usable and final funds;
- operating hours and the hours during which funding, liquidity and exception support are available;
- direct fee, integration cost, compliance cost, float and exception cost;
- unit cost and Monetary Control Act cost-recovery performance for priced services;
- throughput, peak capacity and latency under stress;
- number, size, geography and type of participating institutions;
- adoption by receiving as well as sending institutions;
- liquidity and collateral required per dollar settled;
- manual repairs, reconciliation breaks and straight-through-processing rate;
- ability to change providers or interoperate without losing data, reach or legal certainty.

Three denominator rules are essential: **count and value are different; attempted and completed transactions are different; operator performance and end-user experience are different**. A rail can have 99.99% availability while a small number of missing minutes carry enormous value or systemic importance.

## 12. What the function does not mean

It does **not** mean:

- that the Federal Reserve owns or operates every payment rail;
- that every payment company, wallet, stablecoin or card network is directly regulated by the Board;
- that “safe” means zero failure or a government guarantee of every claim;
- that “efficient” means cheapest, fastest or most centralized;
- that a payment message equals clearing, settlement, finality or customer funds availability;
- that one institution's eligibility for an account guarantees approval, unrestricted balances, credit or every service;
- that central-bank settlement removes fraud, credit, operational or consumer-protection disputes elsewhere in the chain;
- that private clearing displaces the Federal Reserve's currency, account, liquidity or settlement roles;
- that a proposal, pilot, standards document, registration or press release proves operating adoption.

## 13. Current research frontier at the cutoff

1. **FedNow reach versus end-user ubiquity:** launch and institutional availability are established; universal bank participation, universal customer sending capability and fraud/remedy outcomes require separate measurement.
2. **Account-access constitution:** Board guidelines, individual Reserve Bank adjudication, master-account publication and any service restrictions must be kept distinct. The May 2026 Payment Account is a proposal, not current universal access. [Payment Account proposal](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260520a.htm)
3. **Continuous settlement versus continuous liquidity:** 24×7 payment operation does not itself create 24×7 collateral, correspondent funding, Treasury securities settlement or human exception support.
4. **Tokenized claims and central-bank money:** tokenization can change the record or transfer method while leaving issuer, access, redemption, collateral, settlement asset and loss allocation intact.
5. **Private FMI modernization:** longer hours, new interfaces and new asset records should be tested against existing membership, account, default, settlement and finality controls rather than treated as one launch.
6. **Public accountability:** Federal Reserve, Treasury, regulators and private operators publish rich but fragmented evidence; a joined, rail-by-rail scorecard remains an analytical construction rather than a ready-made official dataset.

## 14. Reusable audit template

For any payment-system claim, ask in this order:

1. **Object:** What exactly moves—cash, a bank deposit claim, reserve balance, security, token, message or legal entitlement?
2. **Actors:** Who is payer, payee, issuer, operator, participant, settlement institution, custodian, supervisor and loss bearer?
3. **Authority:** Which statute, rule, policy, operating circular, contract and private rulebook applies?
4. **Access:** Who may connect directly; who uses an intermediary; what restrictions or credit rights attach?
5. **Lifecycle:** When are authorization, clearing, funding, settlement, finality, customer posting and remedy?
6. **Safety:** Where are credit, liquidity, operational, cyber, legal, custody, fraud and concentration risks?
7. **Efficiency:** What time, fee, float, liquidity, collateral, reconciliation and integration resources are consumed?
8. **Failure:** What happens if the payer, participant, operator, settlement bank, vendor or asset issuer fails?
9. **Evidence state:** Is the claimed change proposed, approved, technically available, participant-adopted, transaction-observed or financially material?
10. **Public-interest test:** Who receives the benefit, who bears the cost and loss, and what less concentrated alternative exists?
