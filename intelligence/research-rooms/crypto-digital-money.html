What makes a legal stablecoin trustworthy

Under the GENIUS Act there is no single stamp that makes a stablecoin "trusted": the issuer's approval, the coin's code and keys, its reserves, the platforms selling it and the fixes for mistakes each have to be proven separately.

What we found

  • The GENIUS Act is law, but most of the rules to carry it out were still only proposed as of August 17, 2026. No approved U.S. issuer, state certification or registered foreign issuer had been found.
  • Legal approval and technical control are two separate locks. A regulator's approval doesn't prove who holds the keys that mint, freeze or burn coins, and a valid digital signature doesn't prove the signer had legal authority.
  • The blockchain shows how many coins exist, but reserves sit off-chain with banks and custodians. Matching them takes bank records, a monthly accounting-firm exam and CEO/CFO sign-off, and none of that can prove there's no hidden debt or side deal.
  • A "bridged" or "wrapped" copy of a coin on another blockchain shouldn't count as approved just because it has the same name. Who controls it and who owes redemption have to be checked first.
  • Freezing coins on a government or court order is three separate questions: can the code do it, is there a valid order, and was it actually done and logged? Treasury has only asked, not required, that sellers test this.

Why it matters

The GENIUS Act doesn't put law on a blockchain. It makes named companies, their coin-control keys, their reserves and the platforms that sell their coins answerable to federal law, which pulls private money inside the regulatory system one approval at a time.

What to watch

  • pending · The first named issuer approval, state-regime certification or foreign issuer registration under GENIUS. None had been found as of August 17, 2026.
  • pending · Whether Treasury's final rule makes sellers check stablecoin code for seize, freeze and burn functions. Its August 17 proposal only asks the question.

Who's wiring the new private money system

No single coin or blockchain is winning. Banks, crypto firms, card networks and market utilities are building a layered system where each controls a different checkpoint, and firms are racing to own the connectors between them.

What we found

  • The real contest is over connectors, not coins. No firm controls all seven layers, from the customer's app down to final cash settlement, so the strategic players try to hold two or three neighboring layers that every transaction must cross.
  • Crypto firms are becoming regulated banks and trust companies, but each step is separate. World Liberty's proposed trust bank, for example, has only preliminary conditional OCC approval and can't open before final approval.
  • Open USD makes the adapter model concrete: Bridge is the issuer, Open Standard organizes shared economics and future governance, and Mastercard is a founding investor and access route after buying BVNK. The coin is live on four chains, but launch supply is not payment volume.
  • "24/7" usually covers only part of the trip. The on-chain transfer can be instant while card-network settlement and final bank or reserve settlement still run on older schedules.
  • Lots of brands can hide a few shared dependencies: the same reserve banks, custodians, cloud and key providers, and regulators. More coins and chains doesn't mean more independent plumbing.

Why it matters

The change is running through charters, licenses and rulebooks. Crypto firms are becoming federally supervised banks and trust companies, while DTC, Visa, Mastercard and Swift bolt new rails onto their existing rules instead of being torn down.

What to watch

  • H1 2027 · Whether DTC's connection to the Stellar public blockchain reaches production, and which assets qualify.
  • pending · Final OCC approval for World Liberty Trust Company, and whether USD1 issuing actually moves over from BitGo.
  • pending · The Fed's proposed rules for supervising stablecoin issuers (proposed, not final).
  • pending · OUSD's first independent monthly reserve attestation and the exact account, custodian, measurement and exception coverage.
  • pending · A named Mastercard OUSD customer flow plus Open Standard's board, voting, ownership and partner-reward terms.

How Congress is building the new money plumbing

A big rebuild of U.S. financial plumbing is under way, but not as one bill: Congress, a dozen agencies and market utilities like DTC are each moving separate pieces that are converging.

What we found

  • DTC ran real but limited production trades with tokenized securities on July 15, involving more than 30 firms, while keeping the official record of who holds what. The wider service for participants is scheduled for October, not launched.
  • GENIUS (stablecoins) cleared all four committees and became law. CLARITY (crypto market structure) passed the House on July 17, 2025, but Senate Banking and Senate Agriculture advanced different versions that still need to be reconciled.
  • The PACE bill (H.R. 8395) would let nonbank payment firms register with the OCC and then separately apply for a Fed account to use Fedwire, FedNow and FedACH. It's only a bill, with no automatic Fed access or bank charter.
  • The American Reserve Modernization Act (H.R. 8957) would turn the executive-order Bitcoin reserve into law, with a 20-year holding floor and quarterly proof of reserves. It was introduced but not advanced; its gold-revaluation idea is only a study.
  • The House task force on monetary policy and Treasury markets was reauthorized, effective July 23, 2026, so its hearing series continues into a second round.

Why it matters

This is the legislative assembly line: stablecoins, nonbank payment firms and a federal Bitcoin reserve are being written into statute and handed to existing agencies like the OCC, Fed, SEC and Treasury, not built outside them.

What to watch

  • October 2026 · DTC's scheduled launch of its tokenization service for participants (announced target, not yet live).
  • 2026-10-20 · Treasury's written answers to the House committee after the September 15 hearing.
  • pending · Senate Banking and Senate Agriculture reconciling their versions of CLARITY.
  • pending · GENIUS final rules, the exact effective date and the first approved stablecoin issuer.

What stablecoins reveal, and what they still hide

Public-chain stablecoins let anyone see transfers, but what makes money trustworthy (who backs it, who can redeem, who can freeze it, what happens after fraud or failure) still sits with institutions behind the screen.

What we found

  • Every easier way to pay (checks, cards, Apple Pay) moved complexity into a rule-bound back office. Cards made payments visible to operators, not the public; Apple Pay hides your card number but keeps the same card system.
  • Public blockchains can show addresses, balances, transfers and supply changes. They usually don't show who really holds the coins, which banks hold the reserves, who can redeem, the total conversion cost or what legal remedies exist.
  • Stablecoins genuinely speed up transfers inside the same coin, chain and custody setup. Complexity comes back at bank entry and exit, currency conversion, cross-chain moves, key recovery, sanctions screening, refunds and issuer failure.
  • To earn trust like cash, checks and cards did, stablecoins must answer seven questions: who owes you, what backs it, who can redeem and when, total cost, when payment is final, who can freeze it, and what happens after failure.
  • Japan and Korea's pilots favor visibility for banks and regulators over public visibility. Korea's Project Hangang has no public ledger, and Japan's JPYSC stays inside verified accounts, with public-chain use still in the future.

Why it matters

The public ledger is new, but the rules that make the money trustworthy are still being set by issuers, banks and regulators. The open question is whether those rules become as visible as the ledger, or whether a transparent ledger just fronts another private system.

SEC opens a trial lane for tokenized stocks

On September 17, 2026 the SEC issued a live five-year order letting approved participants trade tokenized versions of listed stocks through automated pools on public blockchains. No such venue is running yet, and custody, clearing and settlement are left to others.

What we found

  • The SEC's order (Release 34-106402) took effect September 17, 2026 and runs through September 17, 2031. It lets new "Tokenized Securities Venues" trade tokenized listed stocks through automated pools on a public blockchain without registering as an exchange.
  • The chain is open but access isn't. The venue chooses who trades, sets fees and can pause or upgrade the contracts. Firms may supply liquidity with their own money without registering as dealers.
  • A tokenized stock can trade against another tokenized stock, a tokenized money market fund or any non-security crypto asset. That opens a door for coins like XRP or RLUSD, but none is named or selected.
  • Securities law, shareholder rights and trading halts still apply, and a company can block a third-party token of its stock by objecting within 30 days. The order doesn't cover share issuance, official records, custody, clearing or legal settlement finality.
  • Separately, a March 2026 SEC interpretation set categories for which crypto assets are securities, and nine nonbinding staff FAQs on September 25 applied it without changing it.

Why it matters

The SEC is using exemption powers it already has, with no new law from Congress, to carve a time-limited trading lane for tokenized stocks, while official records, issuer rights and securities law stay with the traditional institutions.

What to watch

  • pending · The first venue's public notice, which must be posted at least 30 days before it can start trading. None was operating as of September 25.
  • pending · Which tokens, if any, get paired in actual pools. XRP, RLUSD and every other named token are still unselected.
  • pending · The comment deadline, and any SEC move to make the exemption permanent or widen it.
  • 2031-09-17 · The order expires unless the SEC changes or extends it.

SEC proposes blockchain-based official shareholder records

On September 1, 2026 the SEC proposed letting a blockchain serve as all or part of a company's official shareholder list, as long as one registered transfer agent stays legally responsible for it.

What we found

  • The proposal would require an electronic master shareholder file and allow it to run across several linked systems, including one or more blockchains. It is proposed, not final: no final rule and no effective date exist.
  • Whatever technology is used, one registered transfer agent must stay responsible for, and in exclusive control of, each class of shares. The SEC is regulating who answers for the record, not picking a chain.
  • New reporting and procedure rules would come with it: firms must disclose tokenization and outside tech vendors, adopt compliance procedures, and handle restricted-share legends. The SEC also asks how corrections work on a blockchain that can't be edited.
  • Ripple, Securitize and Wormhole are separate players with separate roles. Ripple's investments and partnerships don't make it anyone's transfer agent, and Wormhole just moves data between chains; it doesn't own or keep the record.
  • Several chains can coexist, with one legally responsible record sitting above them. Other countries follow a similar pattern: shared technology, but legal title and money stay under local control.

Why it matters

The SEC isn't banning or blessing crypto here; it is rewriting a decades-old recordkeeping rulebook so a blockchain can quietly become part of the legal shareholder ledger. That is the old system rebuilding its own plumbing through ordinary rulemaking.

What to watch

  • pending · Federal Register publication, which starts the 60-day comment clock and sets the exact comment deadline
  • pending · Final SEC decision on keeping one responsible transfer agent per share class, and final definitions of blockchain records and 'exclusive control'
  • pending · First registered company actually running a multi-chain shareholder record with a named official file

SEC advisers' tokenization advice versus actual SEC action

The SEC's Investor Advisory Committee told the SEC in March 2026 not to grant a blanket tokenization exemption; the SEC's September 17 order carried several similar limits but never cites the committee.

What we found

  • The committee gives advice only; it can't approve a venue, token, chain or rule. On March 12, 2026 it voted 12–0 (two abstaining) on tokenized stock: no blanket exemption, public comment first, and three protections kept.
  • Those protections: holders must be told what they actually own (votes, dividends, rights in bankruptcy), middlemen must stay regulated, and orders must still get the best price. It also warned third-party 'wrapped' tokens may not carry full shareholder rights.
  • The SEC's September 17 order is temporary and conditional, running through 2031, and was effective immediately with comments requested afterward. Commissioner Peirce engaged directly with the committee's draft, but this is similarity, not proven adoption.
  • The committee pushed back on a live SEC proposal: it recommended keeping mandatory quarterly (10-Q) reports rather than letting companies switch to twice-yearly reporting.
  • March and September meetings also discussed a more machine-readable SEC filing system (EDGAR) as input for AI. The study treats that as a working theory, not an announced SEC plan.

Why it matters

Tokenized stock is entering U.S. markets through the SEC's own machinery: an advisory committee drafts the guardrails, then the Commission issues an order in the same shape. That is institutions rewriting themselves from inside, step by procedural step.

What to watch

  • 2026-12-03 · Next committee meeting (virtual): the next round of advice

  • pending · SEC final decision or withdrawal on replacing quarterly 10-Q reports with optional twice-yearly reporting
  • pending · SEC decision on scrapping Rule 611, the rule that sends trades to the best displayed price

Apps can now sell regulated derivatives without registering

On September 17, 2026 CFTC staff said any qualifying 'passive' software app (crypto or not) can market regulated derivatives and earn fees without registering as a broker, while the trades stay inside registered firms.

What we found

  • Staff Letter 26-25 takes relief first given only to the crypto wallet Phantom in March and opens it to all qualifying software providers, expressly including non-crypto apps.
  • Such apps can show prices, send users' orders to a registered exchange or broker, promote specific contracts (including event contracts and perpetuals), refer users to named firms, and take revenue share or per-trade fees.
  • Limits: the app can't hold customer money or assets, give buy/sell signals, or choose how orders are routed or executed. Registered exchanges, brokers and clearinghouses still sign up the customer and hold the collateral.
  • Strings attached: ten conditions including disclosures, broker-style marketing rules, recordkeeping, a filed notice, and shared legal liability between the app and each partner firm.
  • It's a staff promise not to recommend enforcement, not a Commission rule. It is not a DeFi safe harbor, a spot-crypto framework, or permission for AI trading agents. As of the cutoff, no app had been found using it.

Why it matters

Two days after the Senate stalled the CLARITY crypto bill (49–50 on September 15), CFTC staff used existing powers to widen who can sell regulated derivatives; the study says the timing fits but doesn't prove cause. Access spreads out to apps, while accounts, custody and enforcement stay with registered firms.

What to watch

  • pending · First app filing a notice to rely on the letter, and the first filed joint-liability agreement with a registered firm
  • pending · First named wallet or app launch sending event-contract or perpetual orders through this route
  • pending · CFTC rulemaking or guidance that would replace this interim staff letter
  • pending · Any court or state action on whether these apps get the same federal protection from state gambling law as the exchanges they connect to

Coinbase gets its own derivatives clearinghouse

On September 28, 2026 the CFTC registered Coinbase Clearing LLC to clear fully collateralized futures, options on futures and swaps, but no first product, member or trade has yet been established.

What we found

  • This is a Commission registration order under Commodity Exchange Act section 5b, not a proposal, speech or staff no-action letter.
  • Coinbase Global now owns distinct venue, FCM and DCO affiliates; the regulated clearing actor is Coinbase Clearing LLC, held through The Clearing Company of San Francisco.
  • The application rulebook allows FCM-intermediated and qualifying self-clearing members, requires full collateral before accepting a trade and says the DCO therefore does not maintain a mutualized member-default financial-resource package.
  • Coinbase's current domestic perpetual-style futures still publicly name Nodal Clear and use initial and variation margin. No first Coinbase Clearing product, member, collateral notice, settlement institution or transaction was located.
  • The order does not approve spot crypto, securities clearing, USDC or Base settlement, current margined perpetuals, or Coinbase's separately pending single-stock-perpetual product and customer-margin filings.

Why it matters

Coinbase can now bring a regulated central-counterparty function inside its corporate group, giving it more control over admission, collateral, settlement operations and data. But the permission is confined to a fully collateralized model, and concentration, custody, liquidation, affiliate and operating risks remain.

Who actually controls the crypto infrastructure

This hub tracks who legally controls the new crypto and tokenization infrastructure, keeping technology, legal actors, permissions, records and operating proof separate. The SEC's October 1 crypto-custody proposal shows how key control, segregation, audits and onchain records could fit into one custody chain.

What we found

  • The SEC's October 1 crypto-custody action is a proposal, not a final rule; its 60-day comment period starts only after Federal Register publication.
  • Under the proposal, holding any portion of private-key material can count as custody. An adviser could hold assets itself only as a last resort, after showing asset by asset that no qualified custodian will take them, re-checking at least quarterly and moving them once one is available.
  • Onchain records could count as regulatory records only if they are complete, retained, promptly producible in human-readable form and supplemented offchain with ownership and client data.
  • State trust companies would become a conditional custodian class, but the proposal does not automatically approve every trust company or settle capital, insurance, rehypothecation, loss-reporting, exchange or DeFi questions.
  • The SEC puts a dollar figure on about $301.9 million in initial cost and $433.7 million in recurring annual cost, but only $176,472 in narrow administrative benefits. It says the main access and investor-protection benefits cannot be quantified.

Why it matters

The new crypto infrastructure is being built by and attached to existing institutions such as big banks, DTC and licensed trust companies, rather than replacing them. Money and markets are being rebuilt inside the existing system.

  • After Federal Register publication · S7-2026-35 publication date and 60-day comment deadline.
  • October 2026 · DTC's expected general launch of its tokenization service (after July's limited production).
  • 2028-07-18 · GENIUS Act cutoff: U.S. crypto platforms may offer only approved (permitted) stablecoins after this date.

What finance has actually moved onto blockchains

Institutional finance isn't moving onto one new blockchain system; it is shifting piece by piece onto a patchwork of connected, regulated ledgers, with the hardest legal and money pieces still unsolved.

What we found

  • Real now: regulated digital bond issuance (Switzerland's SDX, Clearstream), programmable bank deposits, and limited real-money settlement pilots. Europe now lets some blockchain-issued assets be pledged for central-bank financing.
  • Still weak: one shared ownership record across networks, routine central-bank-money settlement on the same ledger, collateral that works everywhere, shared accounting close, and proof of what happens when something fails.
  • Big incumbents like DTC, Clearstream, SIX, Euroclear, LSEG and Swift aren't disappearing. They're becoming the rule-setters for these networks: who gets in, what counts as the legal record, corrections and defaults.
  • Faster, 24/7 settlement can actually raise liquidity needs, because trades settle one-by-one instead of netting and cash or collateral may not be available at all hours.
  • No platform has yet shown it can survive a real failure (a crash, a default, a mass exit) with a documented legal and accounting fix. The study says that, not the next launch, is the real test.

Why it matters

Banks, depositories and central banks are re-expressing their own authority in code rather than being replaced: they keep the legal record, the money and the final say. That is the old order rebuilding itself procedurally, from inside.

How 16 financial giants are going digital

Sixteen of the biggest banks and fund managers are not racing toward one 'bank on blockchain' model; they are picking five different roles, and none has yet shown the whole chain working at scale under stress.

What we found

  • The 16 incumbents split into five camps: banks turning deposits into digital money, custodians adding digital wallets to their existing books, firms issuing digital bonds and funds, firms putting fund shares straight on public blockchains, and firms still in research mode.
  • The strongest players aren't the ones with the most products. They're the ones that can tie together the legal claim, the official record, the cash, the collateral, and the ability to fix errors and survive a failure.
  • Fidelity was added late and has a lot live: a national trust-bank digital custodian, its FIDD stablecoin, a limited on-chain record for its FYOXX fund shares, and collateral accounts. Its 401(k) reach is distribution muscle, not control over what plans hold.
  • Retirement money could shift onto blockchain quietly, underneath the same 401(k) screen. But no live Fidelity or Franklin model yet shows a tokenized U.S. workplace target-date fund, a plan-level blockchain record, or benefits paid in digital cash.
  • No institution publicly proves full scale plus public stress-testing, error correction, recovery and retiring the old systems across all seven areas studied.

Why it matters

The same banks and fund giants that run today's system are rebuilding their own plumbing for digital money and records from the inside. Retirement accounts could move onto new rails while savers see nothing change on screen.

SWIFT's blockchain ledger is moving real money

Banks report live payments moving over SWIFT's shared ledger, and Oracle now offers banks a way to connect their own tokenized-deposit systems. No bank has yet been named as live on the Oracle connection.

What we found

  • Six bank pairings have completed live transactions, involving eight of the 17 banks in the first group, including Standard Chartered–HSBC, UOB–HSBC in Hong Kong dollars, and Citi with FAB and OCBC in U.S. dollars. DBS sent a U.S. dollar payment from Singapore to Citi New York on Saturday, September 5, taking minutes.
  • The money stays as ordinary bank deposits on each bank's books; SWIFT matches and nets what the banks owe each other, and the banks then settle through their usual channels. On September 10, DBS, OCBC and UOB also reported live domestic Singapore-dollar transactions run the same way.
  • Oracle can supply a bank-side ledger, wallets, signing, payment processing and accounting tools, but it does not run SWIFT's ledger, issue deposits or act as the final-settlement bank. No SWIFT bank has shown a posted journal, close result or auditor reliance through Oracle.
  • The scheme now has a public go-live confirmation process, and SWIFT contemplates publishing each institution's compliance level against the business rules in SwiftRef. The rule text itself is not public.
  • Still unknown: the first named Oracle bank, an Oracle-facilitated transaction, pricing, who can join, and who bears the loss if final settlement fails after a customer already has the money. Oracle's Digital Assets Data Nexus is planned for fiscal 2027.

Why it matters

The existing cross-border payment network is rebuilding itself on blockchain from the inside. Oracle's reach across payments, databases and accounting makes it a credible path to books built from these events, but the public evidence stops before any posted journal or close.

Could XRP quietly power currency conversions?

Ripple has built a big payments, custody, stablecoin and brokerage business, and XRP could in theory act as a hidden bridge between currencies, but there is no public proof yet that it routinely does.

What we found

  • Ripple, the XRP Ledger, the RLUSD stablecoin and the XRP token are four separate bets. Ripple the company can succeed while XRP stays optional.

Who's building the new money machine?

No single coin is winning. The field is becoming many specialized networks linked through a few companies and regulated middlemen, and the native tokens are mostly fuel, not the money institutions actually move.

What we found

  • Each network is going after a different job: Ripple owns the most connecting pieces, Stellar distributes regulated assets, Hedera runs hybrid public/private workflows, XDC does trade finance, Quant coordinates bank ledgers, Chainlink supplies data, and Canton hosts private institutional apps.

  • Securitize complements Ripple rather than competing: Ripple handles money, movement, custody and liquidity, while Securitize handles the legal securities record and shareholder servicing.
  • The big test is moving from hosting one token or one pilot to repeated, legally enforceable use with rules, recovery and stress-proof cash. Power will sit with whoever keeps the official records and the cash.

Why it matters

The new financial plumbing still ends at banks, DTC, custodians and courts for final say. Old institutions are absorbing crypto networks rather than being replaced by them.

What makes a record official on a blockchain

A record is not official because it sits on a blockchain; it is official because a law or regulator gives it a specific job, a named institution is responsible for it, and something legally follows from it.

What we found

  • "Official record" is not one legal status. A record can be official for one purpose (say, regulatory compliance) and carry no weight for another (say, ownership or audit). Paper, a database or a blockchain can all qualify if the rules allow it.
  • On September 24, CFTC staff said covered firms can keep required records natively on a blockchain, without a duplicate off-chain copy just because blockchain is used. It is staff guidance, not a Commission rule, and the firm stays fully responsible.
  • Firms using a public blockchain must still be able to produce their records if the network or block explorer goes down. The required record also includes the data needed to find, read and explain it, not just the transaction itself.
  • SEC staff goes further: a registered transfer agent may use a blockchain as its official Master Securityholder File, which decides registered holders. The related September 2026 SEC transfer-agent rule is proposed, not final.
  • An on-chain record does not decide accounting or audit questions: which entity reports the item, when it is recognized, how it is measured, or whether it is enough audit evidence. Official, true, public and final are separate claims.

Why it matters

A federal regulator let a blockchain count as the legal compliance record through a staff FAQ under existing rules, with no new law. The machinery changes from inside, while responsibility, ownership and audit judgment stay with accountable institutions.

What to watch

  • pending · Whether the SEC finalizes, changes or withdraws its September 2026 transfer-agent modernization proposal.
  • pending · Whether the full CFTC Commission adopts a binding rule or interpretation replacing today's staff-only guidance.
  • pending · A named futures or swaps firm announcing a live system that keeps its required CFTC records natively on-chain.
  • pending · A live blockchain-based securityholder file hitting a fork, outage, mistaken transfer, court order or lost key, and showing how it was corrected.

Real-time books and the future audit

Blockchain, smart contracts and AI can make books update nearly in real time, but none of them is an audit, and today's SEC reporting and audit opinions are still tied to fixed periods.

What we found

  • Katie's real-time-books idea is directionally right with one correction: transactions can be continuously verifiable, controls continuously executed and evidence continuously examined by AI, but only an independent auditor's engagement and report can call something "audited."
  • Atomic settlement ties both sides of a trade together so both complete or neither does. That removes a class of reconciliation and counterparty risk, but it does not prove goods were delivered, the price was fair or who truly owns the asset.
  • Routine items (cash, token balances, receivables, interest, depreciation, reconciliations) can plausibly update continuously. Estimates, impairments, contingencies, consolidation and disclosures still need judgment, so a fully automatic close is not realistic.
  • The audit likely shifts from sampling and rebuilding past transactions toward testing the systems: code, keys, permissions, outside data feeds, AI models, exceptions and corrections. SEC independence rules still bar the auditor from running or designing the client's books.
  • No general SEC continuous financial-statement regime or PCAOB continuous-audit opinion was found. Reporting is still 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K; continuous public assurance is a future standards question.

Why it matters

The plumbing of accounting is being rebuilt inside existing rules: CFTC and SEC now allow on-chain records, and PCAOB amendments cover auditors analyzing electronic data at scale. The legal audit opinion and SEC reporting calendar have not changed yet.

What to watch

  • pending · The SEC proposing or adopting more frequent or continuously updated company financial-statement reporting.
  • pending · The PCAOB, AICPA or IAASB proposing a continuous-assurance reporting model with defined criteria and report form.
  • pending · A public company publishing versioned, continuously updated GAAP statements with an independent assurance label and full correction history.
  • pending · A real failure (fork, outage, key compromise or AI model error) showing whether the code, data feed, rule, model or a human bore the error.

The dollar stays; the rails go private

The likely shift is not crypto replacing the dollar: the dollar stays the anchor, regulated stablecoins and tokenized deposits become its everyday forms, private systems take over routing, and the Fed stays decisive for final settlement and crises.

What we found

  • Money is splitting into separate jobs: the government sets the unit and policy; banks and stablecoin issuers issue private dollar claims; private platforms handle the customer route; markets supply conversion; clearinghouses net; the Fed and banks provide final settlement and emergency cash.
  • The Fed may become less visible in everyday payments but more central as the common settlement point and emergency balance sheet underneath many private systems. CHIPS already works this way: private netting, settled through the Fed.
  • Three things could speed adoption together: GENIUS stablecoin rules (proposed, not final as of Aug 31), AI agents creating machine-driven payments (early groundwork, not proven scale), and the forced switch to post-quantum cryptography.
  • XRP matters only if routing systems repeatedly pick it as the cheapest permitted bridge and institutions must hold financed XRP inventory. Big routed volume is not the same as real token demand; Ripple can succeed while XRP stays optional.
  • This is mostly a building period. Little private-rail market share so far does not disprove the thesis; real movement needs named issuers, final rules, recurring volume, legal finality and a handled failure.

Why it matters

The dollar isn't being overthrown; it is being reorganized through its own statute (GENIUS), its regulators (OCC and FDIC proposals) and Fed accounts. Private firms get the routes; public institutions keep settlement and the crisis backstop.

Who really holds power in digital finance

New digital systems spread out who moves money and data, but the power to define the legal claim, keep the controlling record, accept collateral, supply final cash, fix failures and absorb losses stays with states, regulated issuers, central banks, clearinghouses and courts.

What we found

  • What moves most is the customer interface and transaction execution. Record-keeping moves only in limited ways, collateral moves selectively, and final liquidity and big systemic losses move least.
  • SWIFT's blockchain ledger shares payment commitments, but banks keep their assets and funding and settlement stays off-ledger; 17 banks were preparing live pilots as of July. At DTC, tokens can move entitlements, yet DTC's off-chain books stay official.
  • Europe's Pontes links blockchain platforms to the central bank's TARGET system, with cash settlement still in T2. The Eurosystem plans its own blockchain platform with native tokenized central-bank money: the state stepping into the new machinery. Planned, not yet built.
  • Japan's Project Agorá ran 30 real-value transactions (about CHF 800,000), but central banks and commercial banks still issued and managed the tokens with fallback procedures. A MUFG-led government-bond trial left the cash leg and production finality unresolved.
  • The trade: incumbents may lose the customer route, some fees and first sight of data, but keep claim definition, licensing, collateral rules and emergency cash. Result: a smaller everyday incumbent and a stronger crisis incumbent.

Why it matters

This is the thesis in action: states, central banks and utilities are not being torn down but are writing their core powers into the new machinery (SWIFT, DTC, Eurosystem, Japan). The open risk is that execution profits go private while crisis liability stays public.

What to watch

  • pending · Whether SWIFT's 17-bank blockchain ledger pilots move into live production, and where settlement actually happens.
  • pending · The Eurosystem's planned blockchain platform with native tokenized central-bank money and longer operating hours.
  • pending · The first serious failure in one of these systems, which will show whether old authorities kept real power or only the liability.
