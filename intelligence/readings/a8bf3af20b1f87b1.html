Reserve Bank presidents: appointment, removal and FOMC power

Question: What is the constitutional and statutory status of Reserve Bank presidents when they vote on the FOMC, who appoints and removes them, and what remains legally unresolved?
Bottom line
Reserve Bank presidents are not nominated by the President or confirmed by the Senate. Class B and Class C directors of each Reserve Bank select the Bank’s president and first vice president for five-year terms, subject to approval by the Board of Governors. The boards of the Banks in each statutory regional group then select annually which eligible president or first vice president will occupy that group’s FOMC seat; New York has its own statutory group and therefore supplies the continuously represented Reserve Bank seat.
The best current federal executive-branch legal position is DOJ’s 2019 Office of Legal Counsel opinion:
a Reserve Bank representative voting on the FOMC exercises significant federal sovereign authority and occupies a continuing statutory position;
the representative is therefore an Officer of the United States;
the officer is inferior, rather than principal, because the Board of Governors holds a majority on the FOMC and possesses supervision and removal authority;
the Board’s approval of the underlying appointment as Reserve Bank president satisfies the Appointments Clause because the Board collectively acts as the head of a department; and
the later FOMC assignment does not require a second constitutional appointment because FOMC service is germane to the office for which the Board already approved the president.
That is a powerful and carefully reasoned official position. It is not a controlling Supreme Court holding. The only merits decision directly addressing the historical arrangement, the 1986 district-court opinion in Melcher, used a different theory and said the Bank representatives were not federal officers. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the judgment on equitable-discretion grounds rather than adopting the merits analysis. Modern Supreme Court officer cases and DOJ’s later opinions have overtaken much of Melcher’s conceptual framework, but no modern appellate merits judgment has applied them directly to the FOMC.
The precise ruling is therefore:
The present appointment structure has a strong constitutional defense, but the defense rests principally on DOJ’s inferior-officer, Board-approval and germane-duty analysis—not on a definitive modern judicial holding sustaining Reserve Bank presidents’ FOMC role.

Dodd–Frank’s conflict correction
Before Dodd–Frank, the full Reserve Bank board—including Class A member-bank directors—participated in selecting the president. Congress removed Class A directors from that function because the president leads an institution involved in supervising banks. Board policy also restricts a Class B director affiliated with a supervised thrift holding company. This was a structural conflict-of-interest correction, not conversion to presidential nomination or Senate confirmation.
The Board’s current public process adds a search committee of eligible directors, an external search firm, continuing consultation with the Board’s Committee on Federal Reserve Bank Affairs, finalist interviews by Governors, a formal Bank-board appointment and final Board approval.

3. Why the OLC appointment theory works
Officer status
An FOMC voter exercises legal power that is more than advice. The Committee directs the Reserve Banks’ open-market operations; every voting member participates in that binding federal decision. The position is continuing even though occupants of four group seats rotate annually. OLC therefore concludes that each Bank representative is an Officer of the United States while serving in that role.
This is a stronger modern characterization than Melcher’s private-person theory. The 2025 OLC officer-status opinion retains the same two-part test: a continuing federal position plus significant statutory authority.
Inferior rather than principal
OLC relies on structural supervision:
the seven Senate-confirmed Governors form a majority of the twelve-member FOMC;
the Board approves the underlying president appointment;
the Board supervises the Reserve Banks; and
the Board can remove the president from the Bank office, which necessarily ends eligibility to sit on the FOMC.
Arthrex creates the right modern stress test: can the officer make a final executive decision that no superior may review? A single Bank president cannot issue FOMC policy alone; the decision is the committee’s, and principal officers hold its majority. That supports—without judicially proving—the inferior-officer classification.
Board approval as constitutional appointment
The six eligible local directors make the initial choice, but the choice has no legal effect without Board approval. OLC analogizes this to appointments in which a subordinate identifies the candidate and the department head approves. It treats the multi-member Board collectively as the head of the Federal Reserve System for Appointments Clause purposes.
Germane additional duty
The Bank boards, not the Board of Governors, identify which eligible president fills each group’s FOMC seat. Those Bank boards cannot constitutionally appoint an Officer of the United States. OLC solves this by treating FOMC service as an additional duty germane to an office already constitutionally filled through Board approval. Because every president and first vice president is statutorily eligible and the monetary-policy role has long been part of the office’s design, no second appointment is required.
This is the most contestable link in the chain, but it is materially stronger than saying private boards directly appoint federal monetary officers.
4. Removal is a two-route system
Board of Governors route — 12 U.S.C. §248(f)
The Board may “suspend or remove any officer or director” of a Reserve Bank and must communicate “the cause” of the removal in writing to the person and the Bank.
That wording must not be collapsed into the Governor-protection language of 12 U.S.C. §242. Section 242 says Governors may be removed “for cause.” Section 248(f) grants the Bank-officer removal power and requires a written cause statement. OLC reads §248(f) as plenary, at-will removal authority, disagreeing with Melcher’s suggestion that it is substantively cause-limited.
The safest current statement is:
The Board must state a cause in writing under §248(f); DOJ OLC interprets the power as plenary rather than protected for-cause tenure. No modern controlling court has finally resolved that interpretive question for a Reserve Bank president.
Local Reserve Bank board route — 12 U.S.C. §341
Section 341 authorizes a Bank’s board to dismiss its officers and employees “at pleasure.” Read literally, that permits local dismissal without Board action.
OLC concludes that when the president is an FOMC officer, the local board may constitutionally exercise that power only with the Board of Governors’ approval. Otherwise, a body that is not constitutionally authorized to appoint federal officers could defeat the federal appointing authority’s supervision and removal control. This is a constitutional saving construction supplied by OLC; the approval condition is not stated expressly in §341’s dismissal clause.
Removal from the Bank presidency automatically ends FOMC eligibility because §263 requires a Reserve Bank representative to be a president or first vice president.
5. Selection control in practice
The September 1, 2026 Board–CFPB OIG report supplies the newest operating evidence. It found that:
the Board’s president and first-vice-president approval process contains key controls;
written guidance does not require Banks to identify or report potential conflicts between candidates and external search firms;
roles and responsibilities for some consultations are not clearly defined;
Class C eligibility and family-financial-interest controls can be strengthened; and
the Board issued or accepted ten recommendations across the reviewed leadership-selection processes.
These are governance-control defects, not proof that an appointment was invalid or corrupted. They matter because the OLC constitutional defense depends in part on meaningful Board approval and supervision, not ceremonial ratification.
6. What remains unresolved
No modern appellate merits holding. The D.C. Circuit’s Melcher judgment did not decide the merits, and no later Supreme Court case has tested the present structure under Lucia and Arthrex.
Never-voting presidents. OLC expressly did not decide whether a president who never serves on the FOMC is an Officer of the United States.
Non-FOMC functions. OLC did not decide the constitutional status of a president’s supervision, discount-window, account-access, payment-service, procurement or corporate-management acts.
Class A and B directors. OLC expressly did not decide the constitutionality of the member-bank election method for those directors.
Removal procedure. Public sources do not supply a complete modern process for a Board §248(f) action: investigation, notice, response, evidentiary standard, Board vote, judicial review or interim suspension.
Local-board dismissal approval. The Board-approval condition comes from OLC’s constitutional construction; public Bank bylaws and Board delegations should be tested for implementation.
Actual use. A complete historical ledger of Board suspensions or removals of Reserve Bank officers under §248(f) has not yet been recovered.
FOMC representative election records. Public sources state the statutory grouping and resulting membership, but the complete annual board resolutions and procedures are not gathered in one place.

8. Non-claims and falsifiers
A Bank president is not privately sovereign merely because the President and Senate do not select the person.
Board approval is not Senate confirmation, but it may constitute a valid inferior-officer appointment by a department head.
The five Reserve Bank seats do not control the twelve-member FOMC as a voting majority.
The seven Governors’ majority does not erase the constitutional need to appoint every voting officer properly.
Cook’s Governor-removal protection does not automatically protect Bank presidents.
OLC’s conclusion is not a court judgment.
The campaign’s conclusion would materially change if a controlling court rejected the Board-as-department-head theory, the germane-duty theory, the inferior-officer classification or the saving construction applied to local-board removal.

Wave 1 ruling
Congress did not give five private persons an unchecked monetary-policy franchise. It built a mixed selection route: regional directors identify and elect from Board-approved Bank presidents, while the Senate-confirmed Board majority, federal statutory committee, Board supervision and removal power supply the national control chain. The constitutional defense is substantial—but because its modern form comes from OLC rather than a controlling merits judgment, the seam remains a legitimate subject of congressional redesign or future litigation.
