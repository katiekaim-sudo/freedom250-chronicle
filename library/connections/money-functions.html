The U.S. dollar issuance constitution — who gave the Fed its role and how it can change

Current saved study

The Fed's role is a statutory operating arrangement created by Congress, not a private agreement or surrender of sovereignty. Issuance is already divided: Congress defines the system; Treasury mints coins and prints notes; the Board orders notes; Reserve Banks place notes and issue reserve balances; banks issue deposit claims. Congress can amend, restrict, move or dissolve by ordinary legislation; an executive order cannot. A retail Fed CBDC is barred through 2030 by FRA §16A.

How Congress changed the Fed and defined the dollar

Current saved study

Congress has repeatedly changed the Fed’s powers, governance and payment services. Federal law defines the dollar as a unit for stating amounts. A private dollar claim depends on its issuer’s promise to redeem it; it is not itself a Federal Reserve note.

Federal Reserve purse, audit and stopping-power map

Current saved study

The Fed is heavily but not universally audited, and most auditors cannot stop an operation. Stopping power sits with Congress (statute), the full Board (budgets, examination, §248(f)/(h)), each Bank board, the FOMC (open-market domain), Treasury (§13(3) prior approval) and courts; GAO, OIG, outside and general auditors produce evidence another actor must act on. 'Audit access alone is not control.'
