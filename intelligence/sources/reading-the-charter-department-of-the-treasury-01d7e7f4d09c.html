Reading the Charter — Department of the Treasury

The 10-point hunt

1. Immunities & liability shields. Nothing in the 1789 act or in current §301/§321 grants Treasury itself sovereign-immunity-style protection beyond ordinary federal-agency status — Treasury doesn't need a special immunity clause because it is the sovereign's purse. The interesting immunity work happens downstream, in the entities Treasury charters (Fed, ESF, Mint) — see their own charter notes.

2. Tax exemptions. None in the 1789 act. Current 31 U.S.C. §321(d)(2) does one narrow thing: gifts and bequests accepted by the Secretary "shall be considered as a gift or bequest to or for the use of the United States" for federal income, estate, and gift tax purposes — i.e., donors get the tax treatment of giving straight to Uncle Sam.

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause. 1789 Act §1: Secretary, Comptroller, Auditor, Treasurer, Register — all created as offices, appointment left to the general Appointments Clause (Senate-confirmed, since these were "principal officers" from day one). §7 is the tell: "whenever the Secretary shall be removed from office by the President" — casually assumes at-will removal by the President with zero for-cause language. That's the founding-era default: Treasury's Secretary serves entirely at the President's pleasure. Current §301(b)-(f): every top Treasury officer (Secretary, Deputy Secretary, Under Secretaries, Assistant Secretaries, General Counsel) is presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed, no removal protection anywhere in the text. Compare this to the Comptroller of the Currency (12 U.S.C. §2), who — despite sitting inside Treasury's building — gets a five-year term and "unless sooner removed by the President, upon reasons to be communicated by him to the Senate": a for-cause-adjacent hurdle the Secretary himself never gets. §321(c)(2) makes that carve-out explicit: the Comptroller's duties and powers are the one thing NOT vested in the Secretary.

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations. 1789 Act: silent — pure appropriations creature, warrants "issued from the Treasury... in pursuance of appropriations by law" (§2). Nothing has changed at the parent-department level: the Secretary's own office runs on congressional appropriations like any Cabinet department. The self-funding action is all in Treasury's bureaus (Mint's Public Enterprise Fund, OCC's bank assessments, the ESF) — Treasury-the-department itself has no seigniorage of its own; it's the bureaus underneath it that print money or tax the regulated.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs. §321(d)(4): the one disclosure duty in this material — annual public accounting of gifts/bequests received and how spent. Otherwise ordinary Cabinet-department GAO/appropriations oversight applies; no FOIA carve-out appears in this text.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses. N/A — Treasury is a unit of the U.S. government, not a treaty organization; Congress amends by ordinary legislation (as the 1982 Title 31 recodification and dozens of subsequent Pub. L. amendments to §301 show).

7. Emergency powers. None in the organic material captured here. Treasury's emergency toolkit lives almost entirely in downstream statutes (IEEPA, Trading with the Enemy Act, the Gold Reserve Act, the ESF) rather than in the base organic act.

8. Definitions section quietly load-bearing. None in 1 Stat. 65 — it's an 18th-century five-office org chart, no definitions article at all. In current law, §321(a)(4) is the quiet one: it folds "mint coins, engrave and print currency and security documents, and refine and assay bullion" into the Secretary's general authority, meaning the Mint and the Bureau of Engraving and Printing are legally framed as extensions of the Secretary's own hand, not independent grants of power.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution. Not applicable to a Cabinet department; it does not "dissolve," only reorganize by statute (Reorganization Plans referenced throughout the §301 notes — 1940, 1950 — show how much of Treasury's internal shape was rearranged by executive reorg plans rather than fresh legislation).

10. Anything strange. Section 8 of the 1789 act is the strange one: it bars every Treasury officer from trading, owning ships, dealing in public lands, or speculating in public securities — on pain of forfeiting $3,000 and permanent disqualification from federal office — and then pays a bounty of half that penalty to whoever informs on them. The Founders built a bounty-hunter anti-corruption clause into the very first Treasury statute, aimed squarely at insider trading in the public debt they knew Hamilton's whole financial system depended on.

Layer map

  • 1789 — Act to Establish the Treasury Department (1 Stat. 65): the five-office skeleton (Secretary, Comptroller, Auditor, Treasurer, Register) plus the anti-corruption bounty clause.
  • 1817, 1836, 1849-1913 — piecemeal officer/bureau acts (not captured here) add Assistant Secretaries, the Comptroller of the Currency (1863-64, see OCC note), the Bureau of Internal Revenue, etc. — Treasury accretes bureaus rather than being re-chartered.
  • 1950 — Reorganization Plan No. 26: shuffles internal Treasury functions to the Secretary by executive order authority, referenced repeatedly in the §301/§321 revision notes.
  • 1982 — Pub. L. 97-258 (Title 31 positive-law recodification): re-enacts the whole apparatus as current §301/§321 et seq. — a restatement, not a substantive re-founding.
  • 2010 — Dodd-Frank (Pub. L. 111-203): carves the Comptroller of the Currency's independence language into §321(c)(2) explicitly, and adds the Federal Insurance Office function to §301(a)(9).

Entity Theory read

Treasury is the government's own wallet given a face — it has no independence to speak of because it isn't supposed to have any: every top officer serves at the President's pleasure, and the money is Congress's money, spent under Congress's appropriations. The leash runs straight from the President through the Secretary with no slack anywhere in this text. But Treasury is also a shell that houses genuinely independent creatures inside it — the Comptroller of the Currency most obviously, with his own term and his own removal standard, deliberately fenced off in §321(c)(2) from the Secretary's otherwise-total authority. Whoever controls the presidency controls Treasury outright; whoever controls Treasury does NOT automatically control everything wearing a Treasury Department badge.
