# Trump 2028: Constitutional Entity and Argument Map

Evidence cutoff: **2026-07-25 EDT**

## Executive finding

The best constructed “Miller-style” argument is:

> The Twenty-Second Amendment prohibits a person from being **elected**
> President more than twice. It does not prohibit that person from announcing,
> raising money, registering under campaign-finance law, competing for a party
> nomination or necessarily appearing on a state ballot. Eligibility should
> therefore be decided only at a later, nationally uniform federal checkpoint.

This could be an effective **delay and pressure theory**. It is not a persuasive
merits theory for a direct third presidential election. On the official
constitutional ledger, Trump was elected in 2016 and 2024. The Amendment's
operative text therefore applies without deciding anything about 2020.

The more plausible strategic use of the July 24 speech is to keep three things
attached:

1. Trump's personal claim that he won in 2020;
2. a future Trump candidacy or succession ambiguity; and
3. the administration's active effort to change the legal and operational
   control of the 2026 and 2028 elections.

This is a constructed steelman, not an attribution to Stephen Miller or any
identified government lawyer.

## Four objects that must not be collapsed

| Object | What would establish it | Twenty-Second Amendment effect |
|---|---|---|
| Election vulnerabilities, improper registrations or fraud existed | Investigations, person-level adjudications, prosecutions, audits and concrete court findings | None by itself |
| Trump received the most lawful popular votes in enough states in 2020 | State-by-state evidence and legally available remedies | Does not by itself replace the electors whose votes Congress counted |
| Trump was constitutionally **elected President** in 2020 | The Electoral College and congressional counting process, or an unprecedented valid displacement of that completed process | Would make 2020 an additional Trump election; it would not open 2028 |
| Trump may be directly elected in 2028 | Present Twenty-Second Amendment or a ratified amendment | Present law says no because 2016 and 2024 already count |

No presently identified actor—NARA, the FEC, DOJ, an intelligence agency, a
single court or Congress by ordinary resolution—has a statutory mechanism
simply to reopen the completed 2020 count and retroactively substitute a
different President.

| Ledger | 2016 | 2020 | 2024 | 2028 consequence under current law |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Official constitutional ledger | Trump elected | Biden elected | Trump elected | Trump has been elected twice; direct third election barred |
| Trump narrative ledger | Trump won | Trump won | Trump won | Political “fourth run”; if each “win” were a legal election, the bar would be stronger, not weaker |
| H.J.Res.29 counterfactual ledger | Trump elected | Biden elected | Trump elected | If the proposal were ratified first, a third, nonconsecutive Trump election could be allowed |

This produces the sharpest irony in the theory. H.J.Res.29's benefit to Trump
depends on preserving the official proposition that Biden—not Trump—was elected
in 2020. If Trump were treated as elected in 2016, 2020 and 2024, he would
already reach the proposal's three-election ceiling; 2016 and 2020 would also
be consecutive.

## Current legal rule

The Twenty-Second Amendment says:

> “No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice”

It does not impose a two-**consecutive**-term limit. The official federal
electoral records show Trump elected in 2016 and 2024 and Biden elected in 2020.

Congress considered and rejected broader drafting language covering being
“chosen,” serving or eligibility to hold office. That history creates a real,
unresolved succession question. It does not create ambiguity about the express
bar on a direct third election.

## Strongest hypothetical procedural theory

### Step 1 — separate candidacy from election

Trump announces, raises money and files under the Federal Election Campaign
Act. The FEC records campaign-finance status; it does not adjudicate
constitutional eligibility or grant ballot access.

The FEC has expressly treated candidate status as separate from ballot
eligibility. Its Office of General Counsel has also said the Commission lacks
authority to reject a Statement of Candidacy on constitutional-disqualification
grounds.

Authorities:

- [FEC Advisory Opinion 2011-15](https://www.fec.gov/files/legal/aos/2011-15/AO-2011-15.pdf)
- [FEC MUR 8168 legal analysis](https://www.fec.gov/files/legal/murs/8168/8168_12.pdf)
- [FEC candidate-registration rules](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/registering-candidate/)

### Step 2 — make ballot eligibility contestable

The argument would stress that:

- November voters formally appoint electors;
- the Electoral College later elects the President;
- the Twenty-Second Amendment names the later event, not ballot appearance; and
- a state-by-state exclusion patchwork would undermine national uniformity.

It would analogize the national-uniformity reasoning of *Trump v. Anderson*.
That analogy is limited. *Anderson* concerned enforcement of Fourteenth
Amendment Section 3, including that provision's distinct structure and
congressional enforcement role. It did not hold that states can never apply
ordinary presidential qualifications.

The adverse authorities are *Hassan v. Colorado* and *Lindsay v. Bowen*, which
allowed states to exclude plainly constitutionally ineligible presidential
candidates. Neither decided the Twenty-Second Amendment, leaving the exact
enforcement timing open.

Authorities:

- [*Trump v. Anderson* constitutional summary and opinion link](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt14-S3-2/ALDE_00000070/)
- [*Hassan v. Colorado*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca10/12-1190/12-1190-2012-09-04.html)
- [*Lindsay v. Bowen*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/13-15085/13-15085-2014-05-06.html)

### Step 3 — push the checkpoint to electors and Congress

If ballot inclusion produces a political mandate, the theory would say the
electors possess the constitutional choosing power and Congress has the
statutory counting role.

Current 3 U.S.C. §15 permits an objection only when:

1. electors were not lawfully certified; or
2. one or more electoral votes were not “regularly given.”

One-fifth of each chamber must sign an objection; separate majorities of both
chambers must sustain it. The Vice President's role is ministerial.

The phrase “regularly given” is not defined in the statute. A conventional
reading could include a vote that directly violates the Twenty-Second
Amendment. The constructed counterargument would insist Congress deliberately
omitted an express eligibility ground.

That omission has a useful legislative-history hook. The House-passed
Presidential Election Reform Act, H.R.8873 (2022), would expressly have allowed
an objection when a vote was cast for a candidate ineligible under the
Twenty-Second Amendment and supplied a Twentieth Amendment failure-to-qualify
path. That exact text did not become final law. The enacted Electoral Count
Reform Act instead uses the two generic grounds above.

This is a plausible statutory argument about the **form of an objection**. It
does not empower Congress to override the Constitution by counting a prohibited
third election.

The bipartisan Senate floor explanation of the enacted reform stated that
“regularly given” can reach an elector's vote for a constitutionally ineligible
President or Vice President. That is relevant legislative history, not express
statutory text, and it weakens any claim that the omission silently authorizes
such a vote.

### Step 4 — argue institutional settlement after the count

If Congress counted the votes and declared Trump elected, the argument would
invoke finality, political-question concerns, voter reliance and institutional
settlement. It might then claim the Twentieth Amendment's “failed to qualify”
rule does not apply because the Twenty-Second Amendment is worded as an election
bar rather than an eligibility-to-hold-office rule.

This is the most aggressive step. A completed congressional count cannot
plausibly repeal the Twenty-Second Amendment. The Twentieth Amendment says the
Vice President-elect acts if a President-elect fails to qualify, but it gives
no obvious cure for a permanent constitutional bar.

Authority: [Twentieth Amendment, Section 3](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/amendment-20/section-3/).

## H.J.Res.29: actual legal route, presently inert

Representative Andy Ogles introduced H.J.Res.29 on January 23, 2025. It would
permit no more than three presidential elections and forbid an additional
election after two consecutive terms. As of the evidence cutoff it remained
referred to House Judiciary, with zero cosponsors, no Senate companion and no
legal force.

Its operative route is Article V:

1. two-thirds of the House;
2. two-thirds of the Senate; and
3. ratification by three-fourths of state legislatures within the proposed
   seven-year period.

The President has no formal approval or veto role in that process. The proposal
contains no transition clause explaining how disputed or prior elections would
be treated. If Congress does not act, the proposal dies when the 119th Congress
ends at noon on January 3, 2027 and would have to be reintroduced. If Congress
submits it to the states, its own seven-year ratification clock begins. NARA and
the Archivist administer ratification certification; they do not decide the
proposal's merits.

## Ranked conclusions

1. **High confidence:** current law bars Trump's direct election as President in
   2028 because the official ledger already records two Trump elections.
2. **High confidence:** proof of fraud or vulnerability in 2020 would not by
   itself retroactively replace the constitutional electoral result.
3. **High confidence:** a Form 2 or campaign announcement would create a
   campaign-finance object, not eligibility.
4. **High confidence:** H.J.Res.29 has no current legal effect and creates a
   two-ledger paradox rather than a 2020 forced choice.
5. **Medium-high confidence:** some pre-election enforcement questions—standing,
   timing, state authority and the reach of *Trump v. Anderson*—are genuinely
   open because no court has decided this exact Twenty-Second Amendment case.
6. **Medium confidence:** the strongest constructed strategy is to postpone each
   constitutional checkpoint and turn adverse rulings into political evidence
   of institutional refusal.
7. **Medium-high confidence:** the speech is more tightly connected to the
   active 2026 election-control program than to any presently executable 2028
   legal mechanism.
8. **Not established:** Stephen Miller or any administration lawyer has adopted
   this theory.
