How did the Financial Stability Board's post-crisis bank, resolution, clearing and non-bank programme become the international coordination spine for crypto, stablecoins, tokenisation, operational technology and implementation monitoring?

The FSB ties Basel, market infrastructure and digital finance together

The FSB has spent fifteen years joining bank capital, failure, clearing, non-bank risk and implementation; crypto and tokenisation are the newest layer of that same system, not a separate global blockchain plan.

What we found

  • The FSB was built after the 2008 crisis to coordinate ministries, central banks, supervisors and standard setters. It does not make binding law; countries still have to enact and enforce the standards.
  • The Basel Committee's September 30 dashboard says three quarters of member jurisdictions published all final Basel III rules, but only 47% had all six final standards in force. The United States still had draft rules for all five applicable standards.
  • The 2023 turmoil showed both sides of the system: implemented Basel reforms helped contain the damage, while weak governance, delayed supervisory action and untested contingency funding still let banks fail quickly.
  • The October 2026 backstop review found only about one in five jurisdictions fully complied with the resolution-liquidity standard and about half had material gaps. TLAC can absorb losses; it does not supply every dollar of cash needed during resolution.
  • Bank crypto exposures now sit inside Basel capital, leverage, liquidity, accounting and disclosure standards. That international January 2026 date still needs separate national implementation.
  • Derivatives reform did not just move risk away from banks: it concentrated critical functions in CCPs. The control stack now runs from PFMI resilience through FMI recovery to public resolution, with a seven-tool resolution-resource standard and 14 cross-border critical CCPs.
  • The January 2026 PFMI tracker shows broad legal adoption, but that is not proof of resilience. A separate 2025 outcome review found six serious general-business-risk gaps in a voluntary sample of 34 FMIs across 27 jurisdictions.
  • LEI, UTI, UPI and critical data elements identify different parts of a derivatives record. Together they improve joining and aggregation; they do not prove ownership, data quality, payment or settlement finality.
  • Risk did not simply leave banks after 2008. Nonbanks held 51% of measured global financial assets at end-2024, while the narrower slice with bank-like run, leverage or funding vulnerabilities reached $76.3 trillion.
  • March 2020 exposed the joined stress mechanism: fund redemptions, Treasury sales and margin calls raised cash demand as dealer capacity fell. Central-bank intervention stopped a worse outcome, so policy now targets liquidity spikes and market-wide amplifiers rather than only entity failure.
  • The 2025 leverage framework has nine recommendations but no single global cap. It asks each authority to see leverage, act in core markets, improve bank and nonbank counterparty data, close regulatory gaps and coordinate across borders.
  • The 2026 repo report maps about $16 trillion of government-bond-backed repo; roughly 70% of the non-centrally-cleared segment had zero haircuts, though portfolio margin and intragroup trades make that a signal, not an automatic violation.
  • Private credit is estimated at $1.5-2 trillion and linked to banks, insurers, pensions and private equity. The FSB calls it untested at current scale in a prolonged severe downturn; that is a vulnerability finding, not a crisis finding.
  • The operational constitution now runs from response and recovery through incident reporting, third-party oversight and tested exit. Outsourcing a service never transfers the regulated institution's ultimate accountability.
  • FIRE is a final common incident-report format with a data model, XBRL example and validation rules. It is not a global reporting platform or legal duty; each authority still has to adopt scope, triggers, deadlines and confidentiality rules.
  • The 2023 third-party toolkit looks past the named vendor to critical services, contracts, registers, subcontractors, continuity, substitution and sector-wide concentration. It does not itself designate a cloud, oracle, wallet or AI provider as systemic.
  • The 2024 AI report identifies provider concentration, correlated behaviour, cyber risk and model/data/governance as systemic channels. The 2025 monitor found definitions and data collection still inconsistent, so an AI inventory is not safety certification.
  • The June 2026 AI consultation proposes twelve governance and lifecycle practices, including meaningful human control, kill switches, cyber exercises and third-party controls. The FSB says final practices were delivered October 12, but the final standalone text was not public at the cutoff.
  • The FSB already has three different implementation machines: CFIM allocates the monitoring owner, peer reviews inspect selected adoption and gaps, and the 2017 effects framework asks whether a sufficiently implemented reform achieved its objective without unacceptable side effects.
  • The 2025 interim review did not perform new country audits. It assembled existing evidence and found the same pattern across the program: strong design, uneven national delivery, and too little proof of operation or effect for newer reforms.
  • Modernisation is a meta-reform, not one new rail or rule. Its test is whether policy becomes clearer, faster and more proportionate while retaining crisis resilience—and whether a named later workstream actually uses the new process.
  • An indexed modernisation page carried an embargo after the October 12 cutoff, so its details were excluded. The admissible public record remains the Chair's summary and the June symposium until a valid public release.
  • On October 12, 2026 the FSB said it delivered a regulatory and supervisory modernisation consultation to the G20. It describes effective, efficient, risk-focused and forward-looking principles, but the full public text was not yet located and the final report is planned for 2027.

Why it matters

It supplies the missing international spine behind our institution-by-institution money map: the old post-crisis system is not being discarded; its capital, clearing, data and failure rules are being extended to new digital forms and then reviewed for how they should modernise.

What to watch

  • first public release after 2026-10-12 · The FSB consultation report on regulatory and supervisory modernisation and the 2026 Implementation Monitoring Review report.
  • 2027 · Final modernisation principles and any change to FSB policy design, consultation, cost-benefit analysis, proportionality or implementation monitoring.
  • end-2027 · Actual cross-border-payment KPIs against the eleven G20 targets and jurisdictional action plans.
  • 2026-11 through 2027-04 · The 2026 G-SIB list and the first final/effective receipts for the remaining U.S., UK, EU, India, Brazil and other Basel III clocks.
  • next FSB OTC-derivatives table, SI>1 CCP list or annual resolution report · Updated jurisdiction counts, additions or removals from the 14-CCP list, completed CMG/cooperation clocks and national implementation of the 2024 CCP resolution-resource standard.
  • 2026-2027 CPMI-IOSCO and FSB releases · Final general-business-risk guidance, the promised CSD resolution-planning stocktake and evidence that FMIs changed recovery, wind-down, equity or liquidity arrangements.
  • end-2026 · MMF effectiveness assessment, OEF implementation stocktake and any national implementation report for margin or the nine NBFI leverage recommendations.
  • first public Nonbank Data Task Force test-case report · Proof of better coverage or cross-border sharing for leveraged sovereign-bond strategies, kept separate from the February 2026 aggregate repo map.
  • next FSB private-credit or repo follow-up · Common definitions, fund/loan-level data, insurer and private-equity links, valuation and rating controls, redemption-bearing vehicles, zero-haircut context and actual policy changes.
  • first public final AI sound-practices text · Diff all twelve practices, agentic-AI controls, definitions and case studies against the June consultation; do not treat the delivery statement as the final text.
  • first valid public modernisation consultation and 2026 Implementation Monitoring Review · Extract every principle, definition, recommendation, consultation question, owner and clock; exclude embargoed or staged text before release.
  • first named use of a modernised FSB process · Prove a changed design, consultation, prioritisation, monitoring or effects method and test whether implementation or resilience improved.
  • first public 2026 cross-border-payments KPI report and end-2027 targets · Separate completed policy milestones and jurisdictional action plans from measured end-user cost, speed, access and transparency.
  • 2027 FIRE implementation workshop or earlier national adoption · Legal scope, triggers, deadlines, mandatory fields, confidentiality, third-party coverage and actual FIRE-compatible reports.
  • first material common-provider or digital-rail disruption · Map cloud, model, wallet, key, node, bridge, oracle or shared-ledger dependencies through detection, reporting, recovery, substitution, customer harm and loss.
