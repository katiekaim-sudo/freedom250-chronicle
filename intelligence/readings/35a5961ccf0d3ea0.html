1. Ruling
Secretary Scott Bessent did not publicly claim on August 7 that Treasury had switched off every Iranian bank or Iran's domestic payment system. He said Iran's shadow banking system was “buckling” and that Treasury would continue cutting facilitators off from the U.S. financial system.
The day's two Treasury actions make the object clearer:
OFAC targeted a rahbar-managed bank/foreign-exchange network that Treasury says used exchange houses, foreign bank accounts, shell companies, false invoices and currency conversion to move hundreds of millions of dollars for sanctioned Iranian banks.
OFAC targeted crypto exchanges, operators and wallet addresses that Treasury says supplied covert international-finance access and laundered funds for the regime and IRGC.
Together, the actions attack the adapters between Iranian state revenue and spendable international money. They do not themselves prove that a customer's rial deposit disappeared, a domestic card authorization failed, an Iranian ACH or RTGS system stopped, or the Central Bank of Iran could no longer create rial liquidity.

3. What the August 7 bank/FX action actually does
The bank/FX release identifies a rahbar system around Shahr Bank and the FSAQ group. Treasury describes rahbars as private managers of offshore shells and bank accounts used by sanctioned Iranian banks that cannot openly access the international system.
The reported mechanism is:
Iranian bank or state beneficiary → rahbar/exchange house → shell or front company → foreign bank account → trade invoice or currency conversion → hard-currency or repatriated value
The August 7 designations change the legal and risk state of named people and entities:
U.S.-jurisdiction property and interests in property are blocked.
U.S. persons generally may not transact with them absent a license or exemption.
entities owned 50 percent or more by blocked persons are also blocked even if not separately listed.
foreign financial institutions that knowingly facilitate significant transactions risk secondary-sanctions consequences, including U.S. correspondent/payable-through restrictions.
The designations also produce screening and de-risking effects beyond the exact legal perimeter. Those effects are probable mechanisms, but they must be established institution by institution before they are stated as completed account closures or payment rejections.
4. What the August 7 crypto action adds
The crypto action targets Shelbit, Aban Tether, named operators, linked companies and digital-currency addresses. It extends the same access campaign into private custody and public-chain infrastructure.
The legal act and technical execution are separate:
OFAC designation → sanctions screening → exchange/issuer/custodian match → account or address restriction → attempted-transfer result → any seizure/forfeiture → customer remedy
Only the first step is automatic from the public designation record. An address on the SDN record does not itself prove that a token issuer blacklisted it, an exchange froze it, an authority took control of the keys, assets were seized, or title was forfeited.
5. The domestic system that remains a separate question
The World Bank's 2016 architecture map identified distinct Iranian systems including:
Shetab — electronic/card network connectivity;
Shaparak — card-payment network and settlement governance;
PAYA — automated clearing for smaller-value transfers;
SATNA — real-time gross settlement for large-value transfers;
SEPAM — interbank messaging/transactions;
SAHAB — smaller-value wire transfers; and
other clearing, check-imaging and securities components.
That is a historical architecture baseline, not proof of current availability or finality rules. Treasury's February 14, 2024 action identifies CBI's Informatics Services Corporation as overseeing Shetab and being affiliated with Shaparak; designation of the operator still does not technically turn those domestic systems off.
The strongest current negative control is Treasury's own August 7 crypto release. In describing a targeted illicit network, Treasury says gambling sites retained access to Iran's online payment systems regulated by CBI. That statement is inconsistent with treating the day's action as proof that all domestic payments had already stopped.
6. SWIFT: important, but not the whole banking system
SWIFT carries standardized financial messages. It does not hold customer deposits, debit and credit the underlying accounts, supply settlement liquidity or create final settlement by itself.
The legal history is list- and jurisdiction-specific:
In March 2012, EU law required SWIFT to disconnect EU-sanctioned Iranian institutions.
Many Iranian institutions reconnected in January 2016 after sanctions relief.
In November 2018, SWIFT suspended certain Iranian banks.
In September 2025, the EU reimposed nuclear-related measures, including a prohibition on specialized financial messaging services for listed entities.
No post-snapback operator notice establishing the exact 2025–26 customer-disconnection set was recovered in this pass. No August 7, 2026 announcement established a new blanket disconnection of all Iranian banks.
7. The joined mechanism
The campaign is best understood as a layered choke around external convertibility:
Block the U.S.-linked property and transactions of Iranian financial institutions and designated facilitators.
Deny direct and indirect use of U.S. correspondent accounts and dollar clearing.
Threaten foreign banks with loss or restriction of U.S. correspondent access.
Remove or constrain listed institutions' access to specialized financial messaging under EU law and operator implementation.
Expose foreign accounts, shells, invoices, exchange houses and offshore banks that convert or repatriate oil and trade proceeds.
Target crypto exchanges, custodians, operators and wallet addresses used as alternative exits.
Raise delay, compliance cost, counterparty risk, trapped-balance risk and the price of opacity.
This can severely impair Iran's hard-currency position and international payment reach while a domestic rial system keeps circulating. The pressure is real; the system state must still be named precisely.

9. Current findings
Established
Treasury carried out two separate August 7 OFAC actions against Iran-linked bank/FX and crypto networks.
The official object is Iran's shadow banking and external-finance apparatus, not a declared domestic-bank shutdown.
U.S. legal blocking, U.S.-person transaction prohibitions and secondary-sanctions exposure are operative for the relevant persons and conduct.
Iran has historically operated distinct domestic card, ACH, RTGS and interbank-messaging systems.
SWIFT messaging and movement/finality of money are separate functions.
Attributed official characterization
Treasury says the designated networks moved or laundered hundreds of millions, supplied covert international-finance access and kept sanctioned banks and the regime funded.
Bessent says the shadow system is buckling and Iran is running out of ways to move money.
These are important first-party statements about Treasury's action and assessment. They are not independent proof of the asserted totals, causal effect or systemwide impairment.
Mechanism inference
The combined bank/FX/crypto actions are designed to collapse substitution between routes. When one foreign account, shell, currency converter or exchange is exposed, the network must replace the legal entity, bank relationship, invoice, custodian and sometimes the settlement currency. Repeated exposure can increase friction faster than replacements can be opened.
Not established
that every Iranian bank lost SWIFT access;
that a new blanket SWIFT action occurred on August 7;
that Shetab, Shaparak, PAYA, SATNA, SEPAM or SAHAB stopped;
that named foreign correspondents closed or rejected particular transactions because of the August 7 action;
that listed crypto assets were actually frozen, seized or forfeited;
that a named major Iranian bank became insolvent or entered resolution; or
that Iran lost all hard-currency or cross-border-payment capacity.
10. Return gate
Do not let this become an unlimited Iran-payments landscape. Return to the original question whenever one of these records appears:
a SWIFT or EU/Belgian operator notice naming the disconnected institutions and effective time;
a U.S. order imposing correspondent/payable-through conditions on a named foreign bank;
a bank or regulator record identifying a closed correspondent, rejected payment or trapped balance;
a CBI/operator record of a Shetab/Shaparak/PAYA/SATNA/SEPAM outage or finality change;
an issuer/exchange/custodian record proving an address or account freeze and amount;
a seizure or forfeiture instrument changing custody or title; or
audited or regulatory evidence of bank illiquidity, insolvency, merger, resolution or depositor restriction.
Until one fires, the controlling description remains: a tightening external financial choke, not a proven domestic banking shutdown.
