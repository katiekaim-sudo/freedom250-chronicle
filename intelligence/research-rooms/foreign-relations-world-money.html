The dollar system is being reworked, not replaced

Countries are spreading reserves, settling some trade in local currencies and testing stablecoins, but these are different moves from actually replacing a currency. The study's deep look at Japan finds its money system being rebuilt through new links and rules, with no sign of replacement or a current crisis.

What we found

  • The Bank of Japan decided on September 18, 2026 to raise its overnight target and deposit rate to 1.25 percent, effective September 24. A September 10 speech by board member Masu said cuts to bond-buying would stop at about ¥2 trillion a month from April 2027.
  • The BOJ's audited fiscal-2025 balance sheet shrank by ¥66.742 trillion. Interest it pays on reserves more than doubled and its payment to the Treasury fell.
  • Japan's yen stablecoin JPYSC is live but small and closed. Both of the BOJ's digital-currency tracks remain pre-production, and a 2021 link between Japanese bonds and Hong Kong central-bank money was retired on September 11 with no reason given.
  • At the September 1 G20 meeting in Asheville, all members present agreed the Chair's Statement except China, which objected to four paragraphs, including those on IMF surveillance and sovereign debt.
  • A related deep dive found no public 50-year Saudi deal to sell oil only in dollars, though a real dollar-oil recycling system exists.

Why it matters

It supports the thesis in the money system: established central banks, finance ministries and payment networks are being rebuilt through new links, rules and digital tools while keeping control of final settlement. The study does not find a coordinated plan to replace the dollar or the yen.

What to watch

  • 2027-04 · Whether the BOJ's bond-purchase cuts stop at about ¥2 trillion a month as signaled.
  • pending · Any move by the BOJ from digital-currency testing to an actual issuance decision.
  • pending · Follow-through on the G20 Asheville work on digital assets and payment modernization.

Grading the system behind each G20 currency, with no overall ranking

Currency Watch judges the monetary system behind each G20 currency on separate measures (prices, exchange order, external adjustment, usability for payments, resilience), kept apart from its formal IMF legal status. It gives no combined score or league table.

What we found

  • Scope is 19 G20 countries and 17 currencies, with the euro as one currency plus separate France, Germany and Italy records. Six systems (U.S. dollar, euro, yuan, yen, Saudi riyal, Argentine peso) were graded in a July 20, 2026 pilot, then Turkey, Brazil and Russia in Wave 1; the other eight remain ungraded.
  • An audit of the pilot found nine passes and one failure in comparing the international-role measure across Saudi Arabia and Argentina. The fix applies only going forward; the old grades stay sealed.
  • Lead case: a U.S. import surcharge under the balance-of-payments law (19 U.S.C. § 2132), proclaimed February 20, 2026 with a conditional July 24, 2026 end. The Court of International Trade held it unauthorized on May 7; the Federal Circuit stayed that ruling on June 11 pending appeal.
  • The IMF's 2026 U.S. review said tariffs broadly would have a modest effect on the trade deficit while raising prices and cutting activity. A law's stated purpose is not proof of its effect.
  • The grades are the study's own judgments, not IMF findings.

Why it matters

Gives a steady way to tell whether the working rules of world money are changing, such as a U.S. tariff justified by an old balance-of-payments law, without mixing up announcements, court rulings and real effects.

What to watch

  • pending · The Federal Circuit appeal on the Section 122 surcharge.
  • pending · Trade, price and current-account data showing whether the surcharge actually changed the U.S. balance of payments.

In a crisis, central banks use separate tools, not one switch

When banks run, systems fail, or war and sanctions hit, countries respond in separate steps: keep payments running, lend against collateral, guarantee deposits, wind down failed banks, limit capital moves, borrow abroad, and later decide who takes the loss. A central bank controls only part of that chain.

What we found

  • A central bank can lend its own money against good collateral, but it cannot fix a corrupted ledger, make a broke bank solvent, pay uninsured claims or unfreeze foreign reserves.
  • Korea, from Jan. 2, 2026, lets banks pre-clear corporate loans as collateral for a separate emergency facility (BOK Article 65); the setup is live, but nothing draws automatically. Brazil's Immediate Liquidity Line can be tapped automatically within a preset limit.
  • China, under rules in force Jan. 1, 2026, treats a commercial-bank e-CNY wallet as that bank's deposit, so a run hits the bank first, not the central bank.
  • Europe spreads emergency power across national central banks, the ECB, resolution authorities and national deposit schemes. In the Feb. 2025 TARGET outage, critical payments kept moving while collateral transfers were delayed.
  • The BRICS $100 billion reserve arrangement is in force but has never been drawn, and 70% of access is tied to the IMF. Russia, after the 2022 reserve freeze, showed that capital controls ration foreign currency but do not create it.

Why it matters

Countries are writing crisis tools, including for digital money, into their existing central bank, deposit and payment laws rather than replacing them. The study also notes the U.S. may enter a foreign crisis later through dollar swaps, sanctions reach and Treasury collateral rather than as the first mover.

What to watch

  • pending · The first bank to draw on Korea's pre-cleared loan collateral, with its terms.
  • pending · The first BRICS reserve arrangement draw, or final approval of the revised treaty that would allow non-dollar payment currencies.

G7 and G20 set goals, but other bodies make them happen

The G7 and G20 shape priorities, but they are not legal or operating authorities. Real change happens only when the IMF, FSB, IEA, development banks or national governments act, and so far results lag the goals.

What we found

  • Cross-border payments is the most tracked shared program, but FSB monitoring found no tangible global improvement for end users and judged good progress by 2027 unlikely.
  • The G7 has a quantified but non-binding target to diversify rare-earth and permanent-magnet supply, with an alliance, tracing, stockpile and recycling plans. Its 195-project, €64 billion total still needs project-by-project checking.
  • G20 debt work now has playbooks, a manual, creditor data sharing and a sample MOU, but it does not decide any country's case or grant relief.
  • The IEA/member oil release in March 2026 is the clearest real action linked to G7 coordination, and it was carried out by the IEA and members, not the G7.
  • Treasury published the G20 Asheville finance chair's statement on September 1. China objected to paragraphs 4, 10, 11 and 13, so those parts are not group-wide consensus.

Why it matters

It shows the gap between big forum goals and the national and technical bodies that actually change systems. That supports the thesis's focus on agencies and operating machinery rather than summit statements.

What to watch

  • November 2026 · Delivery dates for the G7+ Ports Network and anti-infiltration plan.
  • October 2026 · FSB payments progress report and jurisdiction plans.
  • end of 2026 · G7 assessment on protecting minors online and AI chatbots.

How the United States participates in and can leave international organizations

The international order is changing one job at a time. Countries are leaving some bodies, rebuilding others and creating smaller groups for specific tasks; they are not replacing the whole system at once.

What we found

  • The international system is not one hierarchy. A UN organ, specialized agency, NATO alliance, IMF shareholder institution, G20 forum and political accord each has a different legal and financial grammar.
  • The United States has different powers in different rooms: one vote, a veto, a permanent seat, a weighted share, a capital subscription, an assessed contribution, a voluntary donation, a host obligation or an informal summit seat are not interchangeable.
  • The Library already has strong controlling maps for the United Nations, the completed WHO exit, the IMF constitution and G7/G20 coordination, so the new project will reuse them rather than duplicate them.
  • The United States remains a UN Charter Member and permanent Security Council member. The January 7 memorandum instead cuts participation or funding to 31 differently constituted UN departments, bodies, programmes, funds, mechanisms and one treaty regime.
  • The UN's cash shortage has forced major peacekeeping reductions and a financial-rule change. That proves operating pressure without proving the affected functions moved into a successor institution.
  • NATO is being rebalanced from inside: Europeans take the three Joint Force Commands while the United States retains SACEUR and leads the land, air and maritime component commands. No Article 13 exit notice was located.
  • The OAS gives every state one vote but assigns the United States 49.990% of the 2026 quota. A delayed 2025 U.S. payment temporarily destabilized its Regular Fund, then was paid in early 2026.
  • IAEA and OPCW retain treaty-grounded verification machinery, while INTERPOL owns the cross-border police-data channel but cannot arrest anyone. The United States continues to use all three incumbents.
  • Wave 2 finds no cross-family successor security architecture: NATO roles shifted inside NATO, the OAS periphery narrowed, and the verifier/data institutions remain active.
  • The U.S. says WHO membership ended on January 22, 2026, but WHA79.2 still treated the United States as a Member in arrears and contemplated suspending its vote in 2027. The exit's legal and money books are not reconciled.
  • The United States rejected the 2024 IHR amendments, not the whole IHR. WHO's current table keeps the U.S. under the 2014 and 2022 text, and no separate six-month withdrawal notice was found.
  • The Pan American Health Organization has its own founding charter and legal identity. It remains a separate regional health body for the United States even while serving as the World Health Organization's office for the Americas.
  • The America First Global Health Strategy has bilateral MOUs, co-investment, joint committees and country funding pipelines. That proves resource migration for U.S. assistance, not replacement of WHO's global alert and normative book.
  • UNESCO membership ends December 31, 2026, but the United States remains a Member until then and separately ratified conventions such as World Heritage continue unless ended through their own instruments.
  • The IMF is a quota institution, not a world government. The U.S. 16.49% vote can block 85% decisions but cannot pass them or enact a borrower's reforms.
  • The World Bank Group is five separate legal entities. U.S. voting power ranges from about 9.48% in IDA to 16.91% in IFC, with separate capital, contracts, exit paths and remedies.
  • The WTO remains active, but its Appellate Body cannot hear appeals. Other Members use the Article 25 MPIA route; the United States is not a participant and instead presses for WTO reform.
  • The OECD tax dispute produced an internalized fork: the U.S. rejected the earlier deal, negotiated a side-by-side regime, and the OECD/G20 framework changed its central record and reporting machinery.
  • BIS participation belongs to the Federal Reserve constellation, not a sovereign U.S. membership node. BIS standards still need U.S. rulemaking before they operate domestically.
  • G7 and G20 are agenda routers without treaty personality, assessed budgets or implementation power. Their commitments matter only when a competent institution or government acts.
  • ICAO owns the common aviation standards and differences record, while FAA and other national authorities implement. Its passport specifications are an interoperability layer, not a sovereign issuer or legal-entity rule.
  • IMO membership is distinct from SOLAS, MARPOL and the other separately consented maritime treaties. IMO organizes the rulebook and audits; flag, port and coastal States enforce it.
  • ITU carries both binding Radio Regulations and generally voluntary technical Recommendations. The United States remained a Council State and its largest 2026 Member-State contributor at CHF11.1 million.
  • WIPO is predominantly user-fee funded rather than assessment funded. On October 1, 2026, its Madrid e-Filing platform became the sole route for initial outbound U.S. international trademark applications—a default-route change toward the incumbent.
  • The United States is not a CERN Member: it has LHC and HL-LHC observer and project rights through DOE, NSF and collaboration agreements but no Member vote or assessment.
  • The United States is a treaty Member of ITER with a veto on specified core Council decisions, a roughly 9.09% construction share and in-kind performance. Its 2026 central-solenoid delivery and Council participation prove operation; an exit would not erase construction, decommissioning or accrued IP obligations.
  • No Wave 5 withdrawal notice or successor transfer was located. Kyoto Vision still lacks the fund, secretariat, facility-access instrument, standards owner or contribution reallocation needed to bypass these incumbents.
  • The U.S. Paris withdrawal became legally effective January 27, 2026, while the separately noticed UNFCCC withdrawal does not become effective until February 27, 2027. Nested treaty regimes can remain on different clocks.
  • Kyoto Vision reaches only parallel doctrine: seventeen endorsers but no legal person, common fund, programme, facility agreement or contribution migration.
  • Artemis reaches shared machinery: 76 signatories, a Principals group, working group, rotating chairs, workshops and agreed recommendations. It remains non-binding and expressly nested in the Outer Space Treaty and COPUOS system.
  • The Board of Peace is a real new charter organization with legal personality, Member votes, a Chairman-controlled Executive Board, funding vehicles and U.S. immunities recognition. Security Council resolution 2803 authorizes a bounded Gaza execution role while retaining UN reporting and renewal gates.
  • The latest independent operating boundary matters: a September UN report said the Board's Palestinian administrative committee had not entered Gaza to assume governance. Shared machinery and planning are proved; completed field handoff and default use are not.
  • The strongest confirmed bypass remains narrow: bilateral U.S. health assistance moved to MOUs, joint committees, co-investment and funding pipelines, while WHO's normative, alert and IHR functions did not transfer.
  • The best statement of the thesis is now modular: preserve selected incumbent legitimacy and veto or renewal gates while moving chosen execution functions into national, bilateral, minilateral or bespoke coalition machinery.

Why it matters

International institutions may retain their legal charters while the newest funding, standards, infrastructure and execution move into smaller coalitions or national systems. A common entity-and-control map lets us distinguish real functional succession from ordinary coordination or political theater.

What to watch

  • 2026-10-05 onward · Capture Artemis principals outputs and test whether recommendations become mission agreements, payload access, standards adoption or resource migration.
  • 2026-11 onward · Capture ITU PP-26 elections, Final Acts and the next U.S. contribution class.
  • 2026-12-31 · Verify UNESCO exit effectiveness and convention-by-convention continuity.
  • 2027-02-27 · Verify UNFCCC exit effectiveness and treatment of U.S. obligations during the notice period.
  • next Board of Peace report · Test donor disbursement, NCAG field entry, ISF deployment, record transfer and Security Council renewal against the migration gates.

U.S.–China is many separate deals, not one relationship

The U.S.–China relationship runs on many separate tracks: leaders, diplomacy, trade, chips, money, Taiwan, the military and people-to-people ties. China's leverage sits mostly in mid-stage processing and production scale, and America's in frontier technology permissions, aerospace and market access.

What we found

  • A leaders' agreement can steer the whole relationship without changing one tariff line, export license or military posture. A narrow rule change can shift trade while the political relationship stays the same on paper.
  • Each track has its own players, legal tools and time needed to find substitutes. The study maps eleven industrial and trade systems by choke point and replacement time.
  • A September 28, 2026 update on the new Board of Trade: matching U.S. and Chinese official publications now give agreed procedures, named principals, meeting cadence, '30-for-30' terms and approved product lists. Domestic tariff changes and real effects were still pending.
  • The study keeps Chinese, U.S. and international sources apart. Official sources show what each side says and has done, not neutral proof of the other's intent. Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan are never folded into mainland figures.

Why it matters

Big-power change is happening through specific tools like tariff schedules, export licenses and trade boards, not one grand treaty. That matches the thesis of change built through existing machinery.

What to watch

  • pending · Each side putting the Board of Trade's agreed tariff terms and product lists into domestic law, and the first real trade effects.
  • pending · Open follow-up checks the study names on chip and AI permissions, Taiwan arms transfers and supply substitution.

U.S.–Russia ties run through many thin channels, not one relationship

The U.S. and Russia deal with each other through a patchwork: strained diplomacy, nuclear deterrence with no treaty after New START expired, ad hoc Ukraine talks, sanctions run through licenses and waivers, and a few working exceptions like the space station. Russia's trade has moved toward Asia and third countries, not stopped.

What we found

  • New START has expired with no successor treaty.
  • Ukraine diplomacy is active but ad hoc, with no public final settlement.
  • U.S. sanctions and export controls work as case-by-case permission systems through licenses, waivers and enforcement. The study builds on earlier work on a September 2026 U.S. sanctions statute.
  • The International Space Station remains a narrow working exception. People-to-people links are very thin.
  • Russia's ties carry different legal weight: partnerships with China and Iran, a mutual-assistance treaty with North Korea, a CSTO defense obligation and EAEU economic integration.

Why it matters

The U.S. relationship with Russia now runs more through U.S. law, such as sanctions, licenses and export controls, than through treaties. The study finds it less institutionalized than the China relationship and more dependent on one-sided law and envoy contact.

What to watch

  • pending · Any successor arms-control arrangement after New START.
  • pending · A public Ukraine settlement or binding joint instrument.
  • pending · Changes to U.S. sanctions, licenses or waivers on Russia.

Scottish, Welsh and Irish party cooperation is not independence

Nothing was declared on September 13, 2026. Cooperation among Scottish, Welsh and Irish nationalist parties is political coordination, not a change in who holds referendum power or in the UK constitution.

What we found

  • A reported SNP, Plaid Cymru and Sinn Fein memorandum scheduled for September 14 is a political-coordination step. An official full text from the parties was not found by the cutoff.
  • The official Welsh-Scottish government Cardiff Agreement was reviewed. It is non-binding and expressly does not change any statutory powers or duties.
  • The study maps the UK, the Crown, the four nations, three legal systems, London and the City of London as separate legal bodies, and keeps them distinct from the Crown Dependencies and Overseas Territories.
  • Its map of the UK move to ledger-based finance finds that legal effect depends on a chain of documents: Acts, regulator rules and approvals, operating terms, private rulebooks and customer contracts.

Why it matters

It is a useful foreign contrast: in the UK, change in money and markets also runs through layers of statute, regulator rules and private rulebooks, while political moves toward self-determination have not changed the legal structure.

Japan's August: defending the yen, rebuilding after a quake, testing digital money

In August 2026 Japan worked several fronts at once: earthquake recovery, a U.S.-coordinated yen purchase, a cautious central bank, staged household relief, signed energy financing and real-money digital settlement tests. None of it shows a new money system or a crisis.

What we found

  • Japan's Finance Ministry confirmed it bought yen on July 31 (U.S. Eastern time) in coordination with the U.S. Treasury, and reported ¥15.3993 trillion of intervention for July 30 to August 26. No U.S. source confirmed the U.S. side, and the reported euro sale is press-only.
  • The July 28 Kumamoto earthquake (magnitude 7.1, provisional) left 38 dead, 368 injured and 31,798 homes damaged as of August 27. The Cabinet authorized ¥147.8 billion of reserve funds on August 28.
  • The Bank of Japan held at about 1.0% by an 8–1 vote on July 31, with one member wanting 1.25%. A later note records a raise to 1.25% at the September 17–18 meeting, effective September 24.
  • Project Agorá ran controlled real-value tests: 28 central banks and firms completed 30 transactions worth about CHF 800,000, including ¥86 million, with the BOJ, Mizuho, MUFG and SMBC taking part. Production settlement was not shown.
  • JBIC signed loans on August 7 for natural-gas power projects in Pennsylvania and Texas (JBIC portions about $801 million and $739 million), with NEXI insuring private portions. Draws, construction and operation are not proven.

Why it matters

A key ally is reworking money and industry through its existing banks, central bank and state lenders, in controlled pilots rather than by replacing them. It also shows U.S.–Japan currency coordination in practice.

What to watch

  • pending · A U.S. primary record of the coordinated yen operation: amount, account and currency sold.
  • pending · The Cabinet-order start date for the main crypto-market reform under Act No. 64, due within one year of July 23, 2026.
  • 2027-04-01 · Targeted start of the two-year 1% food consumption-tax rate, still only executive policy.

What we found

  • The National Archives transcript is the official written record of the speech and Q&A; the White House video settles disputes over what was said and when.
  • Trump said he was 'seeking immediate negotiations' to acquire Greenland, wanted 'right, title, and ownership' rather than a lease, tied it to a planned 'Golden Dome' missile defense, and said 'I won't use force.'
  • On Sept. 22, 2026 the Greenland talks produced a signed public three-party security agreement. Its text keeps sovereignty and mutual-agreement requirements and still needs parliamentary completion, entry into force and real operations.
  • He also made economic claims, including 270,000 federal workers removed, $18 trillion in investment commitments and 1.6% core inflation over three months. These are his statements; the source text here does not check them.

Why it matters

The Greenland track shows a large public demand being turned into a signed agreement that still has to pass each side's normal legal steps before it does anything. The gap between signing and taking effect is where to look.

What to watch

  • pending · Parliamentary approval and entry into force of the Sept. 22 Greenland security agreement, and the first real operating steps under it.

White House transshipment report is a claim, not proof against Canada

The August 13 White House report puts Canada in its highest transshipment-risk group and lays out a plan for customs to track goods, factories, owners and routes. But it proves no Canadian violation, and the tracking system is still being proposed.

What we found

  • "The Great Transshipment Scam" (August 13, 2026, 25 pages) puts Canada in Tier 1, its top risk group. That is the report's own label, not a legal finding against any Canadian firm or shipment.
  • The report mixes estimates that answer different questions and should not be added up: CEA's $34.2-$89.6 billion modeled tariff loss, Commerce's $109 billion benchmark, and Altana's $303 billion of USMCA trade exposed to stricter checks (Altana's own loss estimate was about $40 billion).
  • Its proposed "Detective Border" would link products, factories, owners, shipping papers, routes and AI risk scores to guide customs checks. It is a target design, not a working system.
  • Executive Order 14411 (June 3, 2026) is binding direction to DHS and CBP. CBP's importer-of-record data notice (August 19) said operation would begin September 18.
  • CBP's September 2 advance notice of proposed rulemaking asks about new disclosures, business identifiers, tracing and AI; comments are due December 1. It is an inquiry, not a proposed or final rule.

Why it matters

This is the thesis in a customs office: origin checking is moving toward a data-and-identity system through an executive order, Federal Register notices and rulemaking, not new legislation. So far it is direction and inquiry, not operation.

What to watch

  • 2026-09-18 · Whether CBP's importer-of-record data check actually started operating.
  • 2026-11-30 · EO 14411 180-day deadline for specified implementation work.
  • 2026-12-01 · Comment close on CBP's supply-chain disclosure ANPRM; watch for a proposed or final rule after.
