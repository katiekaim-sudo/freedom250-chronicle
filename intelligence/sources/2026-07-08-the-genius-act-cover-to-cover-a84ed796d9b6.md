## What it is, in one breath

The GENIUS Act makes it a federal crime — $1,000,000 and five years — for anyone but a *permitted payment stablecoin issuer* to issue a dollar stablecoin in the United States, then builds the licensing machine: bank subsidiaries through their banking agency, nonbanks and uninsured national banks through the OCC, state issuers under $10B through certified state regimes. One-to-one reserves in cash, T-bills of 93 days or less, overnight repo, and government money funds; monthly published reserve reports; no interest paid to holders; no FDIC insurance and no pretending otherwise. The signing was 2025-07-18. The switch has not yet been thrown — that is what the next section is about.

## The clockwork — when the machine actually turns on

The law runs on three clocks, and reading them together is the whole game:

**The effective date (Sec 20).** The Act — and all its amendments — takes effect on the *earlier* of **2027-01-18** (18 months after enactment) or **120 days after the primary Federal regulators issue final implementing regulations**. Nothing in the chapter is in force today. Every rule of the new order — the issuance ban, the reserve regime, the bankruptcy super-priority — sits cocked and waiting on the regulators' pens.

**The rulemaking deadline (Sec 13).** Regulators — federal *and state* — were required to promulgate final rules by **2026-07-18**. As originally written on July 8, this note proposed a conditional: **if** final rules landed on the deadline, the 120-day fuse would have put the effective date around **2026-11-15**. They did not. The missed deadline is now part of the record. The publication date of any later qualifying final rules still controls the 120-day branch, but the November Fountain-pass ignition did not materialize through this mechanism.

**The guillotine (Sec 3(b)).** Three years after enactment — **2028-07-18** — digital asset service providers may no longer offer or sell any stablecoin that isn't from a permitted issuer. And Sec 3(g) is the quieter, *earlier* blade: it carries no date of its own, so it fires with the Act's general effective date (~Nov 2026, Jan 2027 at latest) — from that day a non-permitted stablecoin ceases to count as *cash or cash equivalent for accounting*, ceases to be eligible *margin and collateral* for FCMs, clearinghouses, broker-dealers and swap dealers, and ceases to be acceptable for *interbank settlement*. Tether doesn't get banned; it gets demonetized by accounting, and the accounting dies a year and a half before the sales window closes. Four days after that switch, on **2028-07-22**, comes the verified next Metonic strike of the Cancer Eclipse Spine — the eclipse at 29.8° Cancer on the US money nerve. Congress wrote a three-year fuse that detonates inside the Spine window. Nobody in that building knew; the pattern didn't ask.

## The nooks and crannies

**Treasury is the throne.** The Stablecoin Certification Review Committee is Treasury (chair), Fed, FDIC — and the big gates need *unanimity*: state-regime certification, Big-Tech entry, foreign-regime comparability. Beyond the committee, the Secretary personally holds: safe harbors from the issuance ban itself under "unusual and exigent circumstances," justified to two committees **possibly in a classified annex** (Sec 3(c)(2)) — emergency money-law by classified letter; the power to designate foreign issuers noncompliant and blacklist them from the US secondary market by Federal Register notice (Sec 8); a personal $500,000-per-violation fining power over anyone *marketing* a product as a stablecoin that isn't one (Sec 4(e)(3)); the principles that decide whether every state regime lives or dies (Sec 4(c)(2)); national-security waivers of the foreign-issuer rules, in consultation with the DNI (Sec 8(c)(2)); and intelligence/law-enforcement agencies can waive Sec 8 entirely for their own operations (Sec 8(c)(3)). The Fed, by contrast, got a tying-rule pen and a 48-hours-notice emergency lever over state issuers it must share with the states. The OCC got the empire: exclusive licensing of nonbank issuers, preempting state licensing law outright (Sec 4(b), 5(h)) — which is why the vault's OCC application queue reads like a cast list (World Liberty Trust, Sony, Kraken, EDX, Morgan Stanley, Catena).

**The kill switch is a condition of existence.** A "lawful order" is defined as one requiring a person to "**seize, freeze, burn, or prevent the transfer of**" its stablecoins — *burn* is in the statute (Sec 2(16)). No issuer may issue at all unless it has built "the technological capability to comply, and will comply" (Sec 4(a)(6)(B)). Foreign coins can't even be *offered* here without the same capability (Sec 3(b)(2), Sec 8). Treasury coordinates with issuers before blocking a foreign person's property "to the best of the Secretary's ability" — and is expressly not required to warn them first. Every lawful dollar-token in the world, permitted or foreign-registered, must carry a government-reachable self-destruct. That is the programmable-money fact under all the "it's not a CBDC" branding, and it needs no conspiracy reading: it's printed.

**The bankruptcy super-priority is the deepest cut.** New 11 U.S.C. 507(e): if reserves fall short, holders' remaining claims "shall have first priority over any other claim, **including over any expenses and claims that have priority under that subsection**" — ahead of the lawyers and administrators who would run the estate, ahead of wages, ahead of taxes. Reserves are excluded from the ordinary estate waterfall (Sec 11); custodied customer coins are the customer's property, shielded from the custodian's creditors, holders ratable among themselves (Sec 10). Read coldly: an insolvent issuer's estate may have nothing left to *pay for its own bankruptcy* — which means the real policy is that permitted issuers must never be allowed to reach that door. The law quietly creates a class of institution that is too priority-laden to fail, without a resolution fund, without insurance, without a lender of last resort — the Act even states master-account eligibility is neither expanded nor contracted (Sec 4(a)(13)), leaving issuers outside the Fed's door holding T-bills they may have to fire-sale in a run. The run-risk lives in the 93-day paper, exactly where the CEA and the bank lobby fought it all spring.

**The ethics wall has a President-shaped door.** Sec 4(i)(2) declares that existing ethics law prohibits "any member of Congress or senior executive branch official" from *issuing* a payment stablecoin in office — via 18 U.S.C. 208, which by its own terms does not reach the President or Vice President. The family business isn't the officeholder anyway: World Liberty's USD1 runs through the family, and its trust-bank application has sat in the OCC queue since January. Sec 19 then adds stablecoin holdings over $5,000 to federal financial-disclosure forms — the officials must *disclose* the coins the law lets the first family *mint*. The populist reading writes itself, and the statute supplies every word of it.

**The Big-Tech gate is real but has a gap.** A *public* company not predominantly financial — and any *foreign* non-financial company — needs a unanimous SCRC vote plus data-use vows: no using stablecoin transaction data to target ads, no selling it, no sharing with non-affiliates, absent consent (Sec 4(a)(12)). Meta is gated; so is Alibaba. But the prohibition runs on the definition of "public company" — a reporting issuer. A *privately held* giant is not gated by the text. X Money never has to ask the committee. Note also what the gate protects: it is the only consumer-data wall in the Act. Banks and fintech issuers face no equivalent statutory limit on mining their own coin's transaction graph.

**The DeFi carve-out is total.** "Digital asset service provider" excludes protocols, protocol developers, self-custodial interfaces, validators, and liquidity-pool participants (Sec 2(7)(B)); the Act doesn't apply to direct P2P transfers, your own wallets, or moving your own coins between your own accounts abroad (Sec 3(h)). The guillotine falls on *intermediaries* only. Uniswap can serve un-permitted coins forever, as written — Sec 9(e) even orders Treasury to come back with recommendations on whether "digital asset service provider" should reach decentralized finance, i.e., Congress knows and left the door open on purpose.

**Tokenized deposits live outside the Act entirely.** A deposit "recorded using distributed ledger technology" is expressly *not* a payment stablecoin (Sec 2(22)(B)(ii)), and Sec 16 blesses banks tokenizing deposits, running books on ledgers, and custodying coins — while forbidding regulators from forcing custodied assets onto balance sheets or demanding capital against them (the SAB 121 kill, now statute). So JPMD and its siblings answer to *none* of this law's reserve, disclosure, or audit rules — the banks' parallel track runs unregulated-by-GENIUS beside the regulated stablecoin track. The vault's June 24 Future-of-Payments hearing and the July 3 "multiple forms of digital money" notes are this exact seam.

**Foreign-currency and even SDR coins fit the frame.** A payment stablecoin pegs to "monetary value" = *a national currency* — which includes money issued by *foreign* central banks and by *intergovernmental organizations* (Sec 2(17)–(19)). A US-permitted issuer could mint a euro coin, a yen coin — arguably an SDR coin — under this charter. File beside the IMF/XRP thread and the One Machine bridge: the statute's definitions already speak the basket-money language.

**The reserve list is a Treasury-funding flywheel.** Permissible reserves: currency, Fed balances, insured deposits, **T-bills ≤93 days**, overnight repo against short T-bills, government MMFs, "similarly liquid Federal Government-issued assets," and — nook worth underlining — **all of the above in tokenized form** (Sec 4(a)(1)(A)(viii)). Growth in stablecoins is, by statutory design, growth in demand for short Treasury paper: the Bessent demolition thesis written as a reserve schedule. And the tokenized-reserves clause means the Ledger track (DTCC tokenizing Treasuries) and the stablecoin track are one loop: tokenized T-bills backing tokenized dollars.

**Deadlines with teeth, all pointing toward yes.** Regulator silence on a complete application = **deemed approved** at 120 days (Sec 5(d)(3)). OCC silence on a foreign-issuer registration = deemed approved at 30 days (Sec 18(c)(1)(B)). Denials require written findings *with actionable recommendations*, hearing rights, and can't be grounded in the coin running on a public or decentralized network (Sec 5(d)(2)(A)(ii)). States with pre-existing regimes get expedited certification and *presumptive* waivers from the $10B federal handoff, rebuttable only by clear and convincing evidence (Sec 4(d)(3)(C)(ii)) — the NYDFS/Wyoming grandfather clause. Compare the vault's April notes on the bankers' associations begging for delay: the statute was built to be hard to slow.

**Monthly Sarbanes-Oxley.** CEO and CFO certify the reserve report *every month*, on pain of the criminal penalties of 18 U.S.C. 1350(c) (Sec 4(a)(3)) — public-company officers face that exposure quarterly; stablecoin officers face it twelve times a year. Issuers over $50B add annual GAAP financials audited under **PCAOB standards** (Sec 4(a)(10)) — the same PCAOB the vault just watched being captured by personnel and purse. The auditor of the money is the watchdog on the leash; the capture arc and the stablecoin arc are one story at the audit layer. Enforcement runs on the FDI Act chassis: $100k/day civil tiers, six-year reachback after an officer leaves, removal and prohibition powers (Sec 6).

**The SEC and CFTC are evicted.** Sec 17 amends the '33, '34, '40 Acts, Advisers Act, SIPA, and the Commodity Exchange Act: a permitted issuer's stablecoin is *neither security nor commodity*, and the issuer is not an investment company. Banking regulators own the asset class outright — which is why the CLARITY Act (market structure for everything else) is the other shoe, and why the vault's May–June Clarity notes matter to this subplot. One more federalism oddity for the collection: Sec 13 *commands state regulators* to promulgate rules within the year — Congress ordering state agencies to write regulations is the kind of clause that draws a Tenth Amendment lawsuit when someone finally minds.

**Names.** No permitted coin may use "United States," "United States Government," or "USG" in its name, or market itself as legal tender or government-backed — but "USD" is expressly fine (Sec 4(a)(9)). The law forbids the coins from *claiming* to be the dollar while building them to *function* as the dollar's new plumbing.
