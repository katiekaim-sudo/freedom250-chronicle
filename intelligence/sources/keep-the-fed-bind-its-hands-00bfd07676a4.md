# Keep the Fed. Bind Its Hands.

## The Fed’s powers are being split, named, and moved into fixed rails

Updated: 2026-08-20  
Type: synthesis  
Factual spine: [Federal Reserve Restructuring — Factual Timeline](../sources/federal-reserve-restructuring-factual-timeline-4d78a4e48145.html)

---

## The story in one line

The Federal Reserve is not being torn down. Its shell is being kept while the vague rights, old customs, and free hand that made it act like a power unto itself are being forced into law, split among its parts, and moved into fixed market rails.

Put more bluntly:

> **Keep the Fed. Bind its hands. Split its powers. Move the work into fixed rails.**

This is a reading of the record, not a claim that Dallas, Kansas City, the White House, the Supreme Court, the SEC, and the New York Fed have signed one shared plan. The facts show many linked fights. The claim that they form one great change is the layer built on top.

---

## 1. The thing called “the Fed” is not one thing

The name hides the parts.

The Federal Reserve System is made of:

- a Board of Governors in Washington;
- twelve separately chartered Reserve Banks;
- a Federal Open Market Committee made from Board members and Reserve Bank heads;
- the New York Fed’s trading desk, which carries out the FOMC’s orders;
- bank-held stock in the Reserve Banks;
- payment accounts and service ties;
- clearing, cash, wire, and settlement systems;
- deals with Treasury, clearing banks, dealers, and foreign central banks.

The Board is a federal body. Its members are named by the President and confirmed by the Senate. The Reserve Banks are bodies corporate. Their heads are not named by the President or confirmed by the Senate. Yet those heads help set the nation’s money stance, and New York carries it out.

For more than a century, this mixed form held because each part knew the bounds of the game. Presidents did not try to fire Governors. Reserve Banks worked within shared norms. The Board issued guides. Markets treated short letters, notices, staff views, and settled ways of doing things as if they were one seamless body of law.

The seams stayed out of sight because few strong hands pulled on them at once.

Now they are being pulled.

---

## 2. The “fourth branch” problem has been forced into the open

The Constitution sets out three branches. It does not name a fourth branch for bodies called “independent.”

Congress may make an agency. It may give that agency work. But the work still has to fit within the three-branch frame. The Fed is not a court. Congress does not run its day-to-day work. Much of what the Fed does—writing rules, watching banks, carrying out laws, granting or denying access, and wielding public money tools—looks like executive work.

That brings the hard question:

> If the Fed wields executive power, how far may it stand beyond the President’s reach?

The Cook fight made the question real. President Trump tried to fire Governor Lisa Cook “for cause.” Before that act, the words had lain unused for 111 years. No court had been forced to give them a full working meaning.

The Supreme Court did not let the firing take hold at once. It required notice, a chance to answer, and court review. It said cause must mean more than a wish for a friendlier vote. It also said the Fed’s shield from at-will removal fits the Constitution because central-bank freedom has a deep root in American history.

On the same day, the Court struck down the FTC’s removal shield.

So the Court drew a bright line:

- the FTC wields executive power and must fall under presidential control;
- the Fed keeps a special shield because of its old and distinct form.

That does not end the fourth-branch fight. It sharpens it.

The Court’s answer is not that the Fed fits neatly under one branch. Its answer is that the Fed is a special case blessed by history. The more the Court says this aloud, the more the public can ask what makes the Fed different, which of its acts deserve the shield, and whether the shield covers the whole System or only the setting of monetary policy.

The legal mark-up has begun.

---

## 3. The old strength lay in the gaps between law and custom

The Fed’s day-to-day power does not come from statute alone.

It rests on a stack:

```text
Federal Reserve Act
        ↓
Board rules
        ↓
policy statements and guides
        ↓
FOMC orders
        ↓
Reserve Bank circulars and account terms
        ↓
staff readings, short letters, notices, and long-held custom
```

The top of the stack is law. Much of the bottom is not.

Yet the bottom is where the machine runs.

Who gets an account? Which service may that account use? How much may it hold? May it overdraw? Which firms may trade with the Desk? How does the Desk take bids? Which bank settles the trade? When does a tool open? What does “for cause” mean? Who speaks for the whole System?

Often the answer has been set by a guide, a form, a short vote, an operating note, or a way of work that no one chose to test in court.

This gave the Fed room. The law set the outer wall. Practice filled the space within it.

The new fight works by taking that space away.

---

## 4. Kansas City found the gate

The Kansas City Fed’s Kraken act is the cleanest case.

The Board’s Account Access Guidelines set a three-tier test. Kraken’s Wyoming bank fell into Tier 3, the group meant to face the hardest review. But Tier 3 did not mean barred.

Kansas City approved a one-year “limited-purpose account” for Kraken Financial / Payward Financial, the Wyoming SPDI. Its May 8 official supplement limits the account to Fedwire Funds, bars intraday and discount-window credit and interest, imposes a closing-balance limit, and excludes services under the account for the Kraken exchange and affiliates.

The key fact is plain: neither statute nor the Board’s guidelines named that kind of account.

Kansas City did not cast off the Board’s rules. It used them. It said each account choice belongs to an individual Reserve Bank and must turn on the facts of the request.

That is the move:

```text
Board guide meant to bind Reserve Bank choice
                 ↓
Kansas City reads the guide as room to act
                 ↓
new account form is born through a one-bank choice
```

Congress asked where the power came from. Bank trade groups cried foul. The official supplement now establishes the restrictions above; it does not establish a completed direct transaction or which public date starts the one-year term. See [Federal Reserve Restructuring — Factual Timeline](../sources/federal-reserve-restructuring-factual-timeline-4d78a4e48145.html).

The Board then put out its own Payment Account plan. It also asked Reserve Banks to pause new Tier 3 choices while the plan was under review.

The Board’s own words matter: by law, Reserve Banks decide account requests.

Kansas City forced the center to write down what the center had left vague.

The act did not break the System. It made the System show its seams.

## 5. Dallas found the pipes

Dallas is working on a different part of the same body.

Lorie Logan once ran the New York Fed desk that carries out FOMC orders. From Dallas, she has laid out a new way for the Fed to work.

Her pieces fit:

1. Treat the Fed as a bank with a balance sheet, not as a mist-shrouded power.
2. Cut the size of the balance sheet held in normal times.
3. Keep strong tools that can send cash into the system when strain hits.
4. Root the operating target more firmly in the Treasury repo market.
5. Clear Fed repo—and perhaps wider open-market work—through a central counterparty.
6. Use new tech, perhaps blockchains, to link market parts and move collateral faster.
7. Keep market support apart from a change in the stance of monetary policy.

This would recast the post-2008 Fed.

The old form holds a vast book of assets at all times. Its footprint shapes markets. Its rescue acts can blur into ease. Its choices can look like fiscal acts carried out by unelected hands.

The Dallas form is smaller in calm times but quick when strain hits. It relies more on set tools, set rates, set terms, and common pipes.

```text
Large standing balance sheet
        ↓
smaller standing book
+ stronger on-call cash tools
+ faster collateral flow
+ common clearing rails
```

The Fed does not lose all power. It changes the kind of power it holds.

It moves from owner and maker toward gatekeeper, backstop, and grid hand.

---

## 6. The clearing move is the heart of the change

The SEC has ordered much of the Treasury cash and repo market into central clearing. Fed trades are exempt.

An exemption can be read two ways.

The old reading is:

> The Fed stands outside because it is the central bank.

Logan’s reading is:

> The Fed is not forced to join, so the FOMC is free to join by choice.

That turns a shield into a door.

When a trade is centrally cleared, the clearinghouse steps between the first buyer and seller. It becomes buyer to each seller and seller to each buyer. It sets common terms for risk, margin, netting, and default.

If Fed work moves through that frame, the Fed remains the wellspring of reserve money and the maker of the policy order. But it also becomes a user of shared market rails.

Its acts grow more:

- standard;
- seen;
- netted;
- bound to set time and data forms;
- tied to the health and rules of the clearing node.

The Fed may win special terms. It may sit above the loss stack. It may choose the clearinghouse. None of that changes the broad turn: work once done through the Fed’s own bilateral ties would pass through a common rule-bound hub.

That is why the move can be read as a castration.

The body lives. The free hand is bound.

---

## 7. New York is building the machine

The New York Fed is not standing still while Dallas speaks.

It has:

- moved repo and reverse repo to FedTrade Plus;
- moved Treasury buys and Treasury buybacks to the same new platform;
- moved securities lending;
- tested agency mortgage-backed bond buys;
- removed the whole-facility cap on standing repo;
- shifted standing repo to full allotment;
- run the tool twice each day;
- gathered proof that firms shun the tool in part because Fed trades cannot be netted through a clearinghouse.

In May, SOMA Manager Roberto Perli said Fed staff were weighing central clearing. He named the hard open points: which clearinghouse, what kind of membership, and what new moral hazard might arise.

This is not a launch. No FOMC vote, test trade, clearinghouse choice, or go-live date has been made public.

But it is more than a stray Dallas idea.

The plan has moved through a clear chain:

```text
Dallas call
    ↓
New York survey proof
    ↓
SOMA Manager says staff are at work
    ↓
Dallas repeats the call inside New York
```

New York may not be the foe in this tale. It may be the shop that builds the very machine that cuts back its old freedom.

---

## 8. Waller’s fight shows the split within the System

Governor Christopher Waller has put forth another plan: stop running each Reserve Bank as if it needs its own full set of back-office works. Build more national lines of business. Let one Reserve Bank carry a task for the whole System. Keep local work local, but bind broad work to one System frame.

His sharp phrase was the clash between a “Bank mindset” and a “System mindset.”

The push drew fears that Reserve Bank freedom would shrink. Waller then drew a line around what he said must stay local:

- each president’s voice in monetary policy;
- the research that backs that voice;
- bank watch and the discount window;
- ties to local firms and towns.

This is not the same as Logan’s clearing plan or Kansas City’s Kraken act. But it works on the same live seam:

> What belongs to one Reserve Bank, and what belongs to the System as a whole?

Kansas City is using a Bank mindset at the gate. Dallas is using its own voice to press a change in System-wide market work. Waller wants to draw the bounds before twelve banks build twelve paths.

The fight is not just White House against Fed.

It is also Board against Banks, Banks against one another, and local right against System rule.

---

## 9. Stablecoins change who makes dollar-like claims

The GENIUS Act adds another front.

It sets a path for private firms to issue dollar tokens backed by cash, short Treasury debt, repo, and other safe dollar assets. These firms may move value on always-on digital rails while holding much of their backing in claims tied to the state.

That can split the old bank bundle:

```text
bank deposit
= money claim
+ payment tool
+ loan funding
+ bank tie

stablecoin
= money-like claim
+ payment and settlement tool
+ safe-asset backing
- no need to fund bank loans in the old way
```

The Reserve Banks are not of one mind.

- Kansas City has cast doubt on how much stablecoins are used for real-world payments while pushing FedNow and opening a narrow Fed account to Kraken.
- Richmond has shown how Treasury-backed tokens may draw more world demand into dollars and U.S. debt.
- Atlanta is asking whether stablecoins bring narrow banking back in a new dress.
- Boston and New York are studying runs, tokenized deposits, and safe assets.
- Philadelphia is tracing on-chain cash flight.
- Chicago is asking what backstop a token firm might one day need.
- Cleveland is tying the dollar’s crown to an independent central bank.

The shared fact is that the System is no longer asking whether digital dollar claims will exist. It is working out who may issue them, where they may settle, what backs them, and when they may touch central-bank money.

The fight has moved from “coin or no coin” to the gate and the pipes.

## 10. Why “castration” fits better than “demolition”

Demolition means the body falls.

That is not what the record shows.

No leading part of this tale calls for ending:

- the Board;
- the twelve Reserve Banks;
- the FOMC;
- the New York Desk;
- Fedwire;
- FedNow;
- the discount window;
- reserve money;
- the Fed’s role as last lender.

The push is to make each power more narrow, more named, and more bound.

```text
Vague “for cause” words
→ notice, proof, hearing, court review

Vague account guides
→ set Payment Account terms

Reserve Bank custom
→ Board-wide access rules

Fed market exemption
→ choice to enter common clearing

Large always-on balance sheet
→ smaller book plus set on-call tools

Fed funds target
→ possible move toward a broad repo rate

Twelve sets of back-office works
→ national lines of business
```

Each act keeps the power but changes its form.

The power becomes less like a royal right and more like a tool with a tag, a rule, a start time, an end time, and a named hand on the switch.

That is castration in the plain sense used here: not death, but loss of the unchecked force to seed, spread, and act on one’s own will.

## 11. The Fed may be doing this to save itself

There is no need to read Logan, Waller, Perli, or Schmid as foes of central banking.

They may think the old form puts the Fed at risk.

A vast balance sheet draws blame. Vague rules draw court fights. Hidden account choices draw charges of favor. Twelve copies of the same work waste funds. Bilateral market ties feed the sense that the Fed and big dealers form a closed club. Each gap gives foes a place to strike.

The cure, from within the Fed, may be:

- cut the book;
- spell out the rules;
- share the pipes;
- show the terms;
- keep local voices;
- join common market forms;
- hold fast to freedom only where money policy needs it.

From that view, the cut is not meant to kill the Fed’s freedom. It is meant to save the small core of freedom that can still be defended.

The same act can therefore be both:

- a bid to save the Fed; and
- a loss of the traits that made it seem like a fourth branch.

## 12. What is known, and what is still a reading

### Known from the record

- The Supreme Court gave the Fed a special historical shield while striking down the FTC’s removal shield.
- The Cook case forced the dormant “for cause” words into a working legal test.
- Kansas City granted Kraken a limited-purpose account not named in statute or the Board’s account guide.
- The Board then proposed a standard Payment Account and asked for a pause on new Tier 3 choices.
- The Board says account choices are made by Reserve Banks.
- Logan has more than once called for central clearing of Fed repo or wider open-market work.
- New York counterparties said the lack of clearing and netting holds back use of the standing repo tool.
- Perli said Fed staff are weighing central clearing.
- New York is moving Desk work onto FedTrade Plus in stages.
- Waller has called for more System-wide lines of business across the Reserve Banks.
- GENIUS rulemaking is under way.
- H.R. 5396, the Price Stability Act of 2026, has advanced from introduction to a formally reported House bill; it has not passed the House or changed the Fed's dual mandate.
- Cash Treasury clearing is due at year-end 2026; repo clearing is due in mid-2027.

### The reading built from those facts

- The events form one broad shift from loose discretion to bound infrastructure.
- Dallas and Kansas City are lead actors in that shift.
- The Board’s Payment Account plan is a bid to take hold of a path Kansas City opened.
- Central clearing would cut back the Fed’s sovereign-like market stance.
- The legal and market fights together amount to a castration of the Fed rather than its fall.
- The Fed may accept the cut as the price of keeping a smaller, harder core of policy freedom.

Those claims must be tested against what comes next.

---

## 13. The next tests

The story gets stronger if:

- the House passes H.R. 5396 or another Federal Reserve Act amendment that narrows the Fed's operative mandate;
- the Board’s final Payment Account rule turns Kansas City’s one-off form into a System-wide class;
- another Reserve Bank grants a like account;
- the final rule cuts back Reserve Bank choice or, instead, states its bounds more clearly;
- the FOMC starts a central-clearing test;
- New York names a clearinghouse or membership model;
- Logan’s smaller-book plan enters FOMC work;
- a repo rate gains formal weight in the operating frame;
- Waller’s national lines of business go live;
- Jackson Hole joins payment access, clearing, token money, and policy in one frame;
- Cook or a later case draws a hard line between monetary policy and bank rulemaking.

The story weakens if:

- the Payment Account plan dies after comment;
- Kraken’s account ends without a copy or renewal;
- the Fed keeps all its trades outside central clearing;
- FedTrade Plus changes only the front-end screen and no deeper work;
- the balance sheet stays near its post-2008 form;
- the Reserve Banks lose no freedom and the Board gains no new grip;
- the courts treat the Fed’s special shield as settled and narrow, with no further mark-up of its parts.

## Closing

The old Fed drew strength from being hard to name.

It was public and private. National and local. A rule-maker and a bank. A market hand and a market judge. A child of Congress kept at a remove from Congress. An executive-like body shielded from the chief executive.

That mixed form is now under strain from all sides.

The President is testing who may be fired. The Court is testing which shield fits the Constitution. Kansas City is testing who may enter. The Board is trying to write the gate rules. Dallas is testing whether the Fed should give up its market carve-out. New York is rebuilding the tools. The SEC is moving the Treasury market into clearing hubs. GENIUS is making a new class of dollar issuer. Waller is testing what belongs to one Bank and what belongs to the whole System.

None of these acts, alone, ends the Federal Reserve.

Together, they may end the age in which “the Fed” could answer each hard question by pointing to the mist between its parts.

> **Keep the Fed. Remove the mist. Bind the hands. Put the power in the pipes.**
