# Reading the Charter — DTCC / DTC / NSCC / FICC

Source texts (this folder): `1975 - Securities Exchange Act §17A...(verbatim).md`, `2010 - Dodd-Frank Title VIII...(verbatim).md`, `2012 - DTC Public Status Statement & FSOC SIFMU Designation (verbatim excerpts).md`. DTC/DTCC/NSCC/FICC have no public organic charter — they are private companies. This note reads the PUBLIC legal skeleton Congress and DTC's own regulators built around them: a private clearing monopoly the state regulates like a utility and, when it matters, treats like part of the Fed System.

## 1. Immunities & liability shields

None found — and that absence is itself the finding. Unlike the Fed (tax exemption, FRA §7), the IMF (Art. IX immunities), or BIS (Brussels Protocol), DTC/NSCC/FICC carry **no statutory immunity from suit, no archives inviolability, no asset-seizure shield**. What §17A gives them instead is a *regulatory* shield of a different kind: a registered clearing agency's own disciplinary actions against its participants (expulsion, suspension, fines — §17A(b)(3)(G)) are conducted under Commission-approved rules and subject to Commission oversight (§17A(d)), which as a practical matter means DTC/NSCC/FICC's internal enforcement decisions get a layer of federal-regulatory cover that an ordinary private contract counterparty would not have. That is process protection, not sovereign immunity — a real but much thinner shield than the older international-organization entities in this library.

## 2. Tax exemptions

None found in either statute captured. §17A and Dodd-Frank Title VIII are silent on DTC/NSCC/FICC's tax status — as ordinary state-chartered corporations (DTC is a New York limited-purpose trust company per its own public statement, dtcc.com), they are presumptively subject to ordinary corporate taxation like any other trust company, with no special federal carve-out found in the sections captured.

## 3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — the control map

This is the section where DTC/NSCC/FICC's private-company nature collides hardest with public oversight, and the collision runs in layers:

- **Corporate governance (private):** DTCC is a user-owned, at-cost cooperative — its board and officers are elected/appointed per its own (non-public) bylaws, not by any government official. §17A(b)(3)(C) requires only that a registered clearing agency's rules "assure a fair representation of its shareholders (or members) and participants in the selection of its directors" — a floor on process fairness, not a federal appointment power.
- **Registration is existential, not optional:** §17A(b)(1) makes it "unlawful for any clearing agency, unless registered" to perform clearing-agency functions at all — so the SEC does not appoint DTC/NSCC/FICC's leadership, but it holds the on/off switch for the entire business via registration, denial, suspension, and revocation authority (§17A(b), (c)).
- **The Dodd-Frank layer adds a second, harder switch:** once FSOC designates a utility "systemically important" (§5463(a)), the Board of Governors gains standing to *recommend* enforcement action to the entity's primary Supervisory Agency (§5466(e)) — and if the Supervisory Agency refuses, the Fed can escalate to FSOC for a **binding vote** forcing enforcement (§5466(e)(3)-(4)). In a true emergency, the Fed can skip the recommendation step entirely and act directly (§5466(f)) using bank-enforcement powers under 12 U.S.C. §1818 "as if the designated financial market utility was an insured depository institution." A private company's board answers to its members day-to-day, but in a crisis the Federal Reserve itself can step in and enforce against it directly, using the same legal toolkit it would use against a chartered bank.
- **Rescission requires the same ⅔ Council supermajority as designation** (§5463(b)(1)) — so once in the SIFMU club, an entity does not drift out quietly; it takes the same high bar to leave as to enter.

## 4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations

DTC/NSCC/FICC are for-profit-structured (at-cost cooperative) private companies funded by participant fees, dues, and service charges — §17A(b)(3)(D) requires clearing-agency rules to "provide for the equitable allocation of reasonable dues, fees, and other charges among its participants," confirming the fee-funded model is baked into the federal registration standard itself. No congressional appropriation touches DTC/NSCC/FICC's operating budget. The one place public money *could* touch them: §5465(a) lets a Federal Reserve Bank open an actual Fed **account** for a designated FMU and provide Fed services (per FRA §11A(b) and the "first undesignated paragraph" of FRA §13); §5465(b) lets the Fed extend **discount and borrowing privileges** (advances) in "unusual or exigent circumstances" upon majority Board vote after Treasury consultation; §5465(c) lets the Fed **pay earnings** on FMU balances the same way it does for depository institutions; §5465(d) lets the Fed **exempt or modify reserve requirements** for a designated FMU. None of this is appropriated money — it's the Fed's own balance sheet and services, opened by statute to a private company's use, explicitly WITHOUT requiring that company to become "a bank or bank holding company" (§5465(b), last sentence). That is the single strangest fact in the whole file: Congress wrote a clause that gives a private company access to the central bank's own plumbing while going out of its way to say this access does not turn it into a bank.

## 5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

No FOIA exemption, no GAO carve-out found in either statute captured. What exists instead is a *mandatory* examination regime running the other direction — §5466(a) requires the Supervisory Agency to examine a designated FMU "at least once annually," and §5466(d) requires that agency to consult annually with the Board of Governors on examination scope, with the Fed entitled to participate in any examination at its discretion. Rather than being shielded from disclosure, a SIFMU is subject to MORE mandatory federal examination than an ordinary private company — the price of the Fed-account privilege in §5465 is being examined like a bank in §5466.

## 6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

- **For DTC/NSCC/FICC's own rules:** §17A(b)(2) requires clearing-agency rule changes to go through SEC filing (per §19(a) of the Exchange Act); §5465(e) separately requires a designated FMU to give its Supervisory Agency 60 days' advance notice of any rule/procedure/operations change that "could materially affect the nature or level of risks presented" — with the agency able to OBJECT and block the change (§5465(e)(1)(F)), extend review by another 60 days for "novel or complex issues" (§5465(e)(1)(H)), or let an emergency change through with after-the-fact notice within 24 hours (§5465(e)(2)). A private company's own internal rulebook amendments are thus subject to a federal veto mechanism once it is Dodd-Frank-designated.
- **For the statute itself:** §17A and Dodd-Frank Title VIII are ordinary federal statutes, amendable only by Congress — no international-treaty-style withdrawal clause exists because DTC/NSCC/FICC are domestic private corporations subject to domestic law, not treaty parties.
- **Designation withdrawal:** §5463(b) — the Council can rescind a SIFMU designation by the same ⅔ supermajority vote required to grant it, if the entity "no longer meets the standards for systemic importance." No mechanism exists for the entity itself to request withdrawal — the exit door, like the entry door, is Council-controlled.

## 7. Emergency powers

Two distinct emergency mechanisms stack on top of each other:
- **§5465(b)** — the Fed can open discount/borrowing privileges to a designated FMU "only in unusual or exigent circumstances," on majority Board vote after Treasury consultation, and only upon a showing the FMU "is unable to secure adequate credit accommodations from other banking institutions" — this is the FMU-specific cousin of the Fed's general §13(3) emergency-lending authority (captured under the Federal Reserve System entity), aimed squarely at clearinghouses rather than banks.
- **§5466(f)** — the Fed can take DIRECT emergency enforcement action against a designated FMU (bypassing its own Supervisory Agency) if it has "reasonable cause to conclude" an action or condition "poses an imminent risk of substantial harm" to financial institutions, critical markets, or the broader financial system, AND the risk "precludes the Board of Governors' use of the procedures in subsection (e)" (the normal recommend-and-wait-60-days process). This requires a majority Council vote after consulting the Supervisory Agency — fast, but not unilateral.
- **§5465(e)(2)** — even the FMU itself gets an emergency exception: it may self-certify an emergency and implement an otherwise-notice-requiring rule change immediately, reporting to its Supervisory Agency within 24 hours. Three separate emergency triggers, three separate actors (the FMU itself, its Supervisory Agency via the normal channel, and the Fed via the break-glass channel) — a system built assuming things will go wrong fast and giving multiple parties a fast lever.

## 8. The definitions section

§5462's definitions are quietly doing enormous work. Two stand out:
- **"Financial market utility" (§5462(6))** is defined functionally — "any person that manages or operates a multilateral system for the purpose of transferring, clearing, or settling payments, securities, or other financial transactions" — not by corporate form or charter. This is why a private, member-owned New York trust company (DTC) and a derivatives clearing organization and a bank-owned payments company (The Clearing House) can all be swept into the same regulatory category: the definition doesn't care what you ARE, only what you DO.
- **"Systemically important" (§5462(9))** is defined by *consequence*, not by size threshold: "a situation where the failure of or a disruption to the functioning of a financial market utility... could create, or increase, the risk of significant liquidity or credit problems spreading among financial institutions or markets and thereby threaten the stability of the financial system of the United States." No dollar figure, no market-share percentage — it's a pure contagion-risk definition, deliberately elastic, left to the Council's four-factor discretionary test in §5463(a)(2) (aggregate value, aggregate exposure, interdependencies, failure-effect) plus a fifth catch-all: "any other factors that the Council deems appropriate" (§5463(a)(2)(E)). The designation power is, by its own text, boundless at the margin.

## 9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

Neither statute captured addresses DTC/NSCC/FICC's corporate perpetuity or dissolution — that lives in New York state trust-company law and DTC's own (non-public) certificate of incorporation, not in federal statute. What federal law DOES address is designation succession: a SIFMU designation persists until affirmatively rescinded by the same ⅔ Council supermajority that created it (§5463(b)) — the regulatory status, unlike the corporate charter, has its own explicit continuation/termination rule wholly independent of the company's actual corporate life span.

## 10. Anything strange — the clause you'd never expect

Two candidates, and they're related:
- **§5465(b)'s closing sentence** — "Access to discount and borrowing privileges under section 10B of the Federal Reserve Act as authorized in this section does not require a designated financial market utility to be or become a bank or bank holding company." Congress built a bank-shaped hole in the law, put a private clearinghouse through it, and then wrote a sentence specifically denying that walking through the hole makes you a bank. That is the single strangest clause in the file — Congress engineering deliberate legal ambiguity about what kind of creature these entities are, on purpose.
- **DTC's own three-clause self-description** (captured in the 2012 file): "DTC is a member of the U.S. Federal Reserve System, a limited-purpose trust company under New York State banking law and a registered clearing agency with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission." Three separate regulators — the Fed, New York State, and the SEC — each hold a piece of DTC's legal identity, and none of the three holds the whole thing. No single regulator has full visibility or full control; DTC sits in the seam between all three.

## Layer map

- **1973 — DTC founded.** Created to eliminate the Wall Street "paper crisis" of the late 1960s by immobilizing physical stock certificates and moving to book-entry ownership changes. Chartered as a New York limited-purpose trust company; became a member of the Federal Reserve System. No federal statute created DTC — it is a private-sector response, later wrapped in federal regulation.
- **→ 1975 — Securities Exchange Act §17A enacted (Pub. L. 94-29).** Congress creates the federal "registered clearing agency" category and makes registration mandatory for anyone performing clearing-agency functions — DTC (and its sibling NSCC, founded 1976) become SEC-registered clearing agencies under this section. This is the first federal statutory leash on the entity, arriving two years after DTC already existed.
- **→ 1999 — DTCC holding company formed.** The Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation is created as the parent holding company for DTC and NSCC (later FICC, formed 2003 by merger of the Government Securities Clearing Corporation and MBS Clearing Corporation, joins the family). This is a private corporate restructuring — no federal statute required or authorized it; it happened under ordinary state corporate law.
- **→ 2010 — Dodd-Frank Title VIII enacted (Pub. L. 111-203).** Creates the "designated financial market utility" category and, critically, §5465's Fed-account/discount-window/reserve-requirement authority — the first time federal law gives a private clearing entity a POTENTIAL statutory bridge to the Fed's own balance sheet, conditioned on FSOC designation.
- **→ July 18, 2012 — FSOC designates DTC, FICC, and NSCC (along with five other FMUs) as systemically important.** The bridge in §5465 becomes live and specific to these three DTCC subsidiaries. This is the moment the private company crosses from "regulated by the SEC like any clearing agency" to "wired, by name, into the Fed's Title VIII apparatus."

## Entity Theory read

DTC/NSCC/FICC are private companies the state has quietly decided it cannot let fail — and it built the wiring to prove it. They started as an ordinary private-sector fix for a 1960s paperwork crisis, got folded into SEC registration in 1975 because Congress wanted a "national system," and then in 2012 got tagged, by name, as pieces of critical financial infrastructure — which under Dodd-Frank Title VIII means the Federal Reserve can open them an account, lend to them at the discount window, pay them interest on their balances, and exempt them from reserve requirements, ALL without making them a bank. Who really holds the leash: nobody holds just one leash — DTC answers to its own member-owned board day to day, to the SEC for registration and rule changes, to New York State for its trust-company charter, and now to the Fed/FSOC apparatus that can override its Supervisory Agency's enforcement decisions or bypass the Supervisory Agency entirely in an emergency. Does it survive a change of control? The corporate shell almost certainly does — it's a cooperative owned by the industry it serves, not a single controlling shareholder who could be replaced — but its SIFMU status is a live regulatory designation that can be rescinded by the same ⅔ Council vote that created it, meaning the entity's PRIVILEGED access to the Fed's balance sheet is more fragile, and more politically revocable, than the company itself. The bluntest way to say it: this is what "too big to fail" looks like when it's not a bank — a private clearing monopoly wearing a state safety net it never had to ask for by name, because Congress decided in 2010 that if this thing ever seized up, the whole market would seize up with it.
