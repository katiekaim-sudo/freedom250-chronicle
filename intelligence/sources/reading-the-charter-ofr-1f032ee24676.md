# Reading the Charter — OFR

## The 10-point hunt

**1. Immunities & liability shields.** None in this material — OFR is an executive office of Treasury, not a corporate entity with its own suit-immunity.

**2. Tax exemptions.** None.

**3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause.** §5342(b)(1)-(2): Director appointed by the President, Senate-confirmed, serves a 6-year term with a holdover provision (may keep serving until a successor is confirmed) — no removal-for-cause language at all in this material, meaning presumably at-will removal is the default. §5342(b)(4): the "prohibition on dual service" is the interesting structural clause — the OFR Director may not simultaneously head any financial regulatory agency, which forces at least nominal independence from every other regulator whose data OFR is built to collect and audit.

**4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations.** Not directly captured in §§5342-5344 (funding mechanics live in §5345, not captured this pass) — but §5342(c) shows the Director sets the Office's own annual budget "in consultation with the Chairperson" (of FSOC), not through an ordinary appropriations request, and §5342(d)(2) exempts OFR pay-setting from ordinary Title 5 classification/General Schedule pay rules entirely — the Office runs its own personnel system, untethered from standard federal HR law. Per the manifest, OFR is funded by assessments — a follow-up capture of §5345 would confirm the mechanics.

**5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs.** §5344(b)(2)(B): the Data Center "shall not publish any confidential data" it collects — a hard, one-line confidentiality wall around the raw financial-company data it gathers, distinct from its public-facing reference databases (§5344(b)(2)(A)). §5343(d)(2): Congressional testimony from the Director cannot be pre-cleared, reviewed, or approved by any other officer or agency of the United States before delivery — an explicit statutory independence-of-testimony clause, plus a required disclaimer that the Director's views "do not necessarily represent the views of the President."

**6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses.** N/A — ordinary statutory amendment.

**7. Emergency powers.** None as such, but the subpoena power in §5343(f) functions as a compulsory-process escalation path: the Director may subpoena data directly from a financial company, but only on written findings that the data is needed and that OFR has first coordinated with the company's primary regulator (§5343(f)(1)(A)-(B)) — a check built in specifically to stop OFR from freelancing around the existing regulatory chain of command.

**8. Definitions section quietly load-bearing.** Not captured (§5341) — but the operative language leans hard on "financial company," "financial transaction data," and "position data" (§5344(b)(1)(B)(iii)), terms broad enough to potentially reach any entity FSOC or the Director decides poses systemic risk, not just banks.

**9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution.** Not addressed — standing executive office, no term limit on the Office itself.

**10. Anything strange.** §5342(g) — the one-year post-employment "cooling off" ban. Any OFR Director or employee who has had access to confidential transaction/position data or business-confidential information is barred for one year after last having such access from working for or advising *any* financial company — "regardless of whether that entity is required to report to the Office." That's a blanket-industry lockout keyed to data access, not to any specific employer relationship — a much broader revolving-door rule than the norm, built because OFR by design sits on the single richest confidential dataset about the private financial system that any part of the federal government holds. The subpoena power in §5343(f) is the second strange one: a Treasury office with unilateral compulsory-process authority over "any financial company" in the country, gated only by an internal written finding and a coordination requirement — no court order needed to issue it, only to enforce it if defied (§5343(f)(3)).

## Layer map

- **2010 — Dodd-Frank Title I, Subtitle B, §§152-154 (Pub. L. 111-203):** creates OFR inside Treasury, gives it a Senate-confirmed 6-year Director barred from also heading any regulator, splits its work into the Data Center (collection/publication/confidentiality) and the Research and Analysis Center (systemic-risk research), and grants it subpoena power over financial companies' data gated by internal findings and inter-agency coordination.
- No amendments captured in this pass to §§5342-5344 — the structure appears to be as-enacted in 2010, unlike CFPB (Seila Law, 2025 funding cut) and FSOC (2017/2018/2022 amendments) which have each been substantively touched since.

## Entity Theory read

OFR is the data-and-research engine bolted onto FSOC's decision-making — it collects, standardizes, and subpoenas the raw financial information that lets FSOC decide who gets designated systemically important, but it holds no vote of its own and its Director sits on FSOC only as a nonvoting member. Its real power is informational, not regulatory: subpoena authority over any financial company's data, a hard confidentiality wall around what it collects, and a one-year industry-wide cooling-off period for anyone who's touched that data. Whoever controls the Director controls what FSOC sees and when — this is the entity that decides what data reaches the room where the real designation votes happen, without itself ever casting one.
