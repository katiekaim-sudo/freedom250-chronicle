C. Authority response

Field Entry
Response state PARTIAL
Exact proposition answered The Japanese authorities shared staff’s overall economic assessment and broad policy direction, broadly concurred with the preliminary external-sector assessment, agreed that inflation risks were balanced, and indicated continued conditional monetary normalization. They qualified staff’s monetary prescription by warning against undue reliance on point estimates of neutral rates. They remained committed to market-determined exchange rates while stressing concern over one-sided, rapid yen depreciation and reserving action against disorderly movements.
Source/page/date IMF CR 26/75, staff-report authorities’ views, PDF pp.18, 20–21 and 30; Statement by the Executive Director for Japan, PDF pp.101–103, dated 2026-03-27
Attribution boundary Staff assessment, Executive Board assessment, BOJ views, MOF views and the Executive Director’s member-authority statement remain separate. The March 27 staff update is staff—not member-authority—material.

F. Six-lane worksheet

Lane Proposed grade One-sentence reason Primary evidence + vintage Persistence evidence Shock evidence Competing explanation Confidence Legal/event link Data gap
L1 Domestic anchor C The 2% anchor remains credible and adjustment capacity is clear, but more than three and a half years of above-target inflation, repeated upside surprises and a roughly 6% cumulative real-wage loss since 2022 constitute a material persistent impairment. IMF CR 26/75 pp.10–11: 2025 headline inflation 3.2%, core 3.0%, broad price pressure and declining real wages; Table 1 p.47. Headline CPI: 2.5%, 3.2%, 2.7%, 3.2% in 2022–25; core: 1.1%, 3.9%, 2.4%, 3.0%. Expectations rose toward target and BOJ normalization began, preserving an identifiable repair path. Separate March 27 staff statement, pp.99–100: Middle East energy shock adds an estimated 0.3 percentage point to 2026 headline inflation, to 2.2%, with modest expected second-round effects. This post-cutoff estimate does not rewrite the March 2 record. Much of the overshoot reflected rice, food and import-price shocks; January inflation moderated sharply and medium-term expectations were aligning with target, which could support B. Persistence and purchasing-power loss keep the call at C. HIGH Article IV: NO-ADVERSE-FINDING-LOCATED; TP-CNJP-2026-DUALUSE unresolved and not scored Final 2026 inflation/expectations outcomes, actual real-wage turn, and 2026 ESR are outside the freeze
L2 Underlying order B Banking, liquidity and market functions remain broadly resilient, with bounded pockets of regional-bank, FX-funding, CRE and JGB-market strain but no generalized disorder or core-function impairment. IMF CR 26/75 Table 5 p.51, 2025Q1: regulatory capital 16.7%, Tier 1 15.2%, NPL ratio 1.1%, liquid assets 32.9% of assets and 49.8% of short-term liabilities; staff assessment pp.25–30. Capital and liquidity remained robust through 2019–25; NPLs stayed around 1.1–1.3%. Staff and authorities judged systemic risk materially unchanged, though sources shifted and FSAP implementation was uneven. A severe stagflation stress test kept aggregate CET ratios above regulatory requirements, but exposed institution-level pockets. The March 2026 external shock produced sharp market moves without evidence in the frozen package of banking fracture or payment disorder. Gross public debt above 200% of GDP, sizable FX/cross-currency exposures, thin regional-bank buffers and rising JGB volatility could justify C if they begin impairing funding or intermediation. Current operating evidence does not show that transmission. HIGH Article IV general status; no event promoted into this lane Institution-level stress-test dispersion, significant-currency liquidity ratios, FX-derivative positions and shinkin-bank coverage remain incomplete
L3 Exchange order B The free float operates through a deep market without intervention since July 2024 or documented fracture, but persistent one-sided depreciation, partial decoupling from yield differentials and official concern constitute bounded exchange-order strain. IMF CR 26/75 pp.15–16 and 20–21; de jure/de facto free float and no intervention since July 2024, p.97; 2025 external assessment p.54. Average JPY/USD moved from 131.5 in 2022 to 140.5 in 2023, 151.4 in 2024 and 149.7 in 2025. End-2025 REER was about 25% below its 2021 average, yet the 2025 CA-based REER gap midpoint was only +0.1%. April/May/July 2024 intervention episodes were isolated; none occurred in 2025. The separate March 27 shock statement recorded 1.8% yen depreciation amid the Middle East risk-off episode but did not identify disorder or intervention. Market depth, full flexibility, no recent intervention and an external position broadly in line could support A; the unexplained one-sided move and MOF concern support the bounded-strain B. Reserve change is not treated as the central test for this free-floating reserve currency. MEDIUM TP-US-2026-SECTION122 and TP-USJP-2025-TARIFF-FRAMEWORK are context only; no exchange-grade transmission Frozen packet lacks current bid/ask, realized-volatility and post-March intervention data; final 2026 ESR unavailable
L4 External adjustment A The external position is broadly in line across both completed and preliminary assessments, supported by a near-zero CA gap, very large positive NIIP and contained liability vulnerabilities. 2025 ESR, Japan assessment, PDF p.19: 2024 position broadly in line, CA gap +0.6% GDP, REER gap midpoint −3.3%, NIIP 89.8%; IMF CR 26/75 p.54: preliminary 2025 CA gap 0.0%, REER gap +0.1%, NIIP 82% at 2025Q3. CA surplus rose from 3.8% GDP in 2023 to 4.8% in 2024 and 2025, mainly from investment income; strong positive NIIP and diversified foreign assets persisted, while medium-term adjustment projects a gradual surplus decline. Higher U.S. tariffs were absorbed partly through exporter margins and had not materially altered medium-term export trends. The separate March 27 energy shock reduces the projected 2026 CA surplus from 4.6% to 3.8%, still without an assessed external imbalance. A high headline surplus, investment bottlenecks and much larger REER-model undervaluation estimates could imply a stronger-than-desirable position. IMF staff reconciled these through the CA approach and retained the broadly-in-line finding. HIGH TP-USJP-2025-TARIFF-FRAMEWORK; TP-US-2026-SECTION122; TP-CNJP-2026-DUALUSE unresolved Final 2026 ESR, full product-level tariff instruments and measured China-event trade effects remain unavailable
L5 Current-payment usability A Bona fide current payments remain usable with no located MCP, current-payment restriction, arrears or material transfer delay, apart from separately notified security restrictions. IMF CR 26/75 p.97, Fund Relations as of 2026-01-31: Article VIII §§2/3/4 accepted; exchange system free of MCPs and current-payment/transfer restrictions except notified security measures. No contrary operating finding appears across the persistence window; Japan’s open capital account and deep convertible FX market provide corroborating operating evidence. NOT-APPLICABLE — no current-payment restriction or transfer shock identified in the frozen JPY packet. The notified security restrictions may affect selected transactions, and the package does not itemize their scope; without transaction-level evidence, they cannot be treated as a general usability impairment. MEDIUM Legal card §§2–4; trade measures remain legally separate Itemized security-restriction scope and transaction-level transfer-delay evidence
L6 Systemic service A JPY continues to perform its actual reserve, vehicle, hedge and funding jobs through deep FX, derivatives and cross-border funding markets without evidence of systemic-service failure. BIS 2025 Triennial Survey, April 2025: JPY on one side of 16.8% of global FX turnover, third most traded; JPY OTC interest-rate derivatives averaged $411 billion daily, 5.2% of global turnover. IMF COFER 2026Q1: JPY remained a separately identified reserve currency with a 5.44% share. JPY’s FX-turnover share remained broadly stable against the prior triennial survey; yen IRD turnover rose 684% from 2022. BIS global-liquidity data recorded ¥65.6 trillion in yen credit to non-Japanese borrowers at 2025Q1, up 6% y/y after double-digit prior growth. The April 2025 FX survey occurred amid tariff-driven volatility and still documented deep turnover. Carry-trade unwinds transmitted volatility, but the evidence shows continued market operation rather than loss of funding or hedging service. The COFER share fell from 5.84% in 2025Q4 to 5.44% in 2026Q1, and reliance on yen-funded carry trades can amplify shocks; these are role risks, not evidence that the service failed. HIGH International-role overlay; trade events show no observed systemic-role impairment Current invoice/payment share, the precise funding-versus-speculative use of yen borrowing, and confidential COFER-holder composition are unavailable
