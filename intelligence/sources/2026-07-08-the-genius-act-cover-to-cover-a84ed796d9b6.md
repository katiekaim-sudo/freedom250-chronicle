# The GENIUS Act, Cover to Cover

**Question:** Who may issue a payment stablecoin, what legal claim does its holder receive, which institutions control its use, and when do those requirements begin?

This maintained factual reading preserves the original July 8 inquiry into the whole statute and its institutional consequences. Regulatory status retains the saved August 20, 2026 cutoff. The September 7 revision reconciles the legal clocks and separates statutory provisions from hypotheses; it is not a new survey of all implementing actions or a fresh audit of every clause. The complete original reading remains in historical custody.

**Primary authority:** [Public Law 119-27, 139 Stat. 419–466, approved July 18, 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ27/html/PLAW-119publ27.htm). Section numbers below refer to that enrolled law. The operative text controls over this reading guide.

## The maintained answer at the saved cutoff

The Act establishes a permission system for payment-stablecoin issuance and related distribution, reserves, redemption, custody, supervision and treatment. It preserves different federal, state and qualifying foreign routes. A token transfer, a state trust charter, an OCC bank approval and a GENIUS permitted-issuer approval are distinct facts.

The saved August 17 implementation audit located proposals, proposed information collections, one Section 9(e) report and unresolved statutory deliverables. It did not locate a qualifying final rule or a live federal/state/foreign permitted-issuer pathway. The August 20 correction retained the untriggered general effective-date finding and identified Treasury's Section 3 instrument as a proposal, with comments due October 19. Neither finding has been refreshed here to September 7.

For the detailed saved evidence, use GENIUS Act Implementation Gap Audit. Its August 17 public-inspection deadline placeholder is earlier than the separately dated August 20 correction; do not replace the latter with calendar arithmetic.

Section 20 does not erase the Act's separately expressed enactment-based preparation and reporting deadlines. The earlier blanket assertion that nothing in the chapter was in force obscured those distinctions. Treasury's Section 3 proposal is not itself a qualifying final regulation by a primary Federal payment stablecoin regulator, and issuer approval is not the Section 20 trigger.

The approximate September 20 hinge in the saved audit is derived monitoring arithmetic, not a new statutory deadline. No current activation date should be inferred from a nearby market launch, technology conversion or implementation target.

## Issuer identity and the permission routes

**Definitions and issuance (Sections 2–3).** The statutory payment-stablecoin definition, the issuer's legal entity and the recipient/jurisdiction matter. The framework is not confined to a brand or a token ticker. Section 3(a) restricts U.S. issuance to permitted issuers; its criminal provision addresses knowing participation in a violation, with a maximum $1 million fine for each violation, up to five years' imprisonment, or both. Issuance is distinct from the later service-provider offer/sale restriction.

**Federal and state routes (Sections 4–7).** The Act differentiates depository-institution subsidiaries, federal qualified issuers and state qualified issuers. The $10 billion threshold is part of the state/federal allocation and transition framework, with conditions and possible waivers; it is not a universal issuer-size ceiling. The relevant banking agency, NCUA, OCC, state regulator and Stablecoin Certification Review Committee have different functions.

**Committee and Treasury powers (Sections 2–4, 8 and 18).** The SCRC comprises Treasury, the Fed and FDIC. Voting and recommendations depend on the particular function; unanimity should not be generalized to every committee action. Treasury's responsibilities include safe-harbor rulemaking, defined emergency safe harbors, foreign-issuer determinations and enforcement-related functions. The existence of a statutory power does not establish that a particular determination or waiver has been exercised.

**Applications and review (Section 5).** The law sets review periods, written-denial and appeal requirements, and a deemed-approval mechanism for qualifying complete applications after 120 days. A missing public decision is not enough to establish deemed approval: completeness, receipt, the governing period and any applicable action must be evidenced. Charter approval and permitted-payment-stablecoin-issuer approval must not be substituted for each other.

**Supervision (Sections 6–7).** Federal supervision/enforcement and state-regime oversight are separate parts of the framework. The state route includes certification, federal/state coordination and defined emergency intervention conditions. A state's own proposed rule is not an SCRC certification or proof that an issuer is operating under GENIUS.

## Reserves, redemption and the holder's claim

**Reserve schedule (Section 4(a)(1)).** Issuers must maintain identifiable reserves at least one-to-one against outstanding coins. The statute permits defined forms of currency/Fed balances, qualifying deposits or shares, short Treasury instruments, specified repo and reverse-repo arrangements, qualifying government money-market investments and approved similarly liquid federal assets. Treasury bills, notes or bonds may qualify under the stated 93-day maturity tests; “T-bills only” is an incomplete description.

Tokenized-form eligibility applies to the reserve categories specifically cross-referenced by Section 4(a)(1)(A)(viii), subject to applicable law. It is not permission to tokenize every reserve category indiscriminately. The Act also limits reserve reuse/rehypothecation with defined exceptions. These requirements regulate reserve composition and control; they do not establish actual reserve holdings for any named issuer.

**Redemption and disclosure (Section 4).** The Act requires a disclosed redemption policy, conditions for timely redemption and fee changes, monthly reserve-composition reporting, examination by a registered public accounting firm, and officer certification. Knowingly false certifications attract the referenced criminal penalties. Issuers above the specified $50 billion threshold face additional annual financial-statement/audit requirements, including PCAOB standards. A reserve report, officer certification and audited annual financial statement are different evidence objects.

**Holder treatment (Sections 4, 10 and 11).** The framework prohibits issuer interest or yield solely for holding, using or retaining a payment stablecoin, restricts misleading government/insurance representations and regulates custody and segregation. Custody protections and issuer-insolvency priorities address different legal relationships. Section 11 includes special treatment of required reserves and priority for remaining holder claims where reserves are insufficient; this is not deposit insurance or a promise of full recovery in every failure.

The original reading asked whether high holder priority could leave an estate with insufficient resources for administration. That remains an insolvency question, not a statutory promise of rescue or proof that an issuer is too important to fail. The Act's preservation of existing Federal Reserve account-eligibility law is not a grant of an account, credit or lender-of-last-resort access.

## Transfer controls, distribution and exceptions

**Lawful orders (Sections 2, 3, 4 and 8).** The statute defines a lawful order and requires the specified ability and willingness to comply, including orders concerning seizure, freezing, burning or prevention of transfers. Legal capability, the actual technical control design, a particular order and execution of that order require separate evidence. This does not prove one universal government-operated control interface across every coin or network.

**Section 3(g) treatment.** The statutory restrictions concern cash/cash-equivalent accounting treatment, eligibility **as cash or cash-equivalent margin and collateral** for listed intermediaries, and specified wholesale payments/exchange and settlement among banking organizations. They do not say that every possible use as collateral is prohibited. Application to a named coin requires its issuer/status and the statutory exceptions.

**Foreign issuers (Sections 8 and 18).** Section 18 provides an exception from Section 3 for foreign issuers satisfying its conditions, including Treasury comparability, OCC registration, specified U.S. liquidity/reserve requirements and jurisdictional conditions. Section 8 addresses noncompliance and relevant enforcement/waiver powers. Foreign regulation or a company's announcement alone does not prove satisfaction of the U.S. route. The 30-day foreign-registration deemed-approval provision is distinct from the domestic 120-day application mechanism.

**Self-custody and decentralized activity (Sections 2, 3(h) and 9).** The Act has specified exclusions from the DASP definition and rules of construction for direct individual transfers, particular same-owner cross-border transfers and self-custodial wallets. These do not establish that a named platform, interface or business is exempt forever, or that all other law is displaced. Section 9's illicit-finance work remains distinct from the Section 14 non-payment-stablecoin study.

## Banks, other currencies and the wider financial system

**Tokenized deposits (Sections 2 and 16).** The payment-stablecoin definition excludes specified deposits, including those recorded using distributed-ledger technology. Section 16 preserves existing lawful banking activities and addresses custody/accounting treatment. This is a different legal object from a permitted payment stablecoin, not an unregulated token merely because it uses a ledger.

**Securities and commodities (Section 17).** The law amends specified federal securities, investment-company and commodity definitions for payment stablecoins issued by permitted issuers. Those amendments do not remove SEC/CFTC authority over every token, intermediary, activity or separate financial product associated with an issuer.

**Interoperability (Section 12).** Regulators assess interoperability and may prescribe standards under the stated process. The provision is not an adoption order for a particular blockchain, message network or native token.

**Currency scope (Section 2).** The definitions require attention to the referenced monetary value and its issuer. The original question about foreign-currency or SDR-denominated private coins remains a legal-structure question; it does not establish that XRP or another private token is an official SDR, a reserve-basket constituent or a government settlement asset.

**Reserve demand and tokenization.** The original thesis linked stablecoin growth, short Treasury demand and tokenized reserves. The statutory reserve menu supports a possible connection. Net Treasury demand still depends on actual reserve choices, the funding source, substitution from deposits/funds and market behavior. Permission to hold a tokenized reserve does not prove a DTC token, a particular CCP's collateral acceptance, a cash leg or a functioning common settlement system. Use [the clearing timeline](../sources/clearing-house-transition-factual-timeline-6595f41e3831.html) for separately dated implementation facts.

## Governance questions retained from the original reading

- **Non-financial issuers and data:** Section 4(a)(12) establishes conditions for specified non-financial public/foreign company issuers and related data-use restrictions. Whether a particular privately held company or affiliate falls within an exception requires its legal structure and the applicable definition. The original named-company exemption claim is not an established permission here.
- **Ethics and disclosure:** Section 4(i) references existing ethics restrictions, while Section 19 changes financial-disclosure treatment. These are different provisions. The original inference about a President, family member or family business cannot establish legal compliance or an exemption for a specific arrangement.
- **Federal/state power:** Licensing allocation, certification, preemption, coordinated rulemaking and emergency authority create different institutional controls. The statute alone does not prove regulatory capture, a coordinated political purpose or a future constitutional ruling.
- **Application timing:** Deemed-approval provisions matter, but a regulator's silence can only be evaluated against a complete identified application and the applicable statutory process.

## Saved deliverables and unresolved evidence

The July 18, 2026 statutory cluster includes rulemaking, the Section 14 study, the Section 4(a)(6)(C) lawful-order coordination report, Section 15 industry reporting and the specified SCRC non-financial-company interpretive rule. The August 17 audit distinguishes reports whose public text was not located from reports proven not delivered; the former cannot be called late solely because agency searches were empty.

Treasury's March 2026 illicit-finance report is the located Section 9(e) object. It does not satisfy Section 14. Its public copy did not establish the exact delivery date to Congress. The statute's later clocks also include the two-year reciprocity objective, three-year illicit-finance guidance/rulemaking and the separate insolvency-study report. Each requires its own responsible institution, action and source date.

The maintained unresolved questions are the qualifying final-rule trigger; named issuer approval under the correct regime; state certification; foreign comparability/registration; actual reserve and redemption practice; specific lawful-order implementation; and each report's publication versus delivery. These are evidence gaps at the saved cutoff, not authorization to start new watches or a finding of present-day noncompliance.
