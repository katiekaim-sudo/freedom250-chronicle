Reading the Charter — Federal Reserve System

Source text: 1913 - Federal Reserve Act (verbatim).md in this folder. Every claim below cites the exact section of the Federal Reserve Act (FRA).

1. Immunities & liability shields

The Fed's shield isn't a blanket immunity clause like the IMF's Article IX — it's structural. §10 ¶4 makes the Board's members personally untouchable from ordinary bank-conflict rules (they can't hold bank stock or office, FRA §10 ¶4) but also insulates the Board's operating money from Congress entirely: "funds derived from such assessments shall not be construed to be Government funds or appropriated moneys" (§10 ¶4). That line is doing immunity-adjacent work — if it isn't government money, it's harder to reach with the ordinary levers (GAO audit, appropriations riders) that touch government money. §7(c) gives the reserve banks themselves a direct tax shield: "exempt from Federal, State, and local taxation, except taxes upon real estate" — capital stock, surplus, and income all covered.

2. Tax exemptions

FRA §7(c): reserve banks — including capital stock, surplus, and income derived therefrom — are exempt from federal, state, and local taxation, with a single carve-out for real estate taxes. This is a direct, blanket exemption, not a treaty reciprocity clause. It has never been narrowed since 1913 (the Board's own note: "Part of original Federal Reserve Act. Designated subsection (c)...").

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — the control map

Appointment: Board of Governors members are appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, for 14-year terms (§10 ¶1). Chairman and Vice Chairs are separately designated by the President with Senate confirmation for 4-year terms (§10 ¶2). Removal: the FRA says a Board member holds office for the 14-year term "unless sooner removed for cause by the President" (§10 ¶2). "For cause" is the whole ballgame — it is NOT at-will removal. This is the textual anchor for the entire "Fed independence" argument, and it is exactly the clause under live political pressure in 2025-2026 disputes over presidential authority to remove Governors. Reserve bank presidents are a different animal: appointed by each reserve bank's own board of directors (Class B/C directors specifically, §4 ¶4 "Fifth"), subject only to Board of Governors approval — not presidential appointment at all. That's a second, quieter control channel that bypasses the White House and Senate entirely.

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations — off-budget status

This is the crown jewel. §10 ¶4: the Board funds itself by levying assessments ON the reserve banks (§10 ¶3), and those assessment funds are explicitly declared NOT to be "Government funds or appropriated moneys." Translation: Congress's power of the purse — the one lever that disciplines every other agency — does not touch the Fed's operating budget. The reserve banks in turn fund themselves from their lending/securities operations (§7(a)-(b)), pay the statutory capped dividend on mandatory member-bank subscription stock (§7(a)(1)), and apply the Act's surplus/remittance rules. The instrument is half paid and half callable, nontransferable and non-hypothecatable; it carries bounded voting and dividend rights but no ordinary residual claim. The 2025 audited statements also show why the cash path cannot be summarized as an automatic annual sweep: $6.281B was transferred to Treasury while the combined deferred asset increased to $243.481B. See Federal Reserve Financial Constitution — Source Document. Congress created the structure by statute and can amend it by statute.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

§2B(c) requires the Board to publish an "Audit" webpage with GAO reports, its own annual financial statements, and §13(3) emergency-lending reports for a minimum 6 months. §11(s) is the Dodd-Frank transparency regime forced onto the Fed after 2008: it must disclose counterparty names, amounts, rates, and collateral for emergency facilities and open-market counterparties — but only on a delay (1 year after a facility's termination for credit facilities; 2 years for routine open-market counterparties, §11(s)(2)). §11(s)(6) explicitly carves this delayed-disclosure regime OUT of ordinary FOIA (5 U.S.C. §552(b)(3)) until the release date. So: audited, but on the Fed's clock, not the public's.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

There's no "withdrawal" concept for the Fed — member banks don't get to leave the System without losing reserve-bank access, and Congress reserved to itself the unilateral right to amend or repeal the whole Act (this "reservation of right to amend," FRA §31, was not captured verbatim this pass — flagged as omitted). Practically: the entire amendment history captured in this file (1917, 1922, 1933, 1935, 1977, 1980, 2010...) shows Congress amends this constantly and unilaterally; the Fed itself has no veto.

7. Emergency powers

FRA §13(3) is the marquee emergency clause: "in unusual and exigent circumstances," the Board — by affirmative vote of at least 5 members — can authorize ANY reserve bank to lend to "any participant in any program or facility with broad-based eligibility," provided the borrower can't get credit elsewhere and the loan is adequately secured. Post-Dodd-Frank restrictions (added 2010, §13(3)(B)) bar the Fed from rescuing a single named company (no more AIG-style single-entity bailouts — "shall not be considered a program or facility with broad-based eligibility" if built around one company), require Treasury Secretary pre-approval of any new facility, and require insolvency screening of borrowers. §11(r)(2) is the break-glass-in-a-blackout clause: if fewer than 5 Governors are reachable, as few as 2 available members can act unanimously under §13(3) if they document that exigent circumstances exist AND that they tried "all available telephonic, telegraphic, and other electronic means" to reach the others first.

8. The definitions section — quietly load-bearing

FRA §1 defines "bank" to include state banks, banking associations, AND trust companies (broader than the plain-English word suggests) — meaning the Fed's regulatory reach was built to cover more than chartered "banks" from day one. §19(b)(1) — the modern reserve-requirement definitions — sweep in credit unions, savings associations, and even entities "wholly owned by or which consists only of" other depository institutions, meaning reserve requirements can follow money through holding structures, not just literal bank counters. §1 ¶3 quietly defines "board" as the Board of Governors — meaning every other section's reference to "the board" inherits this scope without saying so again.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

Reserve banks have "succession... until dissolved by Act of Congress or until forfeiture of franchise for violation of law" (§4 ¶4, "Second") — meaning they don't have a natural expiration date; Congress would have to actively kill one. On dissolution, whatever surplus is left after debts, dividends, and par stock value "shall be paid to and become the property of the United States" (§7(b), second paragraph) — so in the end-state, Treasury is the residual claimant, not the member-bank shareholders.

10. Anything strange — the clause you'd never expect

§10 ¶8 quietly amends an entirely different statute (Rev. Stat. §324) IN THE MIDDLE of the Federal Reserve Act to establish the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's modern structure and explicitly bar the Treasury Secretary from delaying, preventing, or intervening in OCC rules/enforcement "unless otherwise specifically provided by law" — a second, separate independence guarantee bolted onto a completely different agency, hiding inside the Fed's own charter section on Board membership. Also strange: §10 ¶2's cross-reference bug — the Board's own official annotation admits "the reference to 'the six members'... is an apparent error in the law and should read 'the seven members'" (§10 ¶4 editorial note) — a 90-year-old drafting typo Congress never bothered to fix.

Layer map

  • 1913 — Federal Reserve Act (Pub. L. 63-43, 38 Stat. 251). Creates the twelve reserve banks (§4) as member-bank-owned corporations with a Federal Reserve Board (Treasury Secretary and Comptroller sat ON the Board originally — political appointees embedded from day one), sets the original 12-member-adjacent structure, defines the note-issue/reserve/discount machinery (§§13, 14, 16, 19).
  • → Banking Act of 1935 (49 Stat. 684 et seq.). Total redesign of the top: renames the "Federal Reserve Board" to the "Board of Governors," STRIPS the Treasury Secretary and Comptroller off the Board (they'd sat on it since 1913 — this is when the Fed becomes formally separate from the Treasury at the top), creates the modern 7-member, 14-year-term Board with "removed for cause" protection (§10 ¶2), and completely rewrites §12A to create the modern FOMC structure (Board + 5 rotating reserve-bank presidents, §12A(a)) so that reserve-bank open-market operations require FOMC — not individual-bank — authorization (§12A(b)).
  • → Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977 (91 Stat. 1387). Adds §2A: the dual mandate — "maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates." This is the FIRST time Congress gives the Fed a legislated objective, 64 years after creating the machinery with no stated goal beyond "elastic currency." Also amends §4 ¶10-12 director-selection language to add anti-discrimination requirements.
  • → Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 (94 Stat. 132). Rewrites §19(b) to extend reserve requirements to ALL depository institutions — not just Fed member banks — closing the loophole where state banks could dodge Fed reserve rules by staying outside the System. This is the moment the Fed's reserve-requirement reach becomes universal instead of membership-gated.
  • → Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 (Pub. L. 111-203, 124 Stat. 1376). Bolts real restrictions onto §13(3) for the first time since 1932 — no more single-company bailouts, mandatory Treasury pre-approval, insolvency screening (§13(3)(B)) — plus the entire disclosure regime in §11(s) forcing delayed publication of emergency-lending counterparties, and a new Vice Chairman for Supervision post (§10 ¶2) reporting separately to Congress (§10 ¶12).

Entity Theory read

The Federal Reserve System is a hybrid creature Congress built and can still legally dissolve or restructure, but Congress's ordinary appropriations leash does not reach the Board's operating budget. The Banks are privately capitalized through mandatory member-bank statutory subscription stock; saying the member banks therefore "own" the Banks in the ordinary residual-equity sense is too broad. The shares cannot be transferred or pledged, only half of par is paid unless the remainder is called, dividends and director-election rights are bounded, one member bank receives one vote within its class regardless of shares, and the United States is the statutory residual claimant on Reserve Bank dissolution. Who really holds the leash: the President (appointments), Congress (statutory amendment power), the Board (supervision and Class C directors), regional boards (bounded director and president-selection roles), and the FOMC for open-market direction. The day-to-day money-creation lever sits one layer down, run by a committee where five of twelve voting seats are Reserve Bank presidents. Does it survive a change of control? Yes by express corporate succession until congressional dissolution or forfeiture of franchise; the separate Governor-removal question remains a live constitutional fight.
