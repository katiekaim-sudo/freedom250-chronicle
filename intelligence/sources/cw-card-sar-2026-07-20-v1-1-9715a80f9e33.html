F. Six-lane worksheet

Lane Proposed grade One-sentence reason Primary evidence + vintage Persistence evidence Shock evidence Competing explanation Confidence Legal/event link Data gap
L1 Domestic anchor A The USD-linked anchor has delivered low, stable aggregate inflation without a disproportionate activity cost, despite localized rent pressure and an exogenous conflict shock. CPI averaged 1.7% in 2024; May 2025 inflation was 2.2%, while non-oil GDP grew 4.5% in 2024 and unemployment reached record lows. IMF CR25/223, PDF pp.12, 48. Frozen June 2026 national CPI: 1.8%. Average CPI: 2.5% in 2022, 2.3% in 2023, 1.7% in 2024; non-oil growth: 10.9%, 5.8%, 4.5%. IMF CR25/223, Table 1, PDF p.48. The 2026 Hormuz disruption raised shipping and insurance costs; preliminary staff still found inflation below 2% entering 2026 and projected about 2.3% for 2026. IMF mission statement, 2026-06-03. Low headline inflation may partly reflect administered prices, subsidies, import composition and high real rates; rent inflation remained 8% in May 2025. HIGH Article IV general status; no trade event scored Updated core/trimmed inflation, expectations and distributional housing-cost evidence would test whether the clean headline anchor masks material internal adjustment costs.
L2 Underlying order B The banking and monetary system remains strongly capitalized and profitable, but rapid credit growth, tighter funding and greater short-term external reliance create bounded, persistent strain. 2024 regulatory capital ratio 19.6%, NPL ratio 1.2%, ROA 2.2%; systemic vulnerabilities assessed low. IMF CR25/223, PDF pp.29, 53. Capital stayed near 20% and NPLs declined from 1.8% in 2022 to 1.2% in 2024, but private credit growth ran 12.6%, 10.0% and 13.4%; bank NFA turned negative in 2024 and external liabilities reached SAR486 billion by April 2025. PDF pp.30, 52–53. Preliminary 2026 staff judged banks well positioned for the regional-conflict shock because of strong capital and liquidity buffers, while supporting intensified monitoring and completion of resolution/ELA reforms. Statement 2026-06-03. Strong credit growth may represent productive Vision 2030 financial deepening rather than generalized overheating; SAMA stress tests show resilience to low oil prices and slowdown. HIGH Article IV general status Current bank-level maturity, currency and concentration data; updated NPLs; implemented ELA framework; and post-shock stress-test results could move the grade.
L3 Exchange order A The conventional peg remains credible and operationally well backed by SAMA reserves, with no reported exchange-market fracture or unsustainable reserve loss. SAR 3.75/USD; IMF calls the peg appropriate and credible. End-2024 SAMA NFA was $415 billion, about 15 months of imports and 187% of ARA. IMF CR25/223, PDF pp.28, 56–57. The rate remained 3.75; SAMA NFA moved from $440.5 billion in 2022 to $417.1 billion in 2023 and $414.5 billion in 2024, while import cover remained 18.2, 15.8 and 14.9 months. Table 1, PDF p.48. The peg has operated at 3.75 since 1986. During the 2026 regional conflict and shipping disruption, preliminary staff continued to describe the peg as a credible anchor supporting financial stability and reported ample reserves. Statement 2026-06-03. Reserve strength is partly oil-linked and the peg imports U.S. monetary conditions; declining import cover, persistent future deficits or costly internal adjustment could weaken the present call. PIF and Aramco assets are not treated as SAMA-usable reserves. HIGH Article IV/Manipulation status; TP-US-2026-SECTION122 and TP-GCC-2025-INTEGRATED-TARIFF linked but unscored Updated SAMA usable/encumbered reserve composition, intervention flows, forward positions and high-frequency peg-market pressure would test the A.
L4 External adjustment B The external position is broadly in line and backed by a very strong net asset position, but lower oil receipts, investment imports, falling public saving and rising external borrowing create a credible yet weakening adjustment path. 2024 external position “broadly in line”; CA −0.46% of GDP, staff CA gap −0.97% with a −3% to +1% range, NIIP about 59% of GDP, REER gap 4.8%. IMF CR25/223, PDF pp.56–57; 2025 ESR p.106 gives closely aligned −0.95%/4.7% estimates. CA: 12.1% of GDP in 2022, 2.9% in 2023, −0.5% in 2024; NIIP: 62.8%, 62.8%, 59.3%; external debt: 21.3%, 24.5%, 30.1%. IMF CR25/223, Tables 1 and 3, PDF pp.48, 51. OPEC+ cuts and a 6.2% terms-of-trade decline moved the 2024 CA into deficit. In the 2026 conflict shock, higher oil prices were preliminarily expected to offset shipment-volume losses and narrow 2026 deficits—cyclical support, not structural repair. Investment-linked imports can represent productive diversification, and commodity-model uncertainty is unusually large; strong sovereign external assets give substantial adjustment capacity. HIGH Article IV/Manipulation status; both trade events linked but unscored Detailed financial-flow and foreign-asset composition, current SAMA BOP/IIP series, and evidence that medium-term fiscal consolidation is occurring could move the call.
L5 Current-payment usability A The Fund’s current annex reports no MCP or restriction on bona fide current payments and transfers, while security restrictions remain separately classified. Article VIII §§2(a), 3 and 4 accepted; exchange system stated free of MCPs and current-payment/transfer restrictions. IMF CR25/223, Fund Relations as of 2025-05-31, PDF p.94. The frozen record shows an established Article VIII system and no contrary current-payment, MCP, arrears or undue-delay finding; the report also describes an open capital account. NOT-APPLICABLE to grading: the 2026 shipping disruption impaired physical trade routes, not documented FX availability or legal transfer of current payments. Security restrictions may affect particular transactions, and their notification/approval status is not public in the cited text; that does not establish generalized payment impairment. MEDIUM Article VIII §§2–4; security-restriction subcard; trade events not payment restrictions Current AREAER transaction-level coverage, bank transfer-delay data and exact security-measure scope would strengthen the persistence call.
L6 Systemic service A The riyal reliably performs its actual narrow role as the dominant domestic monetary and funding unit under a USD anchor, without being penalized for lacking a global reserve role. In 2024, foreign-currency shares were only 8.7% of bank assets, 9.1% of deposits and 9.4% of loans; domestic money, credit and deposits continued expanding. IMF CR25/223, Financial Soundness Indicators, PDF p.53. FX shares remained bounded over 2022–24: deposits 11.2%→9.8%→9.1%; loans 7.6%→8.2%→9.4%. The peg and predominantly riyal-based domestic intermediation continued through the period. Preliminary 2026 staff found the peg and banking buffers continued underpinning financial stability during the regional conflict; no currency-substitution or domestic service failure was reported. The available packet documents domestic intermediation better than retail/cross-border payment shares; stability partly reflects the credibility of the USD anchor rather than an autonomous international SAR role. MEDIUM No separate legal impairment; no trade event scored Direct SAR payment, invoice, settlement, hedge and cross-border-funding shares—and current dollarization measures—would test the full service claim.
