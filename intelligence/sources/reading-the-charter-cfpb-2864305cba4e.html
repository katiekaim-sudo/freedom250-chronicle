Reading the Charter — CFPB

The 10-point hunt

1. Immunities & liability shields. Nothing in §§5491, 5497, 5511, 5512 grants the Bureau or its Director personal immunity beyond ordinary federal-officer status. The real shield in this material is structural, not personal: §5491(a) plants the Bureau "in the Federal Reserve System" — inside a central bank that itself sits outside ordinary appropriations control — which is the load-bearing move, not a immunity clause in the classic IMF-Art.-IX sense.

2. Tax exemptions. None appear in the captured sections.

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause. §5491(b)(2)-(3): Director appointed by the President, Senate-confirmed, must be a U.S. citizen. §5491(c)(1): 5-year term. §5491(c)(3): removal "for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" — textbook for-cause removal protection, the single-Director version of the multi-member-commission model. Cornell's own editorial note flags the constitutional fight this triggered: Seila Law LLC v. CFPB (2020) held that for-cause removal protection on a single Director (as opposed to a multi-member board) violates separation of powers. The Court's fix was surgical — it severed the removal restriction, not the Bureau. Net effect: the text of §5491(c)(3) still says "for cause," but as a matter of constitutional law the President can remove the Director at will. The statute and the operative reality have diverged; the Bureau survived, the independence didn't.

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations — the crown jewel. §5497 is the single most important funding clause in the whole federal-entity library. Four moves stack on top of each other: - (a)(1)-(2): the Bureau doesn't ask Congress for money — the Federal Reserve Board of Governors transfers funds to it from "the combined earnings of the Federal Reserve System," capped as a percentage of the Fed's own 2009 operating expenses: 10% (FY2011), 11% (FY2012), 6.5% (FY2013 and each year after, inflation-adjusted). That 6.5% figure was cut from 12% by Pub. L. 119-21 (July 2025) — the first live edit to CFPB's funding architecture since Dodd-Frank, done through a reconciliation bill rather than a fight over the Bureau's existence. - (a)(2)(C) — the appropriations firewall: "the funds derived from the Federal Reserve System pursuant to this subsection shall not be subject to review by the Committees on Appropriations of the House of Representatives and the Senate." Congress's own appropriators are statutorily locked out of the Bureau's primary funding stream. - (c)(2) — the "not Government funds" clause: "Funds obtained by or transferred to the Bureau Fund shall not be construed to be Government funds or appropriated monies." This is the sentence that answers the question "whose money is this?" — the statute's own answer is: nobody's, in the ordinary appropriated sense. It's Fed money, waved through a legal door marked "not the government's." - (c)(3): those same funds are exempt from apportionment under 31 U.S.C. ch. 15 — the OMB apportionment machinery that constrains how every ordinarily-appropriated agency doles out its own money quarter to quarter doesn't touch the Bureau Fund either. - (e): there IS a small conventional-appropriations backstop — $200M/year authorized for FY2010-2014 if the Director determines Fed transfers are insufficient — but that money, unlike the Fed transfers, explicitly IS subject to ordinary apportionment (e)(3). The two funding streams are treated as legally distinct species.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs. §5497(a)(5): GAO/Comptroller General does audit the Bureau annually and reports to Congress — so there's no FOIA-style blackout — but note (a)(4)(F): the Bureau's financial statements "shall not be consolidated with the financial statements of either the Board of Governors or the Federal Reserve System," i.e., even though the money comes from the Fed, the accounting stays legally separate — two books, one pipe.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses. N/A in this material — an executive bureau, not a treaty body; Congress amends by ordinary legislation (as 2025's percentage cut shows).

7. Emergency powers. None in the captured sections.

8. Definitions section quietly load-bearing. Not captured in this pass (§5481, flagged as omitted) — but §5511(c)(4)'s cross-reference to "covered persons" and §5512(c)(9)'s "personally identifiable financial information" carve-out show the whole supervisory reach hinges on how broadly those terms get defined elsewhere — worth a follow-up capture.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution. Not addressed in the captured sections; the Bureau is a standing Executive-agency bureau, not a chartered corporation with a term.

10. Anything strange. §5512(b)(4)(B) — the "Deference" clause: Congress wrote into the statute itself that courts must treat the Bureau "as if it were the only agency authorized to apply, enforce, interpret, or administer" federal consumer financial law — a legislated instruction to the judiciary about how much deference to give, layered on top of whatever deference doctrine (Chevron, and its 2024 demise in Loper Bright) would otherwise supply. It's Congress trying to lock in interpretive supremacy for one agency by statute rather than by relying on judge-made deference rules — a belt-and-suspenders move that reads almost defensively, as if anticipating exactly the kind of court fight (Seila Law, and later challenges) the Bureau has, in fact, faced.

Layer map

  • 2010 — Dodd-Frank Title X / Consumer Financial Protection Act of 2010 (Pub. L. 111-203): creates the Bureau inside the Federal Reserve System, gives it a single Director with for-cause removal, and builds the Fed-transfer funding mechanism with the appropriations-review firewall.
  • 2020 — Seila Law LLC v. CFPB (Supreme Court): doesn't touch the text of §5491(c)(3), but strips its constitutional force — severs the for-cause removal protection rather than invalidating the Bureau, leaving the statute and the operative reality out of sync.
  • 2025 — Pub. L. 119-21, title III, §30001 (reconciliation act, signed July 4, 2025): cuts the permanent Fed-transfer funding cap from 12% to 6.5% of the Fed's 2009 operating-expense baseline — the first substantive edit to the funding architecture itself, done through ordinary budget reconciliation rather than a frontal assault on independence.

Entity Theory read

The CFPB is a creature built to dodge two leashes at once — Congress's purse and the President's removal power — and it's had one of those leashes cut back on it from each direction. Dodd-Frank's drafters hid the Bureau's money inside the Fed's own accounts specifically so no appropriations rider could ever starve it (§5497(a)(2)(C), (c)(2), (c)(3) are the receipts for that intent), and it worked — the funding firewall has held. But the other leash, the Director's removal protection, got cut by the Supreme Court in 2020: the President can now fire the Director at will, statute be damned. And in 2025 Congress found a second way to squeeze the Bureau without touching its independence directly — not by subjecting it to appropriations review (that door is still statutorily welded shut) but by simply shrinking the size of the pipe, cutting the percentage the Fed is allowed to hand over. So the CFPB survives as an entity, but the two doors built to make it un-capturable are both now ajar: whoever controls the presidency controls its Director, and whoever controls a reconciliation-bill majority in Congress can quietly turn down its water pressure — even though neither of them can touch its money through the front door of appropriations.
