NFDC Charter Deep Dive — The Missing Operating Constitution

Research contract

  • Question: What does the National Fraud Detection Center Charter actually establish, what complexity is probably carried by or beneath it, and how does it interact with Inspector General independence, federal-State data instruments and the transition to agency AI oversight?
  • Source standard: official federal statutes, memoranda, agency releases, privacy guidance, records guidance and published comparison instruments. Secondary reporting was used only as discovery and is not needed for the central findings.
  • Document-access test: exact-title and phrase searches across DOJ, the Fraud Division FOIA library, CIGIE/IGnet, Oversight.gov, inaugural-member OIG surfaces and public web indexes; inspection of the first-party releases and their linked PDFs.
  • Frozen cutoff: September 14, 2026, 6:04 p.m. EDT.
  • Critical limitation: the signed NFDC Charter, signature pages, version history and amendments were not located. This report therefore separates proved from public descriptions, strong structural inference, and unresolved clause at every step. It does not manufacture a shadow text.
  • Lifecycle boundary: Workbench deep research only. No records request, Chronicle promotion, watch, automation or external transmission is authorized by this file.

Answer first

Yes—the Charter is the highest-value missing instrument in the emerging fraud architecture. The deeper read changes the model in an important way:

The NFDC Charter appears to be the people, membership and operating- governance constitution for a prosecutor-led, multi-agency center. It is not publicly shown to be the legal authority for every dataset, technical feed, analytic system, investigative opening or downstream payment action.

Six Offices of Inspector General—Agriculture, Labor, Housing and Urban Development, Veterans Affairs, the Small Business Administration and Social Security Administration—publicly call themselves Charter signatories. Across their first-party releases, the common operative commitment is unusually consistent: dedicated analysts and investigators are assigned to a prosecutor-led effort, in a centralized collaborative space, to detect and dismantle complex cross-program fraud.123456 SSA adds the clearest description of the work: its analysts will fuse Social Security- specific intelligence with investigative data from across the federal enforcement community.1 DOL says it is placing investigative resources directly inside the NFDC.2 VA says its agents, analysts, auditors and inspectors gain shared tools, data and resources.5

That is more than branding. It proves an organized personnel layer. But it does not tell us:

  • who signed for DOJ and every participating body;
  • whether all inaugural members are Charter parties;
  • who can admit, suspend or remove a member;
  • who directs an embedded OIG employee day to day;
  • who may open, assign, decline, transfer or close a lead;
  • which information a signatory may see, query, retain or export;
  • how source corrections propagate into derived matches and leads;
  • who owns shared work product and federal records;
  • how privileges, grand-jury material and investigative sensitivities are segregated;
  • whether an analytic or AI system is named or merely contemplated;
  • who accepts model risk when one agency supplies data, another operates the model and a third takes action; or
  • what term, withdrawal, amendment, dispute and reporting rules apply.

Those are not minor details. They determine whether the NFDC is a coordination forum, a staffed lead factory, a shared investigative environment, a joint case-management body, or some bounded combination of them.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore two-part:

  1. The Charter is operationally consequential. It creates a durable human and governance bridge across legally independent OIGs and a DOJ-led center.
  2. The Charter is not the whole machine. Separate federal data-sharing agreements, State MOUs, technical annexes, Privacy Act/SORN authority, system authorization, records schedules and case-specific disclosure rules still control what information and decisions can lawfully move.

These are six independent first-party confirmations, not one DOJ sentence repeated by outside commentators.123456

DOJ separately names eighteen federal organizations or components as inaugural NFDC members: six non-OIG bodies and twelve OIGs or OIG investigative components. It also acknowledges twelve State offices or organizations.7 That roster proves membership as DOJ describes it. It does not prove that all thirty organizations signed the same Charter, that State partners signed it at all, or that every member contributed personnel or data on identical terms.

What was not found

No public copy of the Charter was located, including:

  • no signed PDF or accessible attachment;
  • no signature page or complete signatory schedule;
  • no issuance or effective date;
  • no version number, amendment or renewal history;
  • no membership application or accession form;
  • no termination or withdrawal clause;
  • no records, privacy, AI, security or dispute appendix; and
  • no cross-reference from the public releases to a controlling Charter file.

The Fraud Division's public FOIA library still says that the Division has no FOIA documents available and that the page will be updated as documents become available. The page itself was last updated April 29, before the August launch, so it cannot establish that DOJ has conducted a fresh Charter-specific publication review.8

The accurate statement is therefore not the Charter is secret or the Charter contains X. It is:

The Charter exists and has signatories, but its operative text was not publicly retrievable through the stated cutoff.

Where the Charter sits in the legal-operating stack

The public architecture contains at least seven distinct instrument layers. Collapsing them into the agreement hides the real control system.

Layer Public object What it can do What it cannot be assumed to do
1. Executive organization April 7 Attorney General memorandum Creates the administrative Fraud Division, orders DOJ realignment, directs creation and support of NFDC Bind independent OIGs, disclose every agency record, appropriate funds or authorize AI use
2. DOJ organization August priorities memorandum and organization chart Places NFDC inside the Fraud Division beside National Enforcement and District Fraud Counsel; shows a DOJ management chain Establish the supervision chain for non-DOJ OIG personnel
3. NFDC Charter Six first-party signatory statements Proves an organized membership/personnel commitment to a prosecutor-led center Prove exact data rights, case authority, technical systems, money or Charter clauses not published
4. Federal data agreements DOJ statement that fifteen agreements were announced Potentially authorizes defined federal data exchanges or recurring access Identify parties, datasets, matching terms or prove identity with Charter membership
5. State MOUs Ohio published model; Florida instruments announced Defines purpose, permitted use, source data, security, validation and sometimes cost rules Transfer every State dataset or make source filings true
6. Technical/system instruments Annex/SLA, PIA, SORN, ATO, records schedule Defines schema, frequency, users, security boundary, retention and operating system Create prosecutorial or program authority by itself
7. Case/action instruments Referral, investigative predicate, subpoena, court order, charging or administrative record Moves a lead toward evidence and a legally consequential action Convert an analytic lead directly into guilt, benefit ineligibility or collected recovery

The April 7 memorandum is especially revealing. It gives the Fraud Division operational control over named DOJ units and specifies internal DOJ personnel realignment. When it reaches the NFDC, however, the verb changes: the Division must coordinate with Justice Management, other DOJ components, law- enforcement agencies, Inspectors General and the White House Task Force to establish and support the Center.9 That textual shift is consistent with a federated formation rather than a simple DOJ command transfer.

The August Fraud Division chart then locates NFDC under a Deputy Assistant Attorney General branch, alongside the National Enforcement Section and District Fraud Counsel Section. Separate branches contain IT, Strategic Analysis, Litigation Support, Criminal Investigation and other support functions.10 This proves NFDC's place in the DOJ organization. It does not show whether those sibling support sections operate the NFDC platform, own its models or supervise an OIG detailee.

The most important constitutional seam: prosecutor-led does not erase IG independence

The Inspector General Act already supplies a strong legal basis for the collaboration. Under 5 U.S.C. § 404, an Inspector General conducts, supervises and coordinates investigations relating to its establishment; may coordinate relationships with federal, State, local and nongovernmental entities for fraud prevention, detection and prosecution; and must report expeditiously to the Attorney General when there are reasonable grounds to believe federal criminal law was violated.11

At the same time, 5 U.S.C. § 403 provides that an establishment IG reports to and is under the general supervision of the agency head or deputy, not another agency officer, and that the agency head cannot prevent or prohibit the IG from initiating, carrying out or completing an audit or investigation.12 Section 406 gives IGs substantial access and investigative authorities, including access to agency materials relating to programs they oversee.13

This creates a likely dual-control design:

  • DOJ/prosecutor leadership can own NFDC priorities, prosecutorial strategy, lead standards, legal advice, case deconfliction and the use of DOJ resources.
  • Each participating IG should retain statutory authority over its own office's investigations, employees, access authorities and independent reporting obligations.

That is an inference from the statutes and public structure, not a quote from the missing Charter. It is also the clause family that most needs the real text. Prosecutor-led could describe mission orientation, lead review or day-to-day tasking; it cannot silently repeal the IG Act.

The personnel commitment therefore raises concrete questions that press releases cannot answer:

Personnel question Why it matters
Are personnel detailed, assigned in place, collocated, seconded, or participating virtually? Determines physical/system access, duration and administrative paperwork
Full-time, part-time or case-specific? Determines whether dedicated means exclusive capacity or a portfolio responsibility
Who gives daily assignments? Separates NFDC operational tasking from statutory OIG supervision
Who rates performance, approves leave, disciplines and resolves conflicts? Preserves employer and independence boundaries
Who pays salary, travel, secure space and technology? Distinguishes an in-kind commitment from reimbursable funding or a transfer of funds
Which clearances and need-to-know determinations apply? Controls entry into restricted systems and case compartments
May a home OIG recall or withhold personnel from a matter? Determines withdrawal and mission-conflict rules
Does an embedded analyst speak for the OIG or only contribute expertise? Prevents participation from being mistaken for institutional concurrence

Some of these questions may be answered in individual personnel-detail or reimbursement instruments rather than the Charter. That possibility is itself part of the complexity: a short umbrella Charter could still sit above dozens of bilateral implementation records.

The data seam: access authority is not universal onward-disclosure authority

SSA's release proves that agency-specific intelligence will be fused with federal investigative data. VA refers to shared data and tools. PRAC says it will bring its data and analytical capabilities into the partnership.1514 Those statements establish an information-rich environment. They do not establish one pooled database or a universal right for every participant to see every source.

Three separate boundaries remain:

  1. Original access. An OIG may have statutory access to records of the program it oversees, but the authority under which it obtained a record still matters.
  2. Disclosure and reuse. A record about an individual held in a federal system of records is generally subject to the Privacy Act. Interagency law- enforcement sharing may proceed through a published compatible routine use or another statutory exception, but the purpose and recipient still matter.15
  3. Bulk matching. When a program qualifies as a Computer Matching Act program, 5 U.S.C. § 552a(o) requires a written matching agreement specifying purpose, authority, justification, data elements, estimated record volume and operating dates, among other terms.16

The public Ohio MOU is valuable precisely because it keeps these layers visible. It authorizes a recurring public corporate-data route, contemplates a separate technical annex or service-level agreement for automation, schema and frequency, imposes security and retention terms, requires DOJ to independently validate data before action, excludes Computer Matching Act programs unless an exemption applies, and states that the MOU obligates and transfers no funds.17

Nothing in the public record shows that the NFDC Charter supersedes those bilateral instruments. A stronger working model is:

Charter membership and people → source-specific data permission → technical delivery → controlled analytic use → human/case review

Each arrow needs its own owner and record.

Case, privilege and evidence seams

A centralized collaboration can mix several information states that remain legally non-interchangeable:

  • public corporate and payment records;
  • non-public program and benefit records;
  • OIG investigative intelligence;
  • law-enforcement-sensitive leads;
  • attorney work product and privileged communications;
  • evidence obtained by subpoena or warrant;
  • grand-jury matters; and
  • material cleared for federal-State disclosure.

Grand-jury information is a particularly hard boundary. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 6(e) generally imposes secrecy and permits disclosure only through specified exceptions. Government personnel may receive material when necessary to assist an attorney for the government in enforcing federal criminal law, while State and local disclosure may require a court-authorized route and specified purpose.18 A Charter can establish procedure and responsibility; it cannot create an exception that Rule 6(e) does not provide.

This is why the missing clauses on work-product ownership, compartmentation, case assignment, dissemination and deconfliction are central. Without them we cannot tell whether NFDC is primarily:

  • an early-stage lead-generation environment whose work stops before a case is opened;
  • a shared case-support environment after an investigative owner accepts a lead;
  • a prosecutor-directed national case-coordination center; or
  • different things at different stages.

DOJ publicly says the NFDC generates criminal leads for investigators and prosecutors, and that Florida's task force will use those leads.719 The word lead should therefore remain the public output state until a case- specific record proves the next transition.

Records custody is a constitutional issue, not housekeeping

Federal law requires agency heads to preserve adequate documentation of their organization, functions, policies, decisions, procedures and essential transactions, including information needed to protect the rights of the Government and directly affected persons.20 NARA's interagency-collaboration guidance says agencies should decide recordkeeping responsibility at the beginning, account for different retention schedules and make records available to participating agencies when one agency hosts the platform.21

For NFDC, at least five record families may coexist:

  1. the source agency record;
  2. the transferred extract or query response;
  3. the NFDC-derived match, graph edge, score or narrative;
  4. the analyst's review and disposition; and
  5. the receiving investigative, prosecutorial or program-action record.

A correction to family 1 does not automatically correct families 2 through 5. The Charter or its implementing records should identify:

  • the official copy and custodian for shared work;
  • which agency schedule applies to each derivative;
  • whether an OIG must preserve a copy in its own system;
  • how holds and litigation preservation work;
  • how access logs and analyst annotations are retained;
  • how a corrected source record triggers reprocessing and notice; and
  • which record can be reached by FOIA, Privacy Act access/correction or case discovery.

This is also where the user's massive overhaul before visible money movement thesis becomes strongest. Durable records, custody, repeatable data delivery and staffed review are infrastructure. They can be built long before a public ledger shows a payment stopped, a judgment entered or cash collected.

The AI-oversight transition: the Charter may create the organization before it identifies the use case

PRAC is an inaugural NFDC member and says it will continue bringing data and analytical capabilities into the Center. Its public operating record includes machine learning, predictive risk models, graph analysis and more than one billion records across more than sixty sources.1422 This proves that an AI/ML-capable member enters NFDC with mature tooling. It does not prove that PACE is the NFDC platform or that a particular PACE model uses Florida data.

OMB M-25-21 makes the missing ownership clauses more consequential. The policy defines high-impact AI by the effect of its output, not simply by whether a human remains in the loop. AI serving as a principal basis for law-enforcement risk assessments or suspect identification, detection of fraudulent use of government services, benefits decisions or penalties is presumed high-impact. Human oversight does not by itself remove that classification.23

For high-impact uses, agencies must perform and document predeployment testing, an impact assessment, ongoing monitoring, operator training, human oversight and appropriate remedies or appeals. A decision that a presumed use is not actually high-impact requires written documentation to the agency Chief AI Officer; waivers and determinations have centralized tracking and public- summary expectations subject to law and policy.23

The NFDC creates a difficult federated ownership problem:

Function Possible owner Unresolved accountability question
Source data SSA, VA, USDA, State or another program/data steward Who attests provenance, quality, legal use and correction?
Analytic platform DOJ, PRAC or another contributing component Which ATO, PIA, SORN, inventory and records schedule govern?
Model/rules Platform owner or contributing analytic team Who validates performance, thresholds, subgroup effects and drift?
Lead acceptance NFDC prosecutor, OIG supervisor or investigative agency What makes a flag a lead and who can decline it?
Investigation OIG, FBI, HSI, IRS-CI or other law-enforcement owner Which legal predicate and evidentiary standard apply?
Benefit/payment action Program or payment agency Is the NFDC output a principal basis, and which CAIO owns that use case?
Charge/civil filing DOJ or State prosecutor What independent evidence and legal review support filing?
Remedy/correction Source agency, system owner and action owner How can one correction reach every derivative and downstream decision?

The public DOJ AI inventory was published before the NFDC launch and contains no NFDC-specific use identified in the prior review.24 No NFDC PIA, SORN, ATO, system identifier, model card, high-impact determination or waiver was located in this pass. The Charter cannot substitute for those records. But if it assigns platform ownership, analytic authority or decision roles, it may be the instrument that reveals which agency's AI governance machinery must activate first.

The strongest current interpretation is therefore:

The Charter institutionalizes the cross-agency human network into which AI- capable tools can be inserted. M-25-21 governs the consequential use of those tools at the agency/use-case level. The missing joint is the accountable owner when data, model, reviewer and adverse action belong to different agencies.

What the Charter would change if its text is narrow versus broad

Three plausible forms remain open. They should be treated as mutually exclusive hypotheses until the text resolves them.

Hypothesis A — narrow membership charter

The Charter names purpose, membership, leadership, personnel commitments and meeting/governance procedures, while every sensitive function is delegated to separate agreements and existing law.

Implication: the main overhaul is institutional durability and collocation; the operating machine lives in bilateral instruments and systems records.

Hypothesis B — operational lead-center charter

The Charter also governs analytic projects, lead standards, deconfliction, work-product custody, case referral and access roles while still relying on source-specific data authority.

Implication: the Charter is the real lead-production constitution and the most important missing accountability object.

Hypothesis C — broad shared-environment charter

The Charter purports to govern common data access, shared technology, analytics, dissemination, records and operational control across participants.

Implication: privacy, records, AI ownership, OIG independence and system authorization become much more acute; the Charter would need careful limiting language and substantial implementing documentation.

Current public language supports A or B more comfortably than C. The existence of separate federal and State data agreements argues against treating the Charter as a universal data license. But the references to integrated analysts, shared tools and direct placement inside NFDC make a merely ceremonial Charter unlikely.

Ruling on the overhaul thesis

The deeper Charter inquiry strengthens the groundwork-first thesis, with an important refinement.

The overhaul is not one new database and not yet a public money-recovery ledger. It is a federated operating constitution assembled in layers:

  1. DOJ reorganizes and centralizes prosecutorial fraud capacity.
  2. A Charter recruits legally independent OIGs into a staffed collaborative center.
  3. Federal and State agreements normalize recurring source access.
  4. Technical and privacy instruments make specific data uses executable.
  5. Existing AI/ML-capable partners bring analytic capacity into the formation.
  6. NFDC generates leads, which separate agencies must corroborate and accept.
  7. Investigators, prosecutors, program agencies, courts and collection owners move later clocks.

That architecture can be institutionally massive before it produces visible cash movement. The first observable outputs should therefore be documents and capacity signals—charters, accessions, assignments, agreements, annexes, system records, staffing, inventories and lead-handling protocols—not a clean recovery total.

The Charter is pivotal because it may tell us who can make the network act. It is not sufficient because it cannot, by itself, make every underlying record shareable, every model lawful, every match correct or every lead actionable.

Exact return objects

These are research-return objects, not active watches or authorized requests:

  1. Executed NFDC Charter, all signature pages, exhibits, appendices, amendments, accession forms and version history.
  2. Complete distinction among Charter parties, inaugural members, embedded personnel, data providers, State partners and observers.
  3. OIG personnel assignment/detail instruments, including reimbursement, supervision, performance, recall and conflict terms.
  4. NFDC governance and lead-handling SOPs: intake, prioritization, deconfliction, acceptance, referral, decline and closure.
  5. Fifteen federal data-sharing agreements and any data dictionaries, technical annexes, correction terms and access matrices.
  6. NFDC architecture/system owner, ATO, PIA, SORN mapping, records schedule and incident-response plan.
  7. NFDC-specific AI inventory row, high-impact determination or written non-high-impact determination, impact assessment, validation and monitoring records.
  8. Records/FOIA custody memorandum and source-to-derivative correction procedure.
  9. One lineage packet from source record through NFDC lead, corroboration, investigative owner, action, adjudication and collected recovery.

No request has been filed. If a records request is later authorized, the first request should be modular: Charter and accession records first; personnel and governance instruments second; data/system/AI records third. That keeps a broad system-record request from obscuring the threshold question of what the Charter itself does.
