OCC–FDIC Supervisory Intervention Framework — Factual Analysis
In plain English
Question: What did the OCC and FDIC change on August 27, 2026 about when examiners can demand fixes or take enforcement action against a bank?
Answer: The OCC and FDIC tied enforcement to material financial harm; the Fed did not join.
Evidence checked through: 2026-08-27T17:55:00-04:00
The detailed record below keeps the legal wording, source limits and open questions.
Short answer
The August 27 package rewrites the escalation ladder used by the OCC and FDIC:
when an examiner may communicate a nonbinding observation, issue a mandatory
matter requiring attention (MRA), or support an enforcement action for an
unsafe or unsound practice.
The cleanest description is a supervisory intervention constitution. The
agencies now require a stated causal bridge from imprudent conduct to material
financial harm or Deposit Insurance Fund risk, while retaining a lower,
forward-looking threshold for MRAs. The framework should reduce process-only
MRAs, but it increases the importance and contestability of examiner judgments
about foreseeability, materiality and the financial consequences of governance,
cyber, operational and consumer weaknesses.

The Federal Reserve is not a co-issuer. The rule therefore does not create a
single uniform standard for every federally supervised banking organization.
Joint final-rule standards
Unsafe or unsound practice
A practice, act or failure to act—alone or together with others—must be
contrary to generally accepted standards of prudent operation and either:
if continued, be likely to materially harm the institution’s financial
condition or present a material risk of loss to the Deposit Insurance Fund;
or
already have materially harmed the institution’s financial condition.
The agencies declined to reduce “likely” or “material” to a numeric threshold.
Matter requiring attention
An MRA may issue when:
imprudent conduct, if continued, could reasonably be expected, under
current or reasonably foreseeable conditions, to cause material financial
harm or material DIF-loss risk—or already caused material harm; or
there is an actual violation of a banking or banking-related law or
regulation.
The MRA probability threshold is deliberately lower than the enforcement
threshold. An examiner does not have to wait for actual loss.
Financial condition
The final rule defines harm to financial condition as losses or other negative
effects on capital, asset quality, earnings, liquidity or sensitivity to market
risk. Reputation, governance, policy, documentation, cyber, operational and
consumer concerns do not disappear, but they must reach the material-financial
bridge or an applicable legal-violation bridge to support an MRA.
Tailoring
As a bank’s size, complexity, activities, capital structure or other financial
risk increases:
the threshold for materiality decreases;
analysis becomes more granular, including by business line, product or
service; and
remediation demands and prudent-operation expectations increase.
The inverse applies to lower-risk institutions. This is therefore not uniform
leniency for large banks.
Supervisory observations and other violations
A supervisory observation is nonbinding and below the MRA threshold. It does
not require board presentation, corrective action, an action plan or examiner
tracking. It cannot become an MRA solely because the bank declined to act, but
its underlying facts may affect ratings and may support a later MRA if the
standard is independently met.
An “other violation” is an actual banking-related violation for which the
agency issues neither an enforcement action nor an MRA. Correction may still
be required, but the agency generally may not prescribe the method or impose
unrelated remediation unless another law requires it.
Immediate OCC policy layer
Revised PPM 5400-11 gives the final-rule framework an immediate operating
shape. Among other things, it:
reserves MRAs for material financial risks and, as a matter of OCC
discretion, substantive violations;
requires objective facts, sound reasoning and the Five Cs—concern, cause,
consequence, corrective action and commitment;
defines a repeat MRA as recurrence within three years after the original MRA
was corrected, validated and closed;
restricts broad lookbacks and independent consultants;
permits substantial reliance on satisfactory internal-audit validation; and
directs closure after implementation and effectiveness validation rather
than delaying closure solely to test long-term sustainability.
OCC Bulletin 2026-41 says revised PPM 5310-3 adds escalation, tailoring and
focus; generally permits remediation before enforcement; terminates actions at
“substantial compliance”; changes individual minimum-capital-ratio policy; and
deletes the former persistent-weakness appendix. At the cutoff, however, the
bulletin’s PPM 5310-3 link still resolved to the May 2023 document. Those
claimed revisions are therefore sourced to the bulletin, not to a falsely
treated revised manual.
Separate OCC violations proposal
The same-day OCC-only proposal would replace the final rule’s policy-level
“other violation” treatment with codified “substantive” and “technical”
violations.
A substantive violation would have to be capable of meaningfully affecting the
institution or its customers and satisfy at least one of five categories:
systemic or a pattern;
direct, clear, predictable and more-than-minimal financial-condition impact;
more-than-minimal impact on books and records;
more-than-minimal restitution or adverse customer impact; or
insider misconduct or self-dealing.
A technical violation would not support an enforcement action or MRA. The OCC
could require correction but generally could not prescribe the method, demand
an action plan or track completion. Multiple technical violations could later
support a substantive finding.
The proposal is not effective law. Its comment deadline remains unfixed until
Federal Register publication.
Legal and supervisory seams
The joint regulation permits an MRA for any actual covered violation, while
the preamble and OCC manual say OCC discretion will focus on substantive
violations. The separate proposal confirms that the narrower rule-text
bridge is not final.
“Likely,” “material,” the time horizon and prudent-operation standards remain
judgment terms rather than numeric rules.
Ratings remain a pressure channel even when a concern is only an observation
or other violation.
Application, charter, notice and licensing conditions remain separate and
independently enforceable under 12 USC 1818.
Institution-affiliated parties were removed from the final rule’s scope and
remain governed by prior enforcement standards.
The final text applies prospectively and does not limit agency rulemaking.
The proposal used OCC docket OCC-2025-0174; the final cover says
OCC-2026-0174. The Federal Register object must settle the canonical docket
metadata.
SVB stress test
The final preamble invokes Silicon Valley Bank to argue that examiners should
have concentrated on foreseeable financial risk rather than process. SVB was a
Federal Reserve-supervised state member bank, so this OCC–FDIC rule would not
have governed its primary supervision.
The Federal Reserve’s review found that governance, liquidity modeling,
interest-rate risk, internal audit, deposit segmentation and contingency
funding weaknesses were pathways into the financial failure. The new rule can
help if it forces examiners to articulate that causal chain early. It can harm
if leading indicators remain nonbinding observations while supervisors wait
for stronger balance-sheet evidence. The decisive question is implementation,
not the labels alone.
Freedom 250 fit
Primary plotline: Economy. Secondary plotline: Crypto.
The package adds the prudential-supervision membrane to the new financial
perimeter:
Treasury/GENIUS addresses the stablecoin liability and issuer perimeter;
SEC and CFTC actions address offerings, products, venues, collateral,
clearing and market conduct;
OCC chartering determines entry into the federal banking perimeter; and
this package determines when a prudential supervisor may compel a bank
inside that perimeter to change course.
It does not grant a charter, stablecoin authorization, Federal Reserve account,
custody permission, collateral eligibility, eligible-depository treatment,
clearing approval or funded transfer. Its crypto relevance is indirect but
real: it changes the supervisory conditions under which lawful crypto and
stablecoin banking activities may be challenged or allowed to continue.
It complements the April 10 OCC–FDIC reputation-risk final rule without
duplicating it. April removed reputation risk as an operative supervisory
basis. August narrows the broader escalation machinery by which an examiner’s
concern becomes an MRA or unsafe-or-unsound-practice enforcement case.
Exact return gates
Federal Register publication, document number, citation and effective date
for the joint final rule;
Federal Register publication and controlling comment deadline for the
separate violations proposal;
a valid public copy of revised PPM 5310-3;
final, withdrawn or reproposed disposition of the violations proposal;
alignment or divergence across OCC, FDIC and Federal Reserve supervisory
standards;
observed movement from MRAs into ratings, charter conditions or other
written conditions;
public evidence on MRA counts, classification reversals, remediation costs,
delayed losses and enforcement timing; and
the first material digital-asset banking case applying the new framework.
