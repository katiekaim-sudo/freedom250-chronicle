Crypto & Digital Money

Money is moving onto new digital networks. Stablecoins, tokenized money and crypto networks: what is law, what is live, how it really works, and who controls it.

Money is moving onto new digital networks piece by piece, not all at once: the dollar stays in charge, private companies are taking over the pipes, and almost nothing has yet been proven in a crisis.

Research hub · findings as of 2026-10-03. Each answer opens to the findings and the study that proves it.

The questions

What makes a legal stablecoin trustworthy

Under the GENIUS Act there is no single stamp that makes a stablecoin "trusted": the issuer's approval, the coin's code and keys, its reserves, the platforms selling it and the fixes for mistakes each have to be proven separately.

What we found

  • The GENIUS Act is law, but most of the rules to carry it out were still only proposed as of August 17, 2026. No approved U.S. issuer, state certification or registered foreign issuer had been found.
  • Legal approval and technical control are two separate locks. A regulator's approval doesn't prove who holds the keys that mint, freeze or burn coins, and a valid digital signature doesn't prove the signer had legal authority.
  • The blockchain shows how many coins exist, but reserves sit off-chain with banks and custodians. Matching them takes bank records, a monthly accounting-firm exam and CEO/CFO sign-off, and none of that can prove there's no hidden debt or side deal.
  • A "bridged" or "wrapped" copy of a coin on another blockchain shouldn't count as approved just because it has the same name. Who controls it and who owes redemption have to be checked first.
  • Freezing coins on a government or court order is three separate questions: can the code do it, is there a valid order, and was it actually done and logged? Treasury has only asked, not required, that sellers test this.

Why it matters

The GENIUS Act doesn't put law on a blockchain. It makes named companies, their coin-control keys, their reserves and the platforms that sell their coins answerable to federal law, which pulls private money inside the regulatory system one approval at a time.

What to watch

  • pending · The first named issuer approval, state-regime certification or foreign issuer registration under GENIUS. None had been found as of August 17, 2026.
  • pending · Whether Treasury's final rule makes sellers check stablecoin code for seize, freeze and burn functions. Its August 17 proposal only asks the question.

How Congress is building the new money plumbing

A big rebuild of U.S. financial plumbing is under way, but not as one bill: Congress, a dozen agencies and market utilities like DTC are each moving separate pieces that are converging.

What we found

  • DTC ran real but limited production trades with tokenized securities on July 15, involving more than 30 firms, while keeping the official record of who holds what. The wider service for participants is scheduled for October, not launched.
  • GENIUS (stablecoins) cleared all four committees and became law. CLARITY (crypto market structure) passed the House on July 17, 2025, but Senate Banking and Senate Agriculture advanced different versions that still need to be reconciled.
  • The PACE bill (H.R. 8395) would let nonbank payment firms register with the OCC and then separately apply for a Fed account to use Fedwire, FedNow and FedACH. It's only a bill, with no automatic Fed access or bank charter.
  • The American Reserve Modernization Act (H.R. 8957) would turn the executive-order Bitcoin reserve into law, with a 20-year holding floor and quarterly proof of reserves. It was introduced but not advanced; its gold-revaluation idea is only a study.
  • The House task force on monetary policy and Treasury markets was reauthorized, effective July 23, 2026, so its hearing series continues into a second round.

Why it matters

This is the legislative assembly line: stablecoins, nonbank payment firms and a federal Bitcoin reserve are being written into statute and handed to existing agencies like the OCC, Fed, SEC and Treasury, not built outside them.

What to watch

  • October 2026 · DTC's scheduled launch of its tokenization service for participants (announced target, not yet live).
  • 2026-10-20 · Treasury's written answers to the House committee after the September 15 hearing.
  • pending · Senate Banking and Senate Agriculture reconciling their versions of CLARITY.
  • pending · GENIUS final rules, the exact effective date and the first approved stablecoin issuer.

What stablecoins reveal, and what they still hide

Public-chain stablecoins let anyone see transfers, but what makes money trustworthy (who backs it, who can redeem, who can freeze it, what happens after fraud or failure) still sits with institutions behind the screen.

What we found

  • Every easier way to pay (checks, cards, Apple Pay) moved complexity into a rule-bound back office. Cards made payments visible to operators, not the public; Apple Pay hides your card number but keeps the same card system.
  • Public blockchains can show addresses, balances, transfers and supply changes. They usually don't show who really holds the coins, which banks hold the reserves, who can redeem, the total conversion cost or what legal remedies exist.
  • Stablecoins genuinely speed up transfers inside the same coin, chain and custody setup. Complexity comes back at bank entry and exit, currency conversion, cross-chain moves, key recovery, sanctions screening, refunds and issuer failure.
  • To earn trust like cash, checks and cards did, stablecoins must answer seven questions: who owes you, what backs it, who can redeem and when, total cost, when payment is final, who can freeze it, and what happens after failure.
  • Japan and Korea's pilots favor visibility for banks and regulators over public visibility. Korea's Project Hangang has no public ledger, and Japan's JPYSC stays inside verified accounts, with public-chain use still in the future.

Why it matters

The public ledger is new, but the rules that make the money trustworthy are still being set by issuers, banks and regulators. The open question is whether those rules become as visible as the ledger, or whether a transparent ledger just fronts another private system.

SEC opens a trial lane for tokenized stocks

On September 17, 2026 the SEC issued a live five-year order letting approved participants trade tokenized versions of listed stocks through automated pools on public blockchains. No such venue is running yet, and custody, clearing and settlement are left to others.

What we found

  • The SEC's order (Release 34-106402) took effect September 17, 2026 and runs through September 17, 2031. It lets new "Tokenized Securities Venues" trade tokenized listed stocks through automated pools on a public blockchain without registering as an exchange.
  • The chain is open but access isn't. The venue chooses who trades, sets fees and can pause or upgrade the contracts. Firms may supply liquidity with their own money without registering as dealers.
  • A tokenized stock can trade against another tokenized stock, a tokenized money market fund or any non-security crypto asset. That opens a door for coins like XRP or RLUSD, but none is named or selected.
  • Securities law, shareholder rights and trading halts still apply, and a company can block a third-party token of its stock by objecting within 30 days. The order doesn't cover share issuance, official records, custody, clearing or legal settlement finality.
  • Separately, a March 2026 SEC interpretation set categories for which crypto assets are securities, and nine nonbinding staff FAQs on September 25 applied it without changing it.

Why it matters

The SEC is using exemption powers it already has, with no new law from Congress, to carve a time-limited trading lane for tokenized stocks, while official records, issuer rights and securities law stay with the traditional institutions.

What to watch

  • pending · The first venue's public notice, which must be posted at least 30 days before it can start trading. None was operating as of September 25.
  • pending · Which tokens, if any, get paired in actual pools. XRP, RLUSD and every other named token are still unselected.
  • pending · The comment deadline, and any SEC move to make the exemption permanent or widen it.
  • 2031-09-17 · The order expires unless the SEC changes or extends it.

SEC proposes blockchain-based official shareholder records

On September 1, 2026 the SEC proposed letting a blockchain serve as all or part of a company's official shareholder list, as long as one registered transfer agent stays legally responsible for it.

What we found

  • The proposal would require an electronic master shareholder file and allow it to run across several linked systems, including one or more blockchains. It is proposed, not final: no final rule and no effective date exist.
  • Whatever technology is used, one registered transfer agent must stay responsible for, and in exclusive control of, each class of shares. The SEC is regulating who answers for the record, not picking a chain.
  • New reporting and procedure rules would come with it: firms must disclose tokenization and outside tech vendors, adopt compliance procedures, and handle restricted-share legends. The SEC also asks how corrections work on a blockchain that can't be edited.
  • Ripple, Securitize and Wormhole are separate players with separate roles. Ripple's investments and partnerships don't make it anyone's transfer agent, and Wormhole just moves data between chains; it doesn't own or keep the record.
  • Several chains can coexist, with one legally responsible record sitting above them. Other countries follow a similar pattern: shared technology, but legal title and money stay under local control.

Why it matters

The SEC isn't banning or blessing crypto here; it is rewriting a decades-old recordkeeping rulebook so a blockchain can quietly become part of the legal shareholder ledger. That is the old system rebuilding its own plumbing through ordinary rulemaking.

SEC advisers' tokenization advice versus actual SEC action

The SEC's Investor Advisory Committee told the SEC in March 2026 not to grant a blanket tokenization exemption; the SEC's September 17 order carried several similar limits but never cites the committee.

What we found

  • The committee gives advice only; it can't approve a venue, token, chain or rule. On March 12, 2026 it voted 12–0 (two abstaining) on tokenized stock: no blanket exemption, public comment first, and three protections kept.
  • Those protections: holders must be told what they actually own (votes, dividends, rights in bankruptcy), middlemen must stay regulated, and orders must still get the best price. It also warned third-party 'wrapped' tokens may not carry full shareholder rights.
  • The SEC's September 17 order is temporary and conditional, running through 2031, and was effective immediately with comments requested afterward. Commissioner Peirce engaged directly with the committee's draft, but this is similarity, not proven adoption.
  • The committee pushed back on a live SEC proposal: it recommended keeping mandatory quarterly (10-Q) reports rather than letting companies switch to twice-yearly reporting.
  • March and September meetings also discussed a more machine-readable SEC filing system (EDGAR) as input for AI. The study treats that as a working theory, not an announced SEC plan.

Why it matters

Tokenized stock is entering U.S. markets through the SEC's own machinery: an advisory committee drafts the guardrails, then the Commission issues an order in the same shape. That is institutions rewriting themselves from inside, step by procedural step.

Apps can now sell regulated derivatives without registering

On September 17, 2026 CFTC staff said any qualifying 'passive' software app (crypto or not) can market regulated derivatives and earn fees without registering as a broker, while the trades stay inside registered firms.

What we found

  • Staff Letter 26-25 takes relief first given only to the crypto wallet Phantom in March and opens it to all qualifying software providers, expressly including non-crypto apps.
  • Such apps can show prices, send users' orders to a registered exchange or broker, promote specific contracts (including event contracts and perpetuals), refer users to named firms, and take revenue share or per-trade fees.
  • Limits: the app can't hold customer money or assets, give buy/sell signals, or choose how orders are routed or executed. Registered exchanges, brokers and clearinghouses still sign up the customer and hold the collateral.
  • Strings attached: ten conditions including disclosures, broker-style marketing rules, recordkeeping, a filed notice, and shared legal liability between the app and each partner firm.
  • It's a staff promise not to recommend enforcement, not a Commission rule. It is not a DeFi safe harbor, a spot-crypto framework, or permission for AI trading agents. As of the cutoff, no app had been found using it.

Why it matters

Two days after the Senate stalled the CLARITY crypto bill (49–50 on September 15), CFTC staff used existing powers to widen who can sell regulated derivatives; the study says the timing fits but doesn't prove cause. Access spreads out to apps, while accounts, custody and enforcement stay with registered firms.

What makes a record official on a blockchain

A record is not official because it sits on a blockchain; it is official because a law or regulator gives it a specific job, a named institution is responsible for it, and something legally follows from it.

What we found

  • "Official record" is not one legal status. A record can be official for one purpose (say, regulatory compliance) and carry no weight for another (say, ownership or audit). Paper, a database or a blockchain can all qualify if the rules allow it.
  • On September 24, CFTC staff said covered firms can keep required records natively on a blockchain, without a duplicate off-chain copy just because blockchain is used. It is staff guidance, not a Commission rule, and the firm stays fully responsible.
  • Firms using a public blockchain must still be able to produce their records if the network or block explorer goes down. The required record also includes the data needed to find, read and explain it, not just the transaction itself.
  • SEC staff goes further: a registered transfer agent may use a blockchain as its official Master Securityholder File, which decides registered holders. The related September 2026 SEC transfer-agent rule is proposed, not final.
