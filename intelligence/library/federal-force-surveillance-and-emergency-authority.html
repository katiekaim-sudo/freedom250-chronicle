Who can watch, use force, freeze and spend: a map of the legal levers

A set of maps that keeps apart a legal power, an issued order, a court ruling and what is actually being done, across surveillance, domestic force, war powers, emergency economic powers and how far court orders reach.

What we found

  • Surveillance: the study records FISA Title VII (which holds Section 702) as repealed on June 12, 2026, and treats leftover approvals as a separate matter. The 2026 surveillance court certification was not retrieved.
  • Domestic force: the Guard was called up under 10 U.S.C. section 12406; the Insurrection Act has not been used; the Supreme Court denied a stay in case 25A443.
  • War powers on Iran: there is no enacted authorization for use of force. H.Con.Res.86 is a concurrent resolution, not a law, and the 60-day War Powers clock was not computed.
  • Emergency economic powers: IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, but its power to block property remains. EO 14389 ended the duties, not the emergencies, and Proclamation 11012 rests on a different law, Trade Act section 122.
  • Courts and officers: Trump v. CASA limited nationwide relief as a procedure question. A related map finds FTC commissioners' for-cause protection falling while a Fed governor's protection stands on a stay. Work paused after the sixth batch on August 15, 2026.

Why it matters

These maps track who holds control by reading the binding documents. Much of the change runs through existing statutes, executive orders and court remedies rather than new laws, which is the procedural route the thesis describes.

What to watch

  • pending · Retrieval of the 2026 surveillance court certification and the status of any leftover Section 702 approvals.
  • pending · Whether a 60-day War Powers clock is triggered for Iran actions, and any vote on an authorization.
  • pending · Any use of the Insurrection Act.
