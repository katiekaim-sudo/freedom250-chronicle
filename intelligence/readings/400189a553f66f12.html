United States Name and Passport Typography — Legal-Entity Audit
In plain English
Question: Does changing the capitalization of United States of America create a different legal entity, especially on a U.S. passport?
Answer: The passport's authority comes from federal law and State Department issuance, not the capitalization on its cover.
Evidence checked through: 2026-10-04T12:00:00-04:00
The detailed record below keeps the legal wording, source limits and open questions.
Question
Does United States of America name a different legal entity from
UNITED STATES OF AMERICA or UNITED STATES of AMERICA, particularly when
the capitalized form appears on a United States passport?
Short answer
No. Capitalization alone does not create, identify or select a second legal
entity. The words may be set in title case, display capitals or a machine-
readable format without changing the sovereign or governmental actor they
denote.
There is a real distinction behind some of the surrounding argument, but it is
not typographic. United States can refer, depending on the controlling text,
to the sovereign nation, the federal government, the several States
collectively, federal territory, or specified federal agencies and
instrumentalities. The source's definition, statute, capacity and context do
that work. Letter case does not.
What the passport establishes
The Department of State says that it created the 2026 commemorative United
States passport and describes its cover in ordinary title case as bearing
United States of America. The pictured cover renders the country name in
display capitals. That is the same label in two typographic treatments on the
same official page, not evidence of two issuers.
The controlling legal actor is fixed elsewhere. Under 22 U.S.C. § 211a, the
Secretary of State may grant and issue passports, may designate specified
officials and systems to do so, and acts on behalf of the United States. GPO
produces United States passports for the Department of State. Design and
manufacture are therefore distinct from legal issuance, and none of those
functions creates a new sovereign merely by changing the case of the cover
lettering.
The machine-readable parts of a passport are also formatted to technical
standards. ICAO Doc 9303 prescribes standardized country codes and character
fields for machine-readable travel documents. A machine format is an identity-
matching and interoperability layer, not a rule of entity formation.
Why the capitalization theory fails
On October 1, 2026, the Tenth Circuit addressed the same claimed mechanism in a
person-name case. The appellant argued that an all-capital name was an entity
distinct from the natural person. The court held that the wrong-legal-person
theory lacked merit and cited its earlier rejection of the claim that an
all-capital rendering failed to identify the same person. The case concerns a
human name rather than the country's name, but it directly rejects the claimed
legal operation of capitalization.
Official printing practice points the same way. The GPO Style Manual describes
itself as a printer's stylebook for uniform word and type treatment, not a law
of juridical identity. Capitals, small capitals and title case can carry visual
hierarchy or satisfy document and data conventions without creating a second
person.
The genuine legal distinctions nearby
1. The historic name
Article I of the Articles of Confederation states: The Stile of this
confederacy shall be, "The United States of America." That supplies the
historic formal style. The document itself also varies the capitalization of
United States throughout its text. The variance does not multiply the
confederacy into separate legal persons.
2. Context-specific definitions
Federal law routinely defines United States for a particular title, chapter
or regulation. One current regulation, for example, says for that part that
United States or U.S. means the United States of America and then specifies
the geographic scope. Other provisions use narrower or broader definitions.
The introductory scope words—such as as used in this chapter—are legally
load-bearing; capitalization is not.
3. Section 3002 does not create an all-caps corporation
28 U.S.C. § 3002 begins As used in this chapter—chapter 176, the federal debt-
collection procedure chapter. Its paragraph (15) defines United States to
include a federal corporation, agency, department, commission, board, other
entity or instrumentality so those governmental claimants fit the chapter's
collection machinery. It does not declare that the nation was newly
incorporated, assign an all-caps corporate name, or distinguish
UNITED STATES from United States.
4. The 1871 corporation was the District of Columbia
The District of Columbia Organic Act of 1871 constituted the District of
Columbia a body corporate for municipal purposes, with municipal powers to
contract and to sue or be sued. The statute did not incorporate the United
States under an all-caps name. This confirms the Library's earlier USA Inc.
debunking and narrows it: even where Congress deliberately creates a body
corporate, it does so with operative text naming the body and its powers—not by
typography.
Ruling for the Library
Treat United States of America, UNITED STATES OF AMERICA,
UNITED STATES of AMERICA, United States and U.S. as potentially different
renderings or abbreviations, not automatically different entities.
For any particular instrument, ask instead:
Who issued or signed it?
Under what statute, regulation, charter or delegated authority?
How does that controlling text define United States for its own scope?
In what capacity is the government acting—sovereign, regulator, creditor,
proprietor, litigant, agency or instrumentality?
What legal consequence follows from the instrument itself?
A different answer to one of those questions can identify a different legal
actor or capacity. A different font or capitalization cannot.
