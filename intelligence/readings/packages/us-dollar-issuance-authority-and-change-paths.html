# The U.S. dollar issuance constitution — who gave the Fed its role and how it can change

**Question:** How does the Federal Reserve have a national role over U.S. money and payments; what laws govern that role; and how could the United States change who issues dollars or what form the dollar takes?

**Scope:** United States constitutional and federal statutory authority; the dollar unit, coins, Federal Reserve notes, Treasury notes, Reserve Bank balances, commercial-bank deposits, payment stablecoins and a possible retail CBDC. Primary official sources checked through September 20, 2026 at 19:00 EDT.

**Bottom line:** The Federal Reserve's role is not a private agreement, treaty or surrender of U.S. monetary sovereignty. It is a **statutory operating arrangement created by Congress** under Congress's constitutional money, borrowing, commerce and necessary-and-proper powers. The United States already issues its sovereign currency through a divided public architecture: Congress defines the legal system; Treasury mints coins and physically prints notes; the Board orders and authorizes Federal Reserve notes; the twelve Reserve Banks place those notes into circulation and issue central-bank account balances; and private banks issue redeemable deposit claims denominated in dollars.

Congress remains the ultimate legal architect. It can amend the Federal Reserve Act and Title 31, move functions, change legal-tender or issuance rules, authorize a new form of public money, restrict one, or dissolve the Reserve Banks by ordinary federal legislation—subject to constitutional protections, outstanding obligations and the practical need for a transition. The President cannot do that alone by executive order, and the Federal Reserve cannot rewrite its own statutory foundation.

## 1. The constitutional source

The Constitution places national monetary power principally with **Congress**, not with the President, Treasury or Federal Reserve acting independently.

| Constitutional provision | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Article I, §8, cl. 2** | Congress may borrow money on the credit of the United States. |
| **Article I, §8, cl. 5** | Congress may coin money, regulate its value and the value of foreign coin, and fix standards of weights and measures. |
| **Article I, §8, cl. 6** | Congress may punish counterfeiting of U.S. securities and coin. |
| **Article I, §8, cl. 3** | Interstate and foreign commerce power supports national banking and payment regulation. |
| **Article I, §8, cl. 18** | Congress may enact laws necessary and proper to execute its enumerated powers. |
| **Article I, §10, cl. 1** | States may not coin money, emit bills of credit or make anything but gold and silver coin a tender in payment of debts. |

The Supreme Court's foundational national-bank case, *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819), held that Congress may create a national bank as an appropriate means of executing its enumerated fiscal and monetary powers even though the Constitution does not expressly say “create a bank.” That necessary-and-proper doctrine is the constitutional bridge from Congress's powers to a congressionally created central-bank structure. [Constitution Annotated — *McCulloch*](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S8-C18-3-1/ALDE_00001238/)

The Legal Tender Cases later recognized broad congressional power to issue paper obligations and make them legal tender. The key institutional point is that the power belongs to the United States through Congress; Congress may choose an institutional means for exercising it.

## 2. It is a statute, not an “agreement”

Congress enacted the Federal Reserve Act in 1913 “to furnish an elastic currency,” establish Federal Reserve Banks and create a national reserve and banking mechanism. [Current compiled Federal Reserve Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/COMPS-270)

The resulting structure has three different legal characters:

- **Board of Governors:** a federal government agency created by statute.
- **Federal Open Market Committee:** a statutory federal policy body composed of Board members and rotating Reserve Bank presidents.
- **Twelve Federal Reserve Banks:** congressionally chartered bodies corporate with public duties, limited statutory powers and a mixed governance structure—not ordinary private banks with sovereign currency franchises.

Member banks hold special Reserve Bank stock because the statute requires it. That stock does not let them sell the Reserve Bank, rewrite the Federal Reserve Act, direct national monetary policy or withdraw the currency power from Congress.

The Reserve Banks do not have an expiring private contract. Under 12 USC §341 they have succession until dissolved by an Act of Congress or until their franchise is forfeited for violating law. Congress removed the old charter-expiration issue in 1927. [12 USC §341](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title12-section341) and [Federal Reserve expiration FAQ](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/is-the-federal-reserve-act-going-to-expire.htm).

So the governing chain is:

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    P[The people<br/>Constitution] --> C[Congress<br/>national money and borrowing powers]
    C --> L[Federal Reserve Act and Title 31<br/>statutory architecture]
    L --> T[Treasury and U.S. Mint/BEP<br/>coins, printing, design and fiscal functions]
    L --> B[Board of Governors<br/>note orders, authorization, regulation and oversight]
    L --> R[12 Reserve Banks<br/>issue notes into circulation and maintain reserve balances]
    L --> K[Chartered private banks<br/>issue deposit liabilities in dollars]
    C --> J[Courts<br/>constitutional and statutory review]
    C -. can amend, restrict, transfer or repeal .-> L
```

## 3. “The dollar” is at least five different legal objects

### A. The unit of account

Congress defines the national unit. Under 31 USC §5101, United States money is expressed in dollars and decimal subdivisions. The statute does not define the modern dollar as a fixed weight of gold or silver. [31 USC Chapter 51](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title31-chapter51)

This is the **language of value** used in statutes, taxes, contracts, public accounts and payment obligations. Defining the unit is different from issuing a particular dollar claim.

### B. Coins — direct Treasury issuance

Under 31 USC §§5111–5112, the Secretary of the Treasury, through the United States Mint, mints and issues coins in congressionally authorized denominations and specifications. Congress can create or alter coin programs and specifications; the Secretary has implementation discretion inside those statutes. [31 USC §5111](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/31/5111) and [31 USC §5112](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A31+section%3A5112+edition%3Aprelim%29).

Coins are therefore already dollar currency issued directly through Treasury rather than through the Federal Reserve note mechanism.

### C. Federal Reserve notes — a joint Treasury/Federal Reserve issuance chain

Federal Reserve notes are the dominant physical paper currency, but “the Fed prints money” is legally imprecise.

1. Congress authorizes the notes in Federal Reserve Act §16, codified principally at 12 USC §§411–421.
2. The Board estimates demand and orders new notes.
3. Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing manufactures them.
4. The Secretary of the Treasury controls their form and tenor and the plates and dies.
5. A Reserve Bank applies for notes through its Federal Reserve agent.
6. The Board may grant or reject the application.
7. When issued to a Reserve Bank, the Bank is charged for the notes and statutory collateral/lien rules attach.
8. Reserve Banks place the notes into circulation in response to depository-institution demand and remove unfit notes.

Federal Reserve notes are **obligations of the United States**, not a foreign currency or a private member-bank IOU. Section 16 gives the Board and Reserve Banks the issue mechanism while Treasury physically produces and legally shapes the note. [Federal Reserve Act §16](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section16.htm)

Printing is not itself issuance. Notes sitting unissued in a Bureau or Federal Reserve vault are inventory; the balance-sheet liability arises through the statutory issuance/circulation process.

### D. United States currency notes — surviving Treasury authority

Title 31 still says the Secretary of the Treasury may issue United States currency notes, but caps the amount outstanding and in circulation at $300,000,000 and bars their use as reserves. This is the descendant of the “greenback” authority, not a presently scalable replacement for Federal Reserve notes without congressional amendment. [31 USC §5115](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2023&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-2023-title31-section5115)

This matters because “Treasury has no note authority” is too broad. A limited authority survives. Moving primary paper-currency issuance to Treasury would nevertheless require Congress to change the cap, operating machinery, redemption, balance-sheet and transition rules.

### E. Digital central-bank balances

Money standing to the credit of an eligible account at a Federal Reserve Bank is central-bank money and a Reserve Bank liability. These balances are already digital dollars, but they are generally wholesale balances for banks and other legally eligible institutions—not retail CBDC accounts for every person.

Reserve balances expand or contract through Federal Reserve lending, securities transactions, Treasury and payment flows, currency conversion and other balance-sheet operations. That changes the **quantity and composition** of central-bank money; it does not change who constitutionally defines the dollar.

### F. Private dollar claims

Commercial-bank deposits are private bank liabilities denominated in dollars. They function as money because they are transferable, redeemable at par under ordinary conditions and connected to public settlement, supervision and deposit insurance. They are not Federal Reserve notes or direct U.S. government liabilities merely because an account displays “$.”

Payment stablecoins are another private-claim category. The GENIUS Act defines a payment stablecoin as an issuer's redeemable digital asset designed to maintain a stable value relative to monetary value—and explicitly excludes national currency and bank deposits from the definition. It also says payment stablecoins are not backed by the full faith and credit of the United States, government-guaranteed or federally deposit-insured. [GENIUS Act, Public Law 119-27](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ27/PLAW-119publ27.pdf)

## 4. What makes something legal tender

Congress decides legal-tender status. Under 31 USC §5103, United States coins and currency, including Federal Reserve notes, are legal tender for debts, public charges, taxes and dues. [31 USC §5103](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A31+section%3A5103+edition%3Aprelim%29)

Legal tender does not mean every merchant must accept cash in every transaction. It means the specified currency has the legal capacity to discharge qualifying monetary obligations; other federal, state and contract rules can govern when a seller may refuse a payment form before a debt is created.

Nor does “denominated in dollars” create legal-tender status. A deposit, check, card balance, stablecoin or Treasury security can be measured in dollars while remaining a claim on a particular issuer or intermediary.

## 5. Who can change what

| Proposed change | Who has legal authority | Required route |
|---|---|---|
| Change note design or anti-counterfeiting features | Secretary of the Treasury, with interagency input, inside existing law | Administrative decision and implementation; Treasury has final design authority. [Federal Reserve currency-design FAQ](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/currency_12767.htm) |
| Change annual print quantity | Board forecasts/orders; BEP prints; Reserve Banks distribute according to demand | Administrative and operational action within Federal Reserve Act §16 and appropriations/production law. |
| Add an entirely new circulating paper denomination or change the issuer architecture | Congress primarily | Amend Federal Reserve Act §16 and/or Title 31; then implementing rules and operations. |
| Change coin denominations, composition or mandated designs | Congress for statutory specifications; Treasury for delegated choices | Amend 31 USC §5112 when outside existing discretion. |
| Expand direct Treasury-note issuance beyond the current cap | Congress | Amend 31 USC §5115 and build issuance, balance-sheet, redemption, distribution and monetary-policy machinery. |
| Move paper-dollar issuance from Reserve Banks to Treasury | Congress | Rewrite §§411–421 and Title 31; provide for outstanding notes, Reserve Bank assets/liabilities, settlement, redemption and transition. No constitutional amendment is inherently required. |
| Abolish or reorganize the Federal Reserve System | Congress | Repeal or amend the Federal Reserve Act and dissolve/reconstitute the statutory institutions; address contracts, property, employees, liabilities, monetary operations and payment continuity. |
| Change the dollar's legal-tender status or redefine the national unit | Congress | Amend 31 USC §§5101/5103 and related law; constitutional and contract constraints still apply. |
| Issue a retail Federal Reserve CBDC | Congress must affirmatively authorize; current law also prohibits it through 2030 | New Act of Congress or amendment/repeal of current Federal Reserve Act §16A, followed by privacy, account, AML, consumer, operating and fiscal legislation. |
| Permit regulated private stablecoin issuance | Congress has already created the framework; regulators implement it | GENIUS Act rules, issuer approval, reserves, redemption, examination and enforcement. This does not convert stablecoins into U.S. currency. |
| Change reserve balances or monetary conditions | FOMC, Board and Reserve Banks within statutory authority | Monetary-policy, lending and balance-sheet operations; cannot redefine the sovereign unit or invent retail authority. |

## 6. The actual hierarchy of change

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    A[Constitutional allocation<br/>Congress holds national money powers] --> B{What kind of change?}
    B -->|Change federal issuer, legal tender,<br/>Fed structure or retail CBDC| C[Act of Congress<br/>House + Senate + presentment<br/>or veto override]
    B -->|Implement within existing authority| D[Board, Treasury, Mint, BEP<br/>or Reserve Bank action]
    B -->|Change monetary quantity or rates| E[FOMC, Board and Reserve Banks<br/>within statutory tools]
    C --> F[Regulations, systems, contracts,<br/>balance-sheet and transition work]
    D --> G[Judicial review<br/>must remain within statute]
    E --> G
    C --> G
    H[Executive order] --> D
    H -. cannot amend an Act of Congress .-> C
```

An executive order can direct executive-branch agencies and state presidential policy only within existing law. It cannot repeal the Federal Reserve Act, mint a new statutory currency, change legal tender, dissolve a Reserve Bank or grant the Fed power Congress withheld.

Federal Reserve regulations and policies are lower in the hierarchy than statutes. Operating circulars and service agreements are lower still. They can implement or condition a lawful service; they cannot create sovereign currency authority by contract.

## 7. The current retail-CBDC boundary

The current law is unusually explicit.

- Executive Order 14178, issued January 23, 2025, directed agencies not to establish, issue or promote a CBDC except as required by law. [Executive Order 14178](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/31/2025-02123/strengthening-american-leadership-in-digital-financial-technology)
- Public Law 119-101, enacted July 11, 2026, added Federal Reserve Act §16A. It prohibits the Board and Reserve Banks from issuing or creating a generally available retail CBDC, directly or indirectly through an intermediary, subject to its stated private/open/permissionless exception. The section sunsets December 31, 2030 and says nothing should be construed as allowing CBDC issuance absent an Act of Congress. [Current Federal Reserve Act compilation](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/COMPS-270) and [current 12 USC subchapter](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title12-chapter3-subchapter12).

Therefore, **FedNow is not a CBDC**. It is a payment service through which participating institutions transfer their own customers' funds. A retail CBDC would be a generally available direct liability of the Federal Reserve System. [Federal Reserve FedNow/CBDC FAQ](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/is-fednow-replacing-cash-is-it-a-central-bank-digital-currency.htm)

Congress could change this boundary again. The 2026 prohibition itself proves the point: Congress—not the Fed acting alone—can add, remove or redesign the authority.

## 8. Could Congress make Treasury the primary dollar issuer?

**Legally, yes, by statute.** The Constitution does not require the present Federal Reserve note architecture. Congress used its constitutional powers to choose that architecture and could choose another one.

But the phrase “Treasury issues the dollar instead” hides a very large system conversion. A serious statute would have to answer:

1. Are existing Federal Reserve notes redeemed, exchanged or allowed to remain indefinitely?
2. Which entity books the new currency liability and which assets, revenues or public credit stand behind it?
3. Who decides issuance quantity—Treasury, a monetary authority, a formula or Congress through appropriations?
4. How are Reserve Bank balance sheets and pledged collateral treated?
5. What becomes the settlement asset for banks and FMIs?
6. Who supplies elastic intraday and crisis liquidity?
7. How are monetary-policy operations separated from fiscal spending decisions?
8. Who distributes, authenticates, redeems and destroys physical currency?
9. How are contracts, taxes, accounting systems and foreign official holdings converted?
10. What prevents operational interruption or an unintended fiscal-monetary merger during transition?

The legal step is ordinary legislation; the difficult part is designing the replacement constitution. Changing the name on the note without reallocating the balance-sheet, settlement, liquidity and governance functions would not actually replace the system.

## 9. What would require a constitutional amendment?

A constitutional amendment is **not ordinarily required** to restructure or even abolish the Federal Reserve, authorize Treasury notes, change coinage, or create a congressionally authorized digital form of public money. Congress already has broad national monetary powers.

An amendment would become relevant if the proposal changed the Constitution's allocation of power itself—for example, granting states a sovereign right to coin national money or make their own instruments legal tender despite Article I §10, permanently stripping Congress of its Article I money power, or constitutionally entrenching a particular issuer so future Congresses could not change it by ordinary law.

## 10. The clean sovereignty answer

The United States has not stopped issuing its own dollar.

- Congress defines the monetary unit and legal-tender framework.
- Treasury directly issues coins and retains limited United States-note authority.
- Federal Reserve notes are authorized by Congress, produced by Treasury, issued through statutory Federal Reserve institutions and are obligations of the United States.
- Reserve balances are public central-bank liabilities.
- Bank deposits and stablecoins are private dollar claims connected to, but legally distinct from, sovereign currency.

The Federal Reserve is therefore an **instrument of U.S. statutory monetary sovereignty**, not the owner of that sovereignty. Its operational independence protects specified decisions from day-to-day political direction; it does not place the System above Congress or give it an irrevocable claim to issue the dollar.

## 11. Reusable test for any “new dollar” claim

Ask seven questions:

1. **Unit:** Does the proposal redefine the dollar or merely denominate a claim in dollars?
2. **Issuer:** Is the legal obligor the United States, Treasury, a Reserve Bank, a commercial bank or a private token issuer?
3. **Authority:** What enacted statute authorizes issuance—not merely study, design, registration or a pilot?
4. **Legal status:** Is it legal tender, a deposit, a security, a stablecoin, an account balance or another contractual claim?
5. **Backing and redemption:** What assets, taxing power, collateral, insurance or issuer promise supports par value?
6. **Settlement:** Where does final discharge occur and on whose books?
7. **Change state:** Is the proposal introduced, enacted, effective, implemented, issued, circulating and materially used?
