Wave 4F — Private Market Governance

Research window: Exact 2025 Libra ingress through exact 2025 Capricorn ingress.
Evidence cutoff: June 20, 2026, 11:59 p.m. EDT. Later facts are quarantined as handoffs.
Question: When two late-Libra orders targeted food-supply concentration and proxy-adviser influence, did the executive directly set prices and corporate votes, or did it recruit antitrust, securities and pension-law institutions to reclassify private relationships?
Finding: It recruited existing regulators and legal doctrines. EO 14364 created parallel DOJ and FTC food-supply task forces; by the cutoff both existed, DOJ had filed statements of interest in frozen-potato and seed litigation, and the FTC described an operating cross-agency task force. No new food-pricing rule, criminal case, divestiture, market-price effect or public 180-day briefing was proved. EO 14366 directed SEC, FTC and Labor reviews of proxy advisers and shareholder-voting rules. Labor supplied the strongest descendant: Technical Release 2026-01 treated actual voting control as functional fiduciary conduct and fee advice as potentially fiduciary under the existing five-part test. It did not make every proxy adviser a fiduciary in every relationship. No cutoff-safe SEC final rule, FTC proxy-adviser case or named changed vote was located. The packet therefore shows private governance becoming public law at the interface where advice, voting control, intellectual property and market concentration affect assets the state already protects.

The shared ladder is:

private influence alleged → regulator or court acquires the question → existing legal test → investigation / guidance / filing → firm-specific application → remedy → price, vote or participant outcome

4F.1 — December 6: food affordability becomes an antitrust and security docket

EO 14364 directs the Attorney General and FTC Chair to establish separate Food Supply Chain Security Task Forces. They are to investigate food-related industries, determine whether anticompetitive conduct or foreign control increases costs or creates a security threat, pursue remedies under their respective authorities and brief congressional leaders at 180- and 365-day points.

The order names possible sectors—meat processing, seed, fertilizer and equipment—but makes no liability finding. Existing civil settlements are background, not an adjudication against every firm in those sectors.

February and May 2026: the task-force theory enters actual cases

On February 27, DOJ filed a statement of interest in the frozen-potato antitrust litigation. A statement of interest is a legal-position filing under 28 U.S.C. § 517. It is not a complaint, verdict or remedy, but it moves the executive's competition theory into an Article III record.

On May 11, DOJ filed a statement of interest in seed-industry litigation. The filing argues that patent disclosure and reasonable access to patented seeds matter for follow-on innovation and entry. DOJ took no position on the ultimate disposition of the case.

The FTC's fiscal-year 2027 Congressional Budget Justification says it formed its own task force with representatives from across the Commission to support ongoing competition enforcement, identify new threats and develop enforcement or regulatory approaches. That is institutional formation, not a new appropriation or successful case.

The 180-day congressional-briefing point fell in early June 2026. The order did not require a public report, and no public briefing record was located. It should therefore be classified internal/unpublished or not located, not automatically missed.

Maturity. Two task-force carriers, an operating enforcement portfolio and concrete Federal legal participation in two private disputes.

Counterevidence and limit. No packet evidence shows a task-force-originated indictment or complaint, a finding of foreign control, a consent decree, divestiture, criminal grand jury, new rule, producer payment, consumer refund or price reduction. Litigation filings do not transfer the court's decision right to DOJ. Food inflation has many causes; the order does not establish that concentration or foreign ownership caused a particular price.

4F.2 — December 11: proxy advice is tested as fiduciary conduct

EO 14366 names Institutional Shareholder Services and Glass Lewis and directs three separate regulatory lanes:

  • SEC: review proxy-adviser and shareholder-proposal rules; consider registration, transparency, anti-fraud enforcement, Section 13 group formation and investment-adviser duties;
  • FTC/Justice: investigate potential unfair, deceptive or anticompetitive conduct and examine State antitrust records; and
  • Labor: revise or clarify ERISA treatment of proxy voting, paid advice, pension fiduciary status and transparency.

The order does not itself register either firm, invalidate a recommendation, alter Rule 14a-8, create an antitrust violation or decide that an ESG-related vote breaches fiduciary duty. It asks the competent agencies to apply existing statutes and, where necessary, use APA rulemaking.

April 1: Labor supplies the first operative legal characterization

Technical Release 2026-01 distinguishes two ways a proxy service can acquire ERISA fiduciary status:

  1. a firm that exercises authority or control over shareholder rights belonging to an ERISA plan—including actual discretion over votes—can be a functional fiduciary under ERISA § 3(21)(A)(i); and
  2. a firm providing advice for a fee can be an investment advice fiduciary if the particular relationship satisfies the longstanding five-part test: securities advice, regular basis, mutual understanding, primary basis and individualized advice.

The release also states Labor's view that certain State disclosure laws applying when advice departs from maximizing risk-adjusted return generally are not ERISA-preempted, because ERISA-governed fiduciaries should not provide the nonfinancial advice that triggers the disclosure.

This is a real legal-state change at the guidance layer. It places contracts, workflow and voting authority under a facts-and-circumstances test that can carry liability. It does not rewrite the regulation, automatically adjudicate either named firm or remove plan trustees' own monitoring duties.

SEC Commissioner remarks in January publicly framed the order's questions, but remarks are not Commission action. By June 20, this packet did not locate a final SEC proxy-adviser or Rule 14a-8 amendment, an SEC registration order, an FTC complaint, a Justice antitrust case against the named firms or an adjudicated ERISA breach attributable to the order.

Maturity. Labor guidance with immediate compliance relevance to plan fiduciaries, asset managers, proxy firms and voting-service contracts.

Counterevidence and limit. Fiduciary status is functional and relationship-specific. Research or standardized recommendations alone do not automatically satisfy all five investment-advice elements. A recommendation using climate, workforce or diversity information is not necessarily non-pecuniary; the relevant question is the plan fiduciary's reasonable risk-return determination. Guidance is not proof that a vote changed, returns improved or political influence disappeared.

The private-governance theorem

Both orders work by finding a private intermediary whose conduct affects a protected public object:

  • food processors, seed firms and suppliers affect competitive price formation and supply security; and
  • proxy advisers affect shareholder rights embedded in pension and investment assets.

The executive does not directly operate either market. It changes the legal hearing around the intermediary:

private service or concentration → public-risk claim → task force / agency → existing antitrust, securities or fiduciary test → case-specific record → remedy

This is another form of the wave's counterparty state. In Wave 4A a foreign or corporate counterparty is qualified into a relationship. In Wave 4E a threat is excluded. Here the counterparty remains lawful but its role is re-described: supplier may be monopolist, advice may be fiduciary conduct, voting software may be control.

Both poles

Constructive pole — intermediaries become answerable to the asset holder

  • task forces can integrate agricultural knowledge with antitrust enforcement;
  • courts receive Federal views without losing their adjudicative role;
  • entry and innovation may improve if patent and concentration barriers are correctly identified;
  • plan fiduciaries must understand who actually controls votes and why;
  • methodologies and conflicts can become more transparent; and
  • fiduciary status can follow function rather than job title.

Shadow pole — regulatory review becomes ideological selection

  • security language can turn ordinary competition questions into exceptional-state claims;
  • public investigations can chill lawful scale, foreign ownership or unpopular corporate policy without proof;
  • the executive can label one set of material risks “political” while privileging another;
  • plans may lose useful independent research or default to management recommendations;
  • vague facts-and-circumstances exposure can encourage over-compliance; and
  • claimed consumer protection can mask a struggle over who controls corporate speech and capital.

Both poles remain live. Complaints, discovery, rulings, rulemaking records, contracts, vote logs, returns and participant remedies—not the astrological rhyme—show how the interface changed.
