# The Bench and the Money

The money story has three rooms. The White House writes orders. Congress writes bills. But the third room is where the walls get moved — the bench decides what the other two rooms were allowed to do, and it decides slowly, years after the act, in language nobody reads. The Judicial Watch is the map of that third room.

A whole map of cases — the registry grows with every revision — asking one question eight ways: **who holds the keys to the money?**

The **Throne** cases ask who rules the rulers. The Court took removal power over every commission — then carved out one seat. The Fed alone keeps its shield, and it keeps it on *history*, not text: the central bank as a thing older and stranger than the administrative state. Cook keeps her chair, but the carve-out is a fence around one lot while the whole neighborhood gets rezoned.

The **Purse** cases ask who pays, and who can be stopped. Funding outside appropriations: allowed. Starving the funded agency from inside: still open, and the CFPB lives in that opening. And behind those sit the rescue cases — 2008's long shadow — where the courts said the government may take a company, sweep its profits, sell its bones, and the owners may sue only for money, never for the thing back. The anti-injunction bars are the quietest, hardest law in this whole map: while the receiver works, the courthouse door is shut.

The **Gates** cases are the shortest story with the biggest weight: eligibility is not entitlement. Two circuits now say a Reserve Bank may look at a lawful bank and still say no. The gate to central-bank money is discretionary, and after Custodia's deadline passes it may be settled law for a generation — right as the GENIUS rails get built outside the gate.

The **Perimeter** cases draw the business lines — who may charter, what states may still touch, how far a federal charter reaches. The stablecoin fight will be fought here, and the map's honest finding is that it hasn't started: no GENIUS merits case exists yet. The first one is a watch item, not a memory.

The **Sword** and the **Hoard** are old power made new. Blocking, vesting, tariffs, turnover — from the Algiers Accords to Tornado Cash, the pattern holds: the Executive's sword over foreign money is broad but not boundless. Immutable code is not property. A tariff is not a sanction. And a foreign central bank's account at the New York Fed is close to untouchable — until Congress writes one statute for one pile of money, which it has done, and the Court blessed. The Russian reserves sit exactly on that precedent line, and nothing has crossed it yet.

The **Vault** is where the state's hands actually close on coins — seizure, forfeiture, bankruptcy estates. Celsius customers learned title had left them in the terms of service. BlockFi customers learned the opposite, from different fine print. The Strategic Bitcoin Reserve stands downstream of a forfeiture pipeline the courts have mostly waved through. Whose coins the government holds is a fact; whose coins it *owns* is a judgment, and the two are years apart.

The **Ledger** is surveillance — who may read the payment records, who must report ownership, whether the old third-party doctrine survives the digital ledger. Chatrie put the first crack in it. The bank-records rule still stands, but it stands the way a dam stands after the first leak.

## The reading

The bench is not one actor and this map proves no plan. But the *direction of the residue* is legible: discretion upheld at the gate, the shield narrowed to one chair, the purse fenced but not filled, the sword sharpened abroad and dulled at home, the vault filling, the ledger opening. Every track bends the same way the monetary reset bends — away from general rules anyone can invoke, toward specific keys held by few hands. The courts are not driving the reset. They are paving behind it.

## The story gets stronger if…

- Custodia's cert petition never lands, and Reserve-account discretion hardens into settled law.
- The first GENIUS preemption suit is filed and the federal charter wins big.
- Peterson II's turnover completes — a foreign central bank's money actually moves by court order.
- The REPO Act gets used and a court sustains the vesting of Russian reserves.
- NTEU ends with a rule that an agency may be starved so long as its shell is kept.
- Cantero's second cert petition is granted and preemption swings back toward the banks.
- A court extends Chatrie's logic to financial records and *Miller* starts to fall.

## The story weakens if…

- The Supreme Court takes Custodia and finds an entitlement in "shall be available."
- The en banc D.C. Circuit orders the CFPB rebuilt and funded.
- The first stablecoin-charter case goes to the states.
- Crystallex's sale dies at the OFAC license gate and the creditor queue disbands.
- Russian reserves stay untouched through 2027 — the vesting precedent stays theoretical.
- The Fed carve-out grows instead of shrinking: courts start extending the historical shield to other financial regulators.
