The military gained border-land arrest power, not control of civilian courts

In plain English

Question: Has the military taken authority over ordinary Federal offices or enlarged its jurisdiction into civilian Federal court districts?

Answer: No official record reviewed establishes either claim. The narrower process is real and important: the executive branch has transferred administrative jurisdiction over selected Federal border lands to the Department of War, attached those lands to existing military installations, marked them as restricted National Defense Areas, and used existing military-property statutes to let designated military personnel patrol, temporarily detain and sometimes arrest civilians for offenses tied to those areas. Civilians must be transferred to civilian authorities, and prosecutions stay in ordinary United States district courts. The August 14, 2026 Office of Legal Counsel opinion pushes the arrest edge just beyond an area's line for immediate pursuit, but it does not create military courts or redraw any civilian judicial district.

Evidence checked through: October 12, 2026.

The detailed record below keeps land jurisdiction, police power, custody, prosecution and court jurisdiction separate.

Bottom line

The practical expansion happens by changing the legal character and administrative owner of land, not by announcing general military government.

  1. The President assigns a military border mission.
  2. Civilian land agencies transfer administrative jurisdiction over named Federal parcels to the War Department.
  3. A service secretary places the parcels on military property rolls and attaches them to an existing base.
  4. An installation commander marks the tract as a restricted area and delegates operational control to U.S. Northern Command.
  5. The Secretary or Deputy Secretary may designate trained military or civilian personnel under 10 U.S.C. § 2672, subject to Attorney General-approved guidelines.
  6. Those personnel protect the military property, enforce Federal security and trespass rules, and transfer arrested civilians to civilian custody.
  7. U.S. Attorneys charge Federal crimes in the already-existing civilian district court for the place of the alleged offense.

That is an expanded military-property and arrest perimeter. It is not an expansion of Article III court territory, military trial jurisdiction over civilians, or military control of Federal office buildings generally.

The process, document by document

Stage Actor and record What changes What does not change
1. Mission President, EO 14167, January 20, 2025 USNORTHCOM receives a standing border-security mission and planning duties. The order does not itself transfer land or authorize every form of civilian law enforcement.
2. Land route President, NSPM-4, April 11, 2025 Defense, Interior, Agriculture and Homeland Security are told to provide War Department use and jurisdiction over selected Federal border lands and to transfer jurisdiction under existing law. State territory and civilian judicial districts are not redrawn. Federal Indian reservations are excluded.
3. Administrative transfer Interior public-land orders, or an IBWC → GSA → military-department transfer The civil land custodian transfers administrative jurisdiction over exact parcels for a stated term or mission. New Mexico PLO 7963 transferred 109,651 acres to the Army for three years; a 2026 correction repaired its legal descriptions. Title to all Federal land does not move, and a transfer of administrative jurisdiction is not a transfer of legislative or judicial power. Valid existing rights survive the public-land orders.
4. Installation annex Service secretary and base orders The receiving service adds the tract to its property roll and assigns it to Fort Huachuca, Fort Bliss, Joint Base San Antonio, MCAS Yuma or Naval Air Facility El Centro. The remote tract does not become a new military department, court or general police district.
5. Restricted-area order Installation commander under 50 U.S.C. § 797, 18 U.S.C. § 1382 and service security rules The commander marks and controls access to the tract as part of the installation. Signage and fencing become vital notice evidence. A commander order cannot erase the elements of the criminal statutes or the Fourth Amendment.
6. Officer designation Secretary or Deputy Secretary under 10 U.S.C. § 2672 Designated military or civilian personnel may enforce Federal protective laws, carry firearms, make authorized arrests, serve process and investigate offenses against War Department property or persons. The Secretary must find the powers necessary, find the duties cannot be done as effectively without them, and ensure training is available. Section 2672 expressly preserves Federal law-enforcement, DHS and GSA authority. It does not hand ordinary Federal offices to the military.
7. Immediate pursuit DOJ Office of Legal Counsel, August 14, 2026 OLC says the Posse Comitatus Act does not bar military personnel from following an alleged NDA trespasser just outside the boundary and arresting the person in the immediate vicinity. OLC is an executive-branch legal opinion, not a court judgment. It gives no answer for an arrest far removed in time or place and says military personnel may not be assigned primarily off-base protective duties on this theory.
8. Civil handoff 10 U.S.C. § 2672(f) and operational orders A civilian arrested under the property-protection power is transferred to civilian authorities. The civilian may not be held in a military confinement facility unless independently subject to the UCMJ.
9. Charge and trial U.S. Attorney and United States district court Prosecutors may add military-property counts under 50 U.S.C. § 797 and 18 U.S.C. § 1382 to immigration charges. Venue remains the civilian district where the alleged offense occurred; the military does not acquire a civilian court district.

What the public record shows is operating

The War Department reported on March 17, 2026 that six National Defense Areas covered about 845 miles, or 42 percent, of the border across Arizona, California, New Mexico and Texas. Public military pages identify the current structure through these installation relationships:

  • New Mexico land as an extension of Fort Huachuca;
  • West Texas land from El Paso toward Fort Hancock as an extension of Fort Bliss;
  • South Texas land administered as part of Joint Base San Antonio;
  • Yuma tracts assigned to Marine Corps Air Station Yuma;
  • the Southern California–Imperial Valley area assigned to Naval Air Facility El Centro; and
  • the Del Rio–Falcon area administered through Joint Base San Antonio.

The land route is not paper-only. DOJ announced early New Mexico and West Texas charges and convictions in 2025. Its October 2, 2026 New Mexico report says prosecutors brought 284 combined illegal-entry, military-security-regulation and military-property counts during the four weeks ending that day. A charge is not a conviction, and DOJ's aggregate does not show which actor made each arrest.

The court seam

The first New Mexico complaints exposed the weak point in the new arrangement: notice. On May 14, 2025, Chief U.S. Magistrate Judge Gregory Wormuth dismissed two military-trespass counts without prejudice because the complaint did not allege facts showing the defendant knew he had entered the new military area. The court stressed that a long, difficult border tract with some signs is not the same thing as a fenced traditional base.

Later convictions show prosecutors can establish the charges on stronger facts. In June 2025 the District of New Mexico announced guilty pleas by two men who had previously crossed the NDA and had been warned in Spanish; the Western District of Texas announced both a bench conviction and a jury conviction.

The Tenth Circuit's published public orders in United States v. Toirov addressed pretrial release, not the final merits of the military-trespass elements. No controlling appellate merits decision located through the cutoff decides the broader NDA theory, and OLC stated in August 2026 that no court had yet construed § 2672 in this setting.

The House tried to blunt the notice fight in September 2025. It agreed to an amendment that would have made NDA violations general-intent crimes, raised penalties and written National Defense Areas expressly into 50 U.S.C. § 797. That text does not appear in the final enacted FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act, Public Law 119-60. A House vote is not an enacted expansion.

Reading between the lines

These are inferences from the joined process, not statements made by one source.

1. The scalable lever is land custody

NSPM-4 expressly lets the Secretary extend the model to additional Federal lands along the southern border after an interagency transfer. Once a parcel is lawfully placed under War Department jurisdiction, custody or control, the administration can use the mature law of installation security instead of asking Congress for a general domestic-policing statute.

That is the quiet move: civil Federal land → military administrative jurisdiction → installation annex → controlled perimeter → military-property crimes.

2. The August 2026 OLC opinion closes the boundary-escape gap

The opinion was requested by the War Department General Counsel in April 2026. It does more than restate on-base authority. It says a person cannot defeat military arrest merely by crossing the newly drawn line before troops catch up. OLC limits the answer to immediate pursuit near the NDA and leaves a far-removed arrest open, but it turns a fixed boundary into a narrow moving enforcement edge.

3. The most elastic phrase is “jurisdiction, custody, or control”

OLC says there is no extra requirement that every acre have a separate military use once the tract is under War Department control. The military-property status itself, plus the duty in § 2672, supports protection of the whole area. That reading makes the front-end transfer and designation record more important than the tract's day-to-day use.

4. Courts remain a check, but mostly after the machinery begins

The land transfer, property-roll entry, commander order, patrol and arrest can all occur before a district court rules on a particular complaint. Judicial review then arrives through probable-cause review, suppression, dismissal, trial or civil challenge. The New Mexico notice ruling changed complaint practice but did not dissolve the NDAs.

5. “Federal offices” would be a further step, not the present record

Section 2672 covers War Department buildings, grounds and property. It expressly says it does not displace DHS protection of off-installation Federal facilities or GSA authority. Moving an ordinary civilian Federal office into the military perimeter would therefore require a real custody/control transfer, a separate statutory route, or a property-specific defense function. No blanket transfer of Federal offices was found.

6. “Expanding civilian court districts” is the wrong description of a real caseload change

The boundaries and subject-matter jurisdiction of the district courts remain fixed by Congress. What expands is the set of facts and charges arriving in those courts: a border crossing on newly military-administered land can now produce an immigration count plus military-property counts. That increases the military source of civilian prosecutions without enlarging the court itself.

What would prove a broader expansion

Watch for these documents, in this order:

  1. a land-transfer or custody instrument for property away from the southern-border mission, especially an ordinary civilian Federal facility;
  2. a service-secretary order putting that property on military rolls or attaching it to an installation;
  3. a commander restricted-area order and map;
  4. a Secretary or Deputy Secretary § 2672 designation naming military personnel and the precise arrest powers;
  5. the Attorney General-approved geographic guidelines required by § 2672(i);
  6. an operational order assigning military personnel a primarily off-property enforcement role;
  7. an arrest report showing military personnel, rather than CBP or another civilian agency, made the arrest;
  8. a complaint, suppression ruling or merits judgment testing the authority; and
  9. enacted text changing § 2672, § 797, § 1382, the Posse Comitatus Act or district-court jurisdiction.

The hardest public gap is item 5. The statute requires Attorney General-approved guidelines, but the reviewed official record did not expose the controlling current guidelines or a complete list of § 2672 designations for all six NDAs.

Claim audit

Claim Finding
“The military took authority over Federal offices.” Not established. The record shows selected Federal lands transferred into War Department administrative control. Section 2672 preserves DHS/GSA authority over ordinary off-installation Federal facilities.
“The military's jurisdiction now extends into civilian court districts.” Misframed. Military property and arrest authority expanded geographically. Existing Article III courts kept their boundaries and received the prosecutions.
“Civilians can now be tried by military courts for crossing an NDA.” Not established and contrary to the reviewed process. Civilians are transferred to civilian authorities and prosecuted in U.S. district court.
“Posse Comitatus no longer matters.” Wrong. OLC relies on the independent-military-purpose doctrine and express § 2672 authorization; it does not repeal the Act.
“The arrest authority stops exactly at the NDA line.” Changed by executive legal interpretation. OLC says immediate pursuit just beyond the line is permitted, while leaving far-removed arrest unanswered.
“The Insurrection Act created this authority.” Wrong object. The NDA route rests on EO 14167, NSPM-4, land-transfer law, 10 U.S.C. § 2672, 50 U.S.C. § 797, 18 U.S.C. § 1382 and commander authority. The existing Library map separately finds no Insurrection Act invocation at its August 15, 2026 cutoff.
