Reading the Charter — Freddie Mac

Freddie Mac is not a bank. It is not a government office. It is a bill that Congress signed to birth a mortgage middleman, gave it a wall of legal armor most companies would kill for, and then — twenty years later — handed it to private shareholders while quietly filing the serial numbers off the armor. What's left is a strange half-breed: private profit, public plumbing, and a bailout wire running straight back to the Treasury the day it all breaks. Read below section by section.

1. Immunities & liability shields — suits, process, asset seizure

  • Sue and be sued, but on its own turf. §1452(c)(7) gives the Corporation the ordinary corporate power "to sue and be sued, complain and defend, in any State, Federal, or other court." Nothing special there. The special part is next.
  • Every lawsuit against Freddie Mac gets dragged into federal court, on Freddie's terms. §1452(f): Freddie "shall be deemed to be an agency" for purposes of 28 U.S.C. §§1345 and 1442 — the federal-jurisdiction and removal statutes. All civil actions it's a party to "shall be deemed to arise under the laws of the United States," district courts get automatic original jurisdiction "without regard to amount or value," and Freddie can yank any state-court case into federal court "without the giving of any bond or security." Note the word choice: "deemed to be an agency" — for litigation-plumbing purposes only, not for constitutional or sovereign-immunity purposes. It's agency-status à la carte.
  • The old "treat us like the government itself" clause got cut. §1456(a), before the 1989 FIRREA rewrite, said Freddie got "all immunities and priorities... to which it would be entitled if it were the United States or if it were an unincorporated agency of the United States." That's about as close to declaring yourself sovereign as a corporation can get on paper. FIRREA struck the whole sentence in 1989 and replaced it with a much smaller grant: Freddie can "conduct its business without regard to any qualification or similar statute in any State" (§1456(a)) — i.e., it doesn't need to register/qualify to do business state by state, but it no longer claims the immunities of the sovereign itself.
  • Property and contract rights are frozen against future state/local law. §1456(a): any of Freddie's "rights and remedies" on a mortgage are immune from impairment by any law or administrative action that comes into effect after Freddie acquired the property/mortgage — a built-in shield against states passing borrower-protection laws that would bite Freddie's existing loan book.
  • Liens the company creates on its own property outrank ordinary rules. §1455(b)-(d): Freddie can create "floating liens" on all its property including after-acquired property, these liens are enforceable in D.C. federal court or wherever the property sits, and per §1455(d) this is "fully effective notwithstanding any other law... including... any law of or relating to sovereign immunity or priority." Freddie's own paper beats everyone else's paper, by statute.

2. Tax exemptions

  • §1452(e) — the exemption, in full: "The Corporation, including its franchise, activities, capital, reserves, surplus, and income, shall be exempt from all taxation now or hereafter imposed by any territory, dependency, or possession of the United States or by any State, county, municipality, or local taxing authority." That's franchise tax, income tax, activity tax — the whole state/local stack, gone. The one carve-out: real property Freddie owns is still taxed locally like anyone else's real estate (same section, proviso clause).
  • No exemption from federal income tax is stated here — the corporate income tax exemption fight for Freddie/Fannie runs through the tax code (26 U.S.C.), not this Act; this Act only handles state/local/territorial tax.
  • Securities get SEC-exempt status, which functions like a market-confidence subsidy. §1455(g): all Freddie securities (other than certain non-Freddie-purchased mortgage-backed paper) are "deemed to be exempt securities" under SEC law "to the same extent as securities that are direct obligations of or obligations guaranteed... by the United States." Freddie's bonds get treated, for registration and disclosure purposes, like they ARE Treasury bonds — even though (see #10 below) the statute in the very next section says in black and white that they are NOT backed by the United States. That juxtaposition is the whole ballgame.

3. Control map — who appoints, who removes, for what cause

  • 1970-1989 (original design): Board of 18 — 5 appointed annually by the President of the United States, the rest elected. Presidentially-appointed members could be "removed from office by the President for good cause" (per the pre-2008 text of §1452(a)(2)(B), added in the 1992 amendment). This was a government-controlled board sitting inside a "private" corporation.
  • 1989 (FIRREA) — ownership handed to shareholders. Pub. L. 101-73 converted senior participating preferred stock into voting common stock (§1453 statutory note) and moved toward a stockholder-elected board.
  • 2008 (HERA) — the President formally let go of the leash. §1452(a)(2), as amended by Pub. L. 110-289 §1162(b)(1), struck the presidential-appointment slots and the "removed... for good cause" language entirely. Today the Board is "13 persons, or such other number as the Director [of FHFA] determines appropriate," elected annually by voting common stockholders — full stop. The President appoints nobody to this board by statute.
  • But 2008 is also the year the leash got replaced with a chain. The same year Congress cut the President out of board appointments, it also created FHFA and — outside this Act, under HERA/12 U.S.C. §4617 — gave the FHFA Director power to place Freddie into conservatorship and run it directly. Freddie has been in that conservatorship, uninterrupted, since September 2008. So the formal charter reads "shareholder democracy"; the operating reality since 2008 is "single federal regulator holds total control." Two completely different control maps, one on paper and one in practice — flag this hard, this is the sharpest teeth-vs-paper gap in the whole file.
  • Officer/employee pay: the Board sets compensation "without regard to any other law" except what Congress later enacts to limit it (§1452(c)(9)), but since 1992 a "significant portion" of executive pay must be performance-based, and since 1992 no executive severance deal is valid without advance approval by the FHFA Director (§1452(h)(2)). Since 2008, the Director can also block any pay or agreement with an executive "for matters being reviewed" (§1452(h)(4)) — a live, standing veto over Freddie's own executive comp.

4. Money — self-funding vs. appropriations, off-budget status

  • Officially self-supporting. §1454(a)(5): Freddie's lending operations must be run so they are "within its income derived from such operations" and "fully self-supporting." No appropriated money funds day-to-day operations.
  • But there is a standing federal credit line, sized in statute. §1455(c): the Secretary of the Treasury "may purchase" Freddie's obligations, capped originally at "an amount greater than $2,250,000,000" outstanding — treated as ordinary "public debt transactions of the United States" (§1455(c)(5)). That $2.25 billion line has sat in the code since 1970, quietly, as a backstop nobody ever expected to need... until 2008.
  • 2008 — the backstop got vastly bigger and temporary-but-not-really. §1455(l) (added by HERA, Pub. L. 110-289) let Treasury buy "any obligations and other securities issued by the Corporation... on such terms and conditions as the Secretary may determine and in such amounts as the Secretary may determine" — i.e., no dollar cap at all — once the Secretary determines it's needed to "provide stability to the financial markets," "prevent disruptions in the availability of mortgage finance," and "protect the taxpayer" (§1455(l)(1)(B)). New-purchase authority formally expired December 31, 2009 (§1455(l)(4)), but the Secretary's power to hold, exercise rights under, or sell whatever was already bought under that authority does NOT expire (§1455(l)(2)(D)) — so the 2008-era preferred stock purchase agreements are still live decades later. "Temporary" turned into "permanent, just not growing."
  • No fee, no charge, from the federal government on Freddie's core securities business — §1455(i) bars any federal fee or charge on Freddie's purchase, sale, pledge, issuance, guarantee, or redemption of mortgages/securities, except the FHFA assessments and the Treasury-agreement fees the statute itself allows.

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

  • GAO gets in, but Freddie pays for the privilege. §1456(b): Comptroller General (GAO) can audit Freddie's "programs, activities, receipts, expenditures, and financial transactions," full access to books and records — and then Freddie "shall reimburse the Government Accountability Office for the full cost of any such audit as billed." Transparency, sure, but billed transparency, and it's Freddie writing the check to the auditor.
  • No FOIA mention anywhere in this Act. Freddie is not listed as subject to FOIA in this chapter (unsurprising for a shareholder-owned corporation, even a government-chartered one) — silence here means Freddie's internal records are not reachable the way a plain federal agency's would be, notwithstanding the "deemed to be an agency" litigation language in §1452(f).
  • Public reporting is real but director-gatekept. §1456(c)-(g): annual/quarterly financial reports to the FHFA Director, an independent financial-statement audit, mortgage data collection (income, race, gender, census tract, loan-to-value) reported to the Director, and a public housing-activities report — but before that housing report goes public, Freddie "may exclude" information the Director has "determined is proprietary" (§1456(f)(3)(B)). The regulator, not the public, decides what counts as a trade secret.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

  • Perpetual existence, killable only by Congress. §1452(c)(2): Freddie has power "to have succession until dissolved by Act of Congress." No sunset clause, no expiration date, no shareholder vote can end it — only a fresh act of Congress.
  • Severability is absolute. §1459: if any piece of the Act is struck down, Congress's "controlling intent" is that everything else survives untouched. The pre-1989 version of this section went further, declaring Freddie's powers "exercisable... without regard to any other law" — a blanket supremacy clause over its own charter that FIRREA quietly deleted in 1989 along with the sovereign-immunity language in §1456(a). Two separate "we act like the sovereign" clauses, cut in the same 1989 bill.

7. Emergency powers — what unlocks, who decides

  • The live emergency lever is §1455(l), added in 2008: Treasury gets essentially unlimited authority to buy Freddie's debt and securities the moment the Secretary — one person, no court, no separate board — determines it's needed for financial-market stability, mortgage-finance continuity, and taxpayer protection. The Secretary must weigh things like "the need to maintain the Corporation's status as a private shareholder-owned company" (§1455(l)(1)(C)(v)) — a strange, almost wistful instruction to try to preserve the fiction of privateness even while nationalizing the funding. New purchases sunset Dec 31 2009; the government's grip on what it already bought does not sunset.
  • Separately — and this is the part that actually fired in real life — the true emergency trigger for Freddie isn't in this Act at all. It's HERA's conservatorship power under 12 U.S.C. §4617, which lets the single FHFA Director place Freddie into conservatorship or receivership on the Director's own determination. That's FHFA's charter, not Freddie's, but it is the mechanism that has actually controlled Freddie since September 2008, and it sits right next to the Treasury purchase authority described above. Flagging it here because you cannot understand current-state Freddie without it, even though the operative text lives in a different title.

8. Definitions section — scope traps

§1451 is short but two definitions quietly do heavy lifting:

  • "Residential mortgage" (h) starts out sounding like a house loan and then, through a chain of amendments (1978, 1979, 1980, 1984, 1992), grows to include: energy-conservation loans and solar-system loans insured under the National Housing Act; loans from public utilities financing energy retrofits; unsecured-by-first-lien "loans made where the lender relies... primarily on the borrower's general credit standing"; rehab/renovation/modernization loans secured by subordinate liens; loans secured by a cooperative-housing stock certificate and occupancy lease (not real property at all — personal property plus a lease); and loans secured by manufactured homes "without regard to whether the security property is real, personal, or mixed." What started as "a mortgage on a house" now covers unsecured-ish consumer credit, personal-property liens, and co-op stock pledges. Every expansion widens what Freddie is statutorily allowed to buy — and therefore what taxpayer-adjacent machinery stands behind it.
  • "Conventional mortgage" (i) is defined by exclusion — anything NOT already backed by a federal guaranty/insurance program. This is the category Freddie exists to make a market in; the definition works by carving federal-insurance-backed loans (FHA/VA-type) out of its own scope.
  • "Organization" (e) is defined broadly enough ("any corporation, partnership, association, business trust, or business entity") that every anti-impersonation and eligible-seller rule downstream automatically nets in an enormous range of counterparties without Congress having to name them.

9. Succession / perpetuity & dissolution

Covered above under #6 — perpetual existence per §1452(c)(2), dissolvable only "by Act of Congress." No self-liquidation clause, no shareholder-triggered wind-down, no regulator-triggered dissolution written into this Act (the conservatorship/receivership off-ramp lives in HERA/§4617, outside this charter, and receivership there CAN lead to liquidation — again, the real kill-switch is one title over from the birth certificate).

10. Anything strange — the "not an agency" clause sitting next to the tax break

Here's the contradiction the checklist told us to hunt for, and it's real, and it's blunt:

  • §1452(e), a few lines of statute: Freddie "including its franchise, activities, capital, reserves, surplus, and income, shall be exempt from all taxation" by any state, county, city, or territory. Read alone, that's a sovereign-style privilege — the kind of tax wall real federal agencies get.
  • §1455(g), same chapter: Freddie's securities are "deemed to be exempt securities" under SEC law "to the same extent as" direct U.S. government obligations. Again — treated like Treasury paper for market purposes.
  • §1455(h)(2), literally the very next subsection after the SEC-exempt language: Freddie "shall insert appropriate language in all of the obligations and securities" it issues "clearly indicating that such obligations and securities, together with the interest thereon, are not guaranteed by the United States and do not constitute a debt or obligation of the United States or any agency or instrumentality thereof other than the Corporation."

Put those three next to each other and you get the whole Freddie Mac con in miniature: state-tax-exempt like the government, market-treated like the government, litigation-jurisdiction-"deemed an agency" like the government (§1452(f)) — but the fine print on every bond says, in effect, "don't blame Uncle Sam if we default." That fine print held up fine right until September 2008, when Treasury bought preferred stock and effectively made the implicit guarantee explicit anyway. The label said "not government-backed." The bailout said otherwise. Investors priced Freddie debt at near-Treasury rates for decades because everyone quietly bet the label was a lie — and they were right.

A second strange item worth a flag: §1454(a)(2) hard-codes specific dollar conforming-loan-limit numbers ($417,000 for a single-family mortgage, etc.) directly into the U.S. Code, with a built-in annual escalator tied to a government house-price index (§1454(a)(2), referencing 12 U.S.C. §4542) — meaning Congress literally wrote a national home-price-index-linked mortgage ceiling into permanent law, which quietly makes Freddie (and Fannie) the mechanism that defines, nationwide, what counts as a "normal" versus "jumbo" mortgage.

Layer map — how we got here

  1. 1970 — Emergency Home Finance Act, Title III (the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation Act). Congress creates Freddie Mac as a corporate instrumentality, capitalized entirely by the Federal Home Loan Banks (nonvoting stock only), board partly appointed by the President, granted quasi-sovereign immunities (§1456(a), original text: entitled to immunities "as if it were the United States or... an unincorporated agency of the United States"). Purpose: build a secondary market for conventional (non-federally-insured) home mortgages.
  2. 1989 — FIRREA (Pub. L. 101-73). Converts Freddie from an FHLBank-owned instrumentality into a shareholder-owned stock corporation. Senior preferred stock becomes voting common stock (§1453 note). Strikes the "as if it were the United States" immunity clause (§1456(a)) and the "without regard to any other law" charter-supremacy clause (§1459). Replaces the FHLBank guaranty of Freddie's securities with a capped ($2.25B) Treasury purchase line (§1455(c)). This is the moment Freddie stops looking like a government arm on paper and starts looking like a company that happens to have a federal birth certificate.
  3. 1992 — Federal Housing Enterprises Financial Safety and Soundness Act. Adds capital-adequacy rules, a dedicated safety-and-soundness regulator (OFHEO, HUD-housed), executive-comp reporting, GAO/independent audit detail, and the Affordable Housing Advisory Council. Tightens the screws without changing the basic ownership structure.
  4. 2008 — HERA (Housing and Economic Recovery Act, Pub. L. 110-289). Two things happen in the same statute, pulling in opposite directions: (a) it strips the President's board-appointment and removal power from Freddie's own charter (§1452(a)(2)), making the board formally 100% shareholder-elected; and (b) it creates FHFA (replacing OFHEO) and gives FHFA's Director sweeping conservatorship/receivership power over Freddie under 12 U.S.C. §4617 — a power exercised within weeks of HERA's passage. It also adds the open-ended emergency Treasury purchase authority at §1455(l). Freddie has been in FHFA conservatorship continuously since September 2008 — this is the entity's actual current legal status, even though the text of its own charter (12 U.S.C. §§1451-1459) still describes a shareholder-controlled private corporation. The conservatorship itself is FHFA's power, defined in FHFA's own charter provisions, not in the FHLMC Act reproduced here — but no honest read of "who controls Freddie Mac" can skip it.

Entity Theory read

This thing is a ghost wearing a business suit. Congress built it, dressed it up as a private company so it could borrow cheap and stay off the federal balance sheet, then quietly kept a leash long enough to yank the moment the housing market blew up. The "not backed by the U.S. government" label on every bond was always a polite lie — everyone from Wall Street to the Chinese central bank priced it as a lie, and in 2008 the lie got paid off in full with taxpayer money anyway. Change of control doesn't kill this creature. It already survived one full change of control (1989, appointed board to shareholder board) and another de facto one (2008, shareholder board to federal conservator) without ever dissolving, because the charter made damn sure only Congress itself can pull the plug — and Congress has never wanted to.
