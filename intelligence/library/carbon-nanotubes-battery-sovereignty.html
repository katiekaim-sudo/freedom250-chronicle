Nanotubes help batteries, but the U.S. lacks a full home supply chain

A tiny amount of carbon nanotubes can make battery electrodes work better, especially silicon anodes and thick electrodes. But the U.S. has only pieces of a supply chain, not a proven mass-scale chain from domestic nanotube production to finished cells.

What we found

  • Nanotubes are a helper material, not a new battery chemistry. In ORNL pouch-cell tests a nanotube network kept 99.4 percent of capacity after 200 cycles versus 94.6 percent with carbon black, with a calculated 11.7 percent energy-density gain.
  • Argonne's 2024 tests found 0.1 weight percent of multi-wall nanotubes improved NMC811 rate performance, but these were coin cells, not car-scale cells.
  • U.S. pieces exist: artience/LioChem runs dispersion production in Georgia with two North American customers as of March 2026; CHASM has a 50-tonne-per-year pilot reactor in Oklahoma, with a 1,500-tonne reactor still to be built; Cnano USA's Kansas project has a Chinese parent and a congressional CFIUS request.
  • Cabot's Michigan plant, billed as the first U.S. commercial-scale battery-grade nanotube facility, was only selected for DOE award negotiation in September 2024. That is not a final award, construction or output.
  • Larger proven capacity is abroad: LG Chem lists 2,900 tonnes per year in South Korea, Arkema up to 400 tonnes in France and OCSiAl 60 tonnes in Serbia.

Why it matters

It shows that rebuilding industrial supply chains at home is not just about building plants. A U.S. address can still depend on foreign powder, recipes or ownership, so real control depends on qualification, origin and customer acceptance.

What to watch

  • pending · Whether Cabot's Michigan project moves from award selection to a final award, construction and customer-qualified output.
  • pending · Proof of commissioned output and customer acceptance from Cnano USA in Kansas and CHASM's planned 1,500-tonne reactor.
