# SEC–CFTC Current Authority and Operative Change Ledger

## Research contract

**Question:** Without relying on the proposed CLARITY Act, what can the SEC and
CFTC already do under the statutes they administer, and what have they already
changed for crypto, tokenization and connected market infrastructure?

**Mode:** present-law authority audit and operative-change ledger.

**Scope:** current federal securities and commodities statutes; Commission
rules, interpretations, orders and registrations; effective SRO rules; staff
guidance, withdrawals and no-action positions; and observed operating evidence
where located. CLARITY is used only to mark the present statutory boundary, not
as the engine of the analysis. Banking, payment, AML, tax, state commercial law
and bankruptcy are included only where they delimit SEC or CFTC power.

**Cutoff:** 2026-08-26 EDT. Legal authority, instrument state and operation are
kept separate.

**Mutation boundary:** Workbench research only. No live-vault event, canonical
watch, plotline, app state or legislative forecast was changed.

## Controlling answer

The claim that the SEC and CFTC can proceed without CLARITY is **substantially
correct, but easy to overstate**. Neither agency possesses a free-standing
power to regulate “crypto.” Each has a collection of old, function-specific
powers that already reach large parts of the new stack.

- The **SEC already has a complete securities-side toolkit**. It can interpret
  the statutory security and investment-contract perimeter; register or exempt
  offerings; regulate exchanges, ATSs, brokers, custody, transfer agents,
  clearing agencies and investment companies; approve SRO rules and ETP listing
  standards; and use broad but bounded exemptive authorities.
- The **CFTC already has a complete derivatives-side toolkit plus several
  commodity-side hooks**. It can regulate futures, options, swaps, DCMs, SEFs,
  DCOs and FCMs; review or accept product and rule filings; reach specified
  leveraged, margined or financed retail commodity transactions; police fraud
  and manipulation in commodity cash markets; grant bounded CEA section 4(c)
  exemptions; and adapt collateral, customer-property and operating rules for
  registrants.
- The principal missing object is a **comprehensive federal registration,
  conduct, surveillance and customer-protection constitution for ordinary
  unleveraged spot trading in non-security digital commodities**. The CFTC's
  cash-market antifraud and manipulation authority is not that constitution,
  and SEC regulation of a securities wrapper or mixed venue does not convert
  the underlying commodity into a security.

The present plotline is therefore not “the agencies are waiting for Congress.”
It is: **the agencies are already assembling most securities, derivatives,
clearing, custody and collateral layers through modular uses of existing law;
Congress would chiefly allocate and harden the uncovered spot-market center.**

## Read the instrument before the headline

| Legal state | What changed | What did not |
|---|---|---|
| Statute or existing Commission rule | The agency has durable authority within the text and judicial construction. | It does not authorize action outside the statute's subject, procedures or required findings. |
| Commission rule, interpretation, order or registration | The Commission acted as a body on the exact legal object. An order or registration is operative on its facts and conditions; an interpretation states the Commission's current construction. | It is not legislation. Courts can reject an interpretation; a facts-specific order is not a market-wide permission. |
| Effective SRO rule | The exchange or clearing-organization rule has entered the operative rulebook through the statutory filing route. | Immediate effectiveness is not an affirmative approval order, product launch or observed use. |
| Staff statement, FAQ, withdrawal or no-action letter | The administering staff has changed its public interpretation or enforcement posture for stated facts. | It has no independent force of law, does not bind the Commission, courts or private plaintiffs, and may be withdrawn. |
| Proposal, agenda item, request for comment or speech | It reveals direction, a legal theory or intended use of authority. | No operative rule or exemption has changed. |

## I. SEC: what present law already permits

### A. Current-authority map

| Function | Existing authority | What the SEC can do now | Present ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classify the asset and transaction | Securities Act §2(a)(1); Exchange Act §3(a)(10); *Howey*, *Reves* and other judicial constructions | Interpret and enforce the terms “security,” “investment contract,” stock, note and other enumerated instruments; distinguish a non-security crypto asset from a securities transaction involving it. | The Commission cannot legislate a new asset class into the statutory definition or bind courts to its interpretation. |
| Regulate offerings | Securities Act §§5, 7, 10, 17; statutory and regulatory exemptions; Securities Act §28 | Require registration and disclosure, enforce antifraud law, or create bounded exemptions by rule when the statutory findings are met. | Existing exemptions remain bounded by statute; staff no-action relief is not an exemption; a proposed safe harbor changes nothing until adopted. |
| Regulate securities venues and brokers | Exchange Act §§5, 6 and 15; Regulation ATS | Register and supervise exchanges and broker-dealers, permit ATS operation inside the broker-dealer framework, and apply securities duties to a security leg or mixed trading pair. | It does not acquire comprehensive jurisdiction over an ordinary non-security commodity merely because that commodity trades beside a security. |
| Approve SRO rules and listings | Exchange Act §19(b); Rules 19b-4 and 19b-4(e); §6(b)(5) | Approve or permit effectiveness of exchange and clearing-agency rule changes; establish generic listing standards for qualifying securities wrappers. | Approval of an exchange rule or ETP share is not approval of the issuer, blockchain, investment merits or underlying commodity market. |
| Regulate custody and customer protection | Exchange Act §§15(c) and 17; Rule 15c3-3; Investment Company Act §17(f) | Define or interpret possession/control and qualified-custody routes for regulated brokers and funds; grant narrow relief. | Key control alone does not create SIPA protection, legal title, bankruptcy priority or protection for non-security crypto. |
| Regulate ownership records and transfer agents | Exchange Act §17A(c); Rules 17Ad | Permit DLT to perform all or part of the official master securityholder-file function while enforcing record accuracy, production and safeguarding duties. | Federal securities guidance does not decide all state corporate, UCC, contract or insolvency questions. |
| Register and supervise securities clearing | Exchange Act §17A; Rule 17Ab2-1 | Register clearing agencies temporarily or permanently, supervise SRO rulebooks and standards, and condition or exempt exact functions. | The SEC cannot supply the bank cash leg, stablecoin redemption, central-bank access or cross-regime bankruptcy treatment. |
| Regulate investment companies | Investment Company Act, including §§6(c), 17 and 22 | Grant class or application-specific exemptions and adapt dealing, custody and affiliated-transaction rules for tokenized funds. | Registration-statement effectiveness is not product approval; token form does not erase fund-law duties. |
| Grant exemptions | Securities Act §28; Exchange Act §36; Investment Company Act §6(c) | Create conditional rules, orders, pilots, safe harbors and application-specific relief when public-interest and investor-protection findings are satisfied. | The provisions contain subject and procedural limits. Exemption cannot transfer CFTC, banking, Fed, state-law or bankruptcy jurisdiction to the SEC. |
| Enforce existing law | Securities Act §17(a); Exchange Act §10(b), Rule 10b-5 and other provisions | Investigate and prosecute securities fraud, unregistered offerings, exchange/broker violations and misleading disclosures involving crypto. | Enforcement discretion cannot rewrite a statute, immunize conduct from private plaintiffs or adjudicate non-SEC legal regimes. |

Primary statutory spine: [Securities Act definition of “security”](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A15%20section%3A77b%20edition%3Aprelim%29), [Exchange Act definition](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A15%20section%3A78c%20edition%3Aprelim%29), [Securities Act §28](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section77z-3), [Exchange Act §36](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A15%20section%3A78mm%20edition%3Aprelim%29), and [Investment Company Act §6(c)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section80a-6).

- Regulation Crypto Assets, S7-2026-27, remains a proposal.

## II. CFTC: what present law already permits

### A. Current-authority map

| Function | Existing authority | What the CFTC can do now | Present ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulate commodity derivatives | CEA §§2(a)(1)(A), 4, 5, 5a, 5b and 5c; Parts 37–40 | Regulate futures, options and swaps; register and supervise DCMs, SEFs, DCOs, FCMs and other intermediaries; review or accept product and rule filings. | A spot reference price or digital underlying does not remove a derivative from the CEA, but derivatives authority does not become a general spot-exchange regime. |
| Reach leveraged retail commodity trades | CEA §2(c)(2)(D) | Treat specified leveraged, margined or financed retail commodity transactions as if they were futures unless an exception—most importantly actual delivery within 28 days—applies. | Ordinary fully paid spot trades outside the provision are not comprehensively regulated by this hook. |
| Police commodity cash markets | CEA §6(c)(1) and Regulation 180.1 | Bring fraud and manipulation cases involving cash commodity markets, including virtual currencies. | General antifraud/manipulation enforcement is not venue registration, prudential supervision, custody regulation or continuous market surveillance. |
| Approve or accept products and rules | CEA §5c(c); Parts 38–40 | Approve products voluntarily submitted under Regulation 40.3 and receive self-certifications under Regulations 40.2/40.6; enforce DCM core principles and product/rule conditions. | Approval or certification is product- or rule-specific and remains subject to all applicable CEA duties. |
| Regulate clearing, margin and customer property | CEA §§4d and 5b; Parts 1, 22, 30, 39 and 190 | Supervise DCO/FCM segregation, eligible collateral, valuation, haircuts, custody, risk and default treatment; grant conditional relief inside that constitution. | A permitted stablecoin or digital asset is not automatically a permitted investment, eligible margin in every account, or bankruptcy-remote customer property. |
| Permit foreign futures access | CEA and Part 30; foreign-board-of-trade framework | Classify qualifying foreign crypto derivatives as foreign futures and condition how U.S. FCMs intermediate and margin them. | Staff interpretation/no-action does not domestically register the foreign venue or displace foreign and customer-property law. |
| Grant exemptions | CEA §4(c) | By rule, regulation or order and after required process, exempt specified transactions or persons to promote responsible innovation and fair competition when the statutory tests are met. | Section 4(c) has eligibility, public-interest, Act-purpose and anti-evasion limits; it cannot create a new congressional spot mandate or rewrite other statutes. |
| Coordinate and harmonize | Existing SEC and CFTC rulemaking, exemptive and supervisory powers | Harmonize definitions, data standards, margin, dual-registrant treatment and coordinated exemptions where each agency already has jurisdiction. | Coordination cannot merge statutes or confer one agency's jurisdiction on the other. |

Primary statutory spine: [CEA §2 and the retail-commodity provision](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title7-section2), [CEA §4(c) and transaction provisions](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2023&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-2023-title7-section6), [CEA §6(c)(1), codified at 7 U.S.C. §9](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title7-section9), and [CFTC Regulation 180.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-I/part-180/section-180.1).

### C. CFTC items that have **not** created a comprehensive spot regime

- A chair speech, “crypto sprint,” request for comment or advisory is not a
  Commission rule.
- Permission for a DCM to list one spot or perpetual product does not register
  every centralized spot platform, dealer, broker or custodian.
- Cash-market antifraud and anti-manipulation authority does not by itself
  impose routine capital, custody, segregation, disclosure, listing,
  examination or bankruptcy rules on ordinary spot venues.
- Staff collateral relief does not amend the Commission's permitted-investment
  rule, give every DCO the same eligibility decision, or establish use.

## III. What the agencies have already changed together

The joint program is an adapter protocol between two statutes, not a merger.

1. **Current-law spot path:** joint staff publicly said registered SEC and CFTC
   exchanges are not prohibited from facilitating specified spot commodity
   products. Each venue still needs the correct rule, product and operating
   path.
2. **Shared classification language:** the March 2026 SEC interpretation was
   issued with CFTC guidance, reducing disagreement over the asset/contract
   boundary without changing either statute.
3. **Formal coordination:** the agencies' 2026-03-11 memorandum covers
   definitions, clearing, margin, collateral, dual registrants, reporting,
   examinations, surveillance and enforcement. It creates a coordination
   mechanism, not transferred jurisdiction. [SEC–CFTC MOU](https://www.cftc.gov/media/13471/SEC_CFTC_MOU031126/download)
4. **Coordinated exemptive agenda:** the chairs identified definitions, data,
   capital, margin, P2P, leveraged/margined transactions and perpetuals as
   areas where they can coordinate existing rulemaking and exemptive powers.
5. **Cross-margin constitution:** the paired SEC/CFTC CME–FICC actions show the
   agencies can join securities and futures positions today when the account,
   lien, customer-property, insolvency and supervisory consequences are stated
   explicitly.

The chairs' strongest formulation is itself bounded: they described
harmonizing rules and using existing exemptive authority, not acquiring a new
undifferentiated crypto mandate. [Joint chairs' statement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/joint-statement-atkins-pham-090525)

## IV. What does **not** require CLARITY

Subject to ordinary notice, findings, Commission voting and judicial-review
requirements, the agencies can already:

- decide whether a transaction involves a security or regulated derivative;
- register, supervise, discipline and condition securities and derivatives
  intermediaries already within their statutes;
- approve or permit SRO, DCM, DCO and product-rule changes;
- create bounded exemptions, pilots and temporary registrations within their
  statutory exemptive powers;
- permit tokenized securities, shareholder records, entitlement records and
  settlement systems that satisfy existing securities law;
- permit crypto ETP wrappers and in-kind ETP operations;
- approve crypto futures, options and product-specific perpetuals;
- regulate specified leveraged or financed retail commodity transactions;
- adapt custody, collateral, margin, segregation and continuous-operation
  expectations for existing registrants;
- police securities fraud and commodity cash-market fraud/manipulation; and
- coordinate definitions, reporting, margin and dual-registrant treatment
  where both statutes already reach the activity.

## V. What still needs Congress—or another sovereign authority

### A. The principal congressional gap

A comprehensive federal market constitution for **ordinary, unleveraged spot
trading in non-security digital commodities** requires a statutory grant if it
is to include the things that cash-market antifraud authority does not supply:

- venue, dealer, broker and custodian registration;
- routine examinations and continuous surveillance;
- capital, segregation, custody and conflicts standards;
- listing, disclosure, recordkeeping and reporting duties;
- customer-protection and failure-resolution rules; and
- a clean allocation between the SEC and CFTC that is durable against changes
  in agency interpretation.

This is the center that CLARITY or another market-structure statute could
create. It should not be described as the source of every crypto power around
it.

### B. Other boundaries neither agency can solve alone

| Boundary | Why SEC/CFTC action is insufficient |
|---|---|
| Statutory definitions and jurisdiction | Agencies can interpret ambiguity and exempt within delegated limits; they cannot repeal statutory text, transfer congressional jurisdiction or prevent courts from rejecting their reading. |
| Bank and stablecoin-issuer powers | Charters, deposits, reserves, redemption, capital, liquidity and prudential supervision belong principally to banking/stablecoin authorities, not securities or commodities regulators. |
| Federal Reserve access and dollar finality | Fed accounts, payment services, intraday credit and central-bank settlement are Federal Reserve and statutory eligibility questions. A token or trading permission does not supply them. |
| AML, sanctions and tax | Treasury/FinCEN, OFAC, IRS and Congress own material parts of these regimes. SEC/CFTC market permissions do not displace them. |
| State commercial and corporate law | Issuer records, property rights, controllable electronic records, securities entitlements, perfection and priority often turn on state corporate law, UCC Articles 8, 9 and 12, contracts and choice of law. |
| Bankruptcy and customer property | SEC/CFTC can define regulated account treatment within delegated rules, but generally rewriting SIPA, the Bankruptcy Code or cross-regime statutory priority requires Congress. SEC staff says non-security crypto is not SIPA-protected and even unregistered investment contracts fall outside the relevant definition. The CME–FICC order works by making a narrow choice inside existing law, not erasing the statutes. [SEC Trading and Markets FAQs](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/trading-markets-frequently-asked-questions/frequently-asked-questions-relating-crypto-asset-activities-distributed-ledger-technology) |
| Private rights and judicial review | Staff forbearance and agency interpretations do not bind private plaintiffs or Article III courts. Under *Loper Bright*, a reviewing court independently determines statutory meaning while respecting actual delegations. Durable immunity or a new cause of action requires statutory authority. [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-451_7m58.pdf) |

## VI. The corrected regulatory thesis

The most accurate thesis is narrower—and stronger—than “Congress will bring
clarity”:

> **The SEC and CFTC are already constitutionalizing tokenized finance through
> the securities and derivatives systems they administer. They are changing
> classification, wrappers, exchange rules, broker custody, transfer-agent
> records, clearing registrations, perpetual products, collateral and market
> hours through existing interpretation, approval, registration, exemptive and
> supervisory powers. The remaining congressional center is not permission to
> begin; it is a comprehensive and durable federal constitution for ordinary
> spot non-security digital-commodity markets, plus any cross-statute customer
> property and jurisdictional reallocations Congress chooses to make.**

That framing changes what Freedom 250 should watch. The material clocks are no
longer only bill passage and final rulemaking. They are:

1. exact Commission orders and their conditions;
2. effective SRO, DCM and DCO rule changes;
3. staff relief converted into real participant use;
4. first funded collateral, custody, clearing and default-path evidence;
5. the record that controls title and correction when systems disagree; and
6. any court decision that narrows or rejects the agencies' current-law
   interpretations.

## VII. Highest-value follow-up research

1. **CFTC listed-spot operating census.** Identify every product, DCM, clearing
   path, launch date, asset, custody chain and retail-transaction theory behind
   the “listed spot” initiative. Separate staff permission, product filing,
   live trade and customer-protection state.
2. **Relief-to-production ledger.** For DTC, HQLAx, SEC broker custody, CFTC
   Letter 26-05 and 24/7 operations, find the first named participant, funded
   use, haircut, volume, incident and examination evidence. Relief without use
   is a legal opening, not a functioning market.
3. **Customer-property concordance.** Run one failure fact pattern through SEC
   Rule 15c3-3 and SIPA, CFTC Parts 1/22/30/190, DTC/DCO/FCM rulebooks, UCC
   Articles 8/9/12 and the Bankruptcy Code.
4. **Exemptive-authority ceiling memo.** Compare SEC §§28/36/6(c) and CFTC §4(c)
   procedure, required findings, anti-evasion limits, judicial review and the
   largest lawful class exemption each could issue without new legislation.
5. **Enforcement-discretion reversal ledger.** Separate dismissed actions,
   withdrawn appeals, staff withdrawals, Commission interpretation, binding
   adjudications and unresolved private litigation. This is the fragility map
   for the present permissive turn.
