Reading the Charter — CFTC

Every citation below points to a section actually captured verbatim in this folder. Where the checklist item isn't answered by what's captured, that's flagged as a gap, not filled from memory.

1. Immunities & liability shields

None found. These four files carry no sovereign-immunity, suit-immunity, or asset-protection clause for the Commission or its officers — the opposite, in fact: §2(a)(1)(B) makes a principal (individual, association, partnership, corporation, or trust) liable for the acts of its agents acting within the scope of employment. That's a liability-expanding clause aimed at regulated firms, not a shield for the Commission. A genuine immunity clause, if one exists for CFTC officers/records, isn't in the sections captured here — flagged as a gap.

2. Tax exemptions

None found in these four files, and none expected — the CFTC has no securities, obligations, or salaries carved out here. Not a load-bearing gap; this agency's charter doesn't run on the tax-exemption logic of, say, the IMF or the regional Feds. Not further pursued.

3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause

Appointment — §2(a)(2)(A): "There is hereby established, as an independent agency of the United States Government, a Commodity Futures Trading Commission. The Commission shall be composed of five Commissioners who shall be appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate." Not more than three of five may be the same political party. Terms are five years, staggered (originally seeded 1/2/3/4/5 years so no single President refills the whole board at once), and a Commissioner "hold[s] office... until his successor is appointed and has qualified," but not beyond the next session of Congress after the fixed term expires.

Chairman — §2(a)(2)(B): appointed by the President with Senate consent from among the five, and "shall serve as Chairman at the pleasure of the President." The President can swap chairmen at will; the demoted chairman just reverts to serving out their term as an ordinary Commissioner.

Removal — the gap that matters: §2(a)(2) contains no express removal-for-cause standard for the five Commissioners themselves. Compare the FTC Act's "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" language — nothing like it appears anywhere in the captured text of §2. This was checked specifically this session by re-fetching the full current text of 7 U.S.C. §2 from Cornell LII (retrieved 2026-07-10) side by side with the vault file: the vault file's (a)(2)(A), (a)(2)(B), and (a)(3) [vacancies] are word-for-word complete and accurate, and the surrounding subsections ((a)(1)(C)–(D), (a)(4) General Counsel) contain no removal clause either. Confirmed, not fixed: if Commissioner removal protection exists at all, it isn't written into this statute — it would rest entirely on judge-made doctrine layered on top (the Humphrey's Executor line), not on the charter's own words. That doctrine is currently contested territory in the courts and is not statute text, so it isn't quoted here.

§2(a)(4) General Counsel (read this session, not separately captured verbatim — one sentence, low stakes): the General Counsel is appointed by the Commission and serves "at the pleasure of the Commission." Ordinary at-will staff appointment, not a control-of-the-agency question.

4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations — THE FAULT LINE

§16(d): "There are authorized to be appropriated such sums as are necessary to carry out this chapter for each of the fiscal years 2008 through 2013." That is the entire operative funding clause, verbatim, and it expired in 2013. No re-authorization since. Per the funding-clause amendment history captured in the §16 file, this subsection has been re-windowed by Congress roughly every few years since 1974 (FY1975–78, then 1982, 1986, 1989, an open-ended "such sums as necessary" version in 1992, a rare dollar-figure version in 1995, FY1995–2005, then the FY2008–2013 text now sitting on the books unrenewed for over a decade).

No user fees. No transaction assessments. No share of exchange revenue, no seigniorage, nothing off-budget. §16(c) even spells out that Commissioner expenses are paid only "on the presentation of itemized vouchers therefor approved by the Commission" — ordinary appropriated-funds housekeeping, not an independent income stream.

This is the fault line. Every other major U.S. financial regulator has escaped the ordinary appropriations leash in some way: the Federal Reserve funds itself from its own operations and remits the excess to Treasury; the FDIC runs on bank-paid premiums; the SEC and the OCC run on fees assessed on the industries they regulate. The CFTC has none of that. It is the only one of the major financial cops still fed entirely, year to year, out of Congress's discretionary appropriations process — on a statutory authorization window that lapsed in 2013 and nobody has bothered to renew, because annual appropriations riders have simply kept funding it anyway without needing the underlying authorization current. Whoever writes the CFTC's line item in the annual appropriations bill holds the actual leash on this agency — independence on paper (§2(a)(2)(A): "independent agency"), dependence in practice (§16(d)).

5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

§12(a)(1): the Commission may investigate and publish general statistical findings, but may not "publish data and information that would separately disclose the business transactions or market positions of any person and trade secrets or names of customers," and may withhold anything tied to a pending investigation. Data obtained from a foreign futures authority is protected from compelled disclosure if that authority certifies disclosure would violate its own law (§12(a)(1)(A)–(B)) — with a specific carve-back for congressional, administrative, judicial, and bankruptcy proceedings. The statute explicitly designates this subsection as a FOIA Exemption 3 statute: "For purposes of section 552 of title 5, this subsection shall be considered a statute described in subsection (b)(3)(B) of section 552."

Congress always gets in anyway — §12(a)(1) and §12(e): "Nothing in this subsection shall be construed to authorize the Commission to withhold information or data from Congress," and on request of any congressional committee "acting within the scope of its jurisdiction," the Commission "shall furnish" trader names, addresses, and positions. No discretion, no confidentiality shield reaches Congress.

§12(i) — no GAO carve-out at all: "The Comptroller General of the United States shall conduct reviews and audits of the Commission," with full access to "any books, documents, papers, or records of the Commission." The only limit is the Comptroller General also can't publish individual trade secrets/business transactions in a public report — but even that data "shall be provided upon request by any committee of either House of Congress." Net effect: zero audit exemption from GAO, zero real secrecy from Congress. Compare this to entities elsewhere in the library with GAO exam-confidentiality carve-outs — the CFTC has none.

6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

Not applicable in the treaty-organization sense (no member states to withdraw). The functional equivalent — who can rewrite the CFTC's rules — is simply: Congress, by ordinary statute, at any time, as the amendment history embedded in every captured §12 and §16 source line shows (nine-plus amending Public Laws apiece from 1922 to 2010). No supermajority, no entrenchment clause, no special procedure protecting the Act from ordinary repeal. This is itself notable in the entity-theory sense: nothing here is locked in constitutionally the way, say, the Federal Reserve's structure has become politically locked in by convention — it is one ordinary statute away from being rewritten.

7. Emergency powers

Not captured verbatim in any of these four files. In the course of re-fetching §2 this session to check item 1, a cross-reference surfaced to §12a(9) (7 U.S.C. §12a(9)) — the Commission's power to declare an "emergency" and order a contract market to raise margin levels — inside the omitted SEC/CFTC-boundary text at §2(a)(1)(C)(v)(V). That's a pointer to where emergency power lives in the Act, not the operative text itself, and it is not quoted here because it wasn't captured verbatim this session. Flagged as a clear next-capture target: §8a and §12a carry the Commission's emergency and enforcement powers and aren't in this folder yet.

8. The definitions section

Only 3 of 51 numbered definitions in §1a are captured (by design — see that file's frontmatter), but the one that matters most is here in full: (9) Commodity is a named list (wheat, cotton, livestock, orange juice concentrate, etc.) followed by a deliberately open tail — "and all other goods and articles... and all services, rights, and interests... in which contracts for future delivery are presently or in the future dealt in." That tail is the quietly load-bearing part the checklist warns about: it turns a 1936 list of farm crops into a definition that can swallow anything anyone starts trading futures on. (27) Future delivery is defined only negatively — it "does not include any sale of any cash commodity for deferred shipment or delivery" — so its true boundary is set by exclusion, not by what it affirmatively covers. (47) Swap, added whole-cloth by Dodd-Frank in 2010, is the modern jurisdiction-expander: six clauses of "in general" (A) sweeping in everything from interest-rate swaps to credit default swaps to "any... transaction that is, or in the future becomes, commonly known to the trade as a swap" (a self-updating catch-all), balanced against a long exclusions list (B) that carves securities-law instruments back out to the SEC.

9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

Not addressed in any of the four captured files. No corporate-life-span clause, no wind-up/asset-disposition provision. Ordinary posture for a federal regulatory agency: it exists until Congress repeals the Act, full stop, no charter renewal date to watch. Nothing to flag here beyond the absence itself.

10. Anything strange

The clause nobody expects, sitting in plain sight at §16(h): "A swap — (1) shall not be considered to be insurance; and (2) may not be regulated as an insurance contract under the law of any State." Two sentences, added by Dodd-Frank in 2010, and they exist because of one company: a credit default swap is functionally an insurance policy against default, AIG sold an ocean of them through a unit no insurance regulator was watching, and when the bets went bad in 2008 the federal government had to backstop it. This clause is Congress nailing the door shut afterward — swaps are federally regulated financial instruments now, permanently, and no state insurance commissioner gets to claim jurisdiction over them ever again, no matter how insurance-like they behave.

Runner-up: the elaborate foreign-authority confidentiality chain in §12(e) — the Commission can hand trader names and positions to a foreign regulator, a foreign central bank, or a foreign government ministry, but only if it's first satisfied the recipient won't leak it except in an adjudicatory proceeding. A U.S. financial regulator quietly built out a whole international confidentiality-reciprocity architecture inside a "public disclosure" section named for exactly the opposite purpose.

Layer map

  • Grain Futures Act, 1922 (ch. 369, 42 Stat. 998) — the root. Built the disclosure regime (§8, now §12) and the Commission's basic operating powers (§12, now §16) around grain futures only, hooked to interstate-commerce jurisdiction.
  • Commodity Exchange Act, 1936 (ch. 545, 49 Stat. 1491) — renamed the Act, blew the commodity list open past grain (§1a(9)'s named list plus its open tail), and built the contract-market designation system the whole regulatory structure still runs on.
  • CFTC Act, 1974 (Pub. L. 93-463, 88 Stat. 1389) — created the Commission itself as a new independent five-member agency (§2(a)(2)), pulling jurisdiction away from the Department of Agriculture, and granted it exclusive jurisdiction over futures (§2(a)(1)(A)) — the single biggest jurisdictional grab in the Act's history.
  • Shad-Johnson Accord, 1982 (Pub. L. 97-444) — drew the line between the CFTC and the SEC: futures and options-on-futures to the CFTC, options-on-securities to the SEC (§2(a)(1)(C)–(D), described but not verbatim-captured in the §2 file's omission note).
  • Commodity Futures Modernization Act, 2000 (Pub. L. 106-554) — deregulated most over-the-counter swaps out of CEA coverage entirely (the "Enron loophole" era); its fingerprints are in the 2000 amendment marks on both the §12 and §16 source lines captured here.
  • Dodd-Frank, 2010 (Pub. L. 111-203, Title VII) — swung the pendulum back: added the "swap" definition (§1a(47)), extended CFTC exclusive jurisdiction to swaps (§2(a)(1)(A)'s reference to the "Wall Street Transparency and Accountability Act of 2010"), and closed the insurance loophole (§16(h)).

Entity Theory read

The CFTC is a five-headed committee-creature, not a king — staggered five-year terms mean no single President refills the whole board in one term, and the "not more than three of one party" rule forces a minority seat by law. But look at where the armor is thin: the chair sits "at the pleasure of the President" outright (§2(a)(2)(B)), and the statute never once writes down a for-cause removal standard for the ordinary Commissioners the way its sister agencies got. Whatever protects a sitting Commissioner from being fired at will lives in old court doctrine bolted on from outside, not in this charter's own words — and that doctrine is being tested in the courts right now. Feed it, though, and the real leash shows: it eats ordinary year-to-year appropriations off a funding authorization that quietly expired in 2013 (§16(d)) and was never renewed, while the Fed prints its own budget, the FDIC lives off bank premiums, and the SEC runs on industry fees. Of all the entities standing between Katie's money and the market, this is the one still standing at the counter every October with its hand out to Congress.
