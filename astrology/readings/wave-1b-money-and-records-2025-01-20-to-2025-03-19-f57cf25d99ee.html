Wave 1B — Money and Records

Research window: January 20 through March 19, 2025.
Question: Before the later payment-modernization orders, how did the incoming regime begin moving review upstream into funding portals, payment files, system access, written justifications and public records?
Finding: This subwave acquired a real operating body unusually quickly. The center tried a categorical funding review, Treasury opened a bounded payment-process engagement, career staff and DOGE-linked employees queried and automated payment-file review, courts imposed competing limits, a credit-card audit was already operating, and a public grant-payment feed appeared by March 10. What did not transfer was just as important: program agencies remained the legal payment decision-makers, Treasury remained the payment operator, privacy and appropriations law constrained access, and a public line item did not become a universal ledger or a fraud judgment.

Astrology is used here as archetypal weather and chapter structure. The dates establish the institutional chain; the sky describes its natural thematic rhyme without proving causation or requiring exact-day correspondence.

The chain in one view

    ↓

This is not a story of Treasury silently inheriting every spending decision. It is a story about where observation and review can be inserted before release, and about which legal actor still owns the final instruction.

1B.1 — January 20–31: the funding pause meets the payment portals

January 20 — foreign assistance becomes the first money-review test

Mover. The President, OMB, agency heads and the Secretary of State.

Institutional change. EO 14169 directed a 90-day pause of new foreign-development obligations and disbursements while programs were reviewed. OMB was assigned enforcement through apportionment; agency heads retained the program review; the Secretary of State held concurrence, early-resumption and program-specific waiver roles.

Maturity. Effective direction with a defined review and waiver architecture.

Counterevidence and limit. The order did not make Treasury the legal owner of foreign-assistance programs. It did not erase congressional appropriations, program statutes, existing obligations or judicial review. The decision chain remained divided among the program agency, State, OMB and Treasury payment operations.

Plotline. The regime begins with a proposed rule: no money should move without being legible to the new policy center and affirmatively aligned with its terms.

January 26 — Treasury opens an end-to-end payment-process engagement

Mover. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Bureau of the Fiscal Service officials, Treasury employee Tom Krause and temporary Treasury employee Marko Elez.

Institutional change. The later official court record establishes that Fiscal Service initiated a four-to-six-week engagement with the Treasury DOGE Team on January 26. Its purposes were to understand the full payment process across multiple systems, identify security and efficiency gaps, improve payment integrity, and make sure payment files carried appropriate Treasury Account Symbols and related codes. The engagement required source-code review and access to sensitive production data.

Maturity. Operating system-review project.

Counterevidence and limit. Treasury officials recognized that the cross-system access was broader than prior single-user practice and carried operational, privacy and insider-risk concerns. Krause did not have direct personal system access; Elez was the technical user. A secure sandbox and read-only controls were intended to prevent changes to production systems.

Primary record. Alliance v. Bessent, March 7 opinion, pp. 10–14.

January 26–February 10 — foreign-aid policy becomes a file-review path

Mover. Treasury leadership, Fiscal Service career staff, the Treasury DOGE Team, State Department officials and the originating agencies.

Institutional change. Treasury first asked Fiscal Service to identify USAID files in the Payment Automation Manager landing zone for State review. The process changed the next day when State planned to intercept files before submission. On January 31, Fiscal Service was directed to query four Treasury Account Symbols associated with possible foreign-aid payments and send matching files to designated State officials. Career staff performed the manual query; Elez later assisted in automating it.

Maturity. A real, bounded pre-processing review path.

Observed result. The court record identifies one payment halted on February 10 after the Department of the Interior, acting for the Millennium Challenge Corporation, asked Fiscal Service not to process it. Four other files were forwarded to State and then processed normally. This is proof of an operating review, not proof that Treasury independently rejected a program payment.

Counterevidence and limit. The originating or program-side official remained the actor that instructed whether the file should proceed. Treasury's February 4 congressional letter said the review had not itself suspended or rejected agency instructions and that the responsible agency continued to drive the payment process.

Plotline. The new control plane first appears as a flag-and-return function: identify the object, route it to the policy holder, preserve the legal certifier.

January 27–29 — OMB attempts a government-wide assistance gate

Mover. Acting OMB Director Matthew Vaeth and agency senior political appointees.

Institutional change. OMB M-25-13 ordered agencies to pause new awards, disbursements under open awards and related activity at 5 p.m. on January 28, subject to law. It also required program-by-program information returns, identification of legally mandated actions, political-appointee oversight and review of notices of funding opportunity.

Maturity. The directive affected operations before its stated clock. The March 6 judicial record found that states encountered payment-portal lockouts and delay/rejection messages on January 28, including access problems in the Payment Management System.

Reversal. A D.C. court administratively stayed the directive on January 28. M-25-14 rescinded it on January 29.

Counterevidence and limit. Rescission did not settle whether related executive-order pauses continued through other instructions. The Rhode Island court found that the directive's substantive effect continued and entered a temporary restraining order on January 31, then a preliminary injunction on March 6. The court did not bar every lawful, program-specific review; it barred categorical interference with appropriated and obligated funds beyond the agencies' actual authority.

Primary court record. Rhode Island preliminary-injunction opinion.

Plotline. This is the first clear boundary lesson of the wave: a central funding leash can be attempted quickly, but money retains the legal history of appropriation, obligation, award terms and program authority. The record cannot be flattened into “the President stopped all grants” or “the rescission restored everything immediately.”

1B.2 — February 3–21: access becomes a litigated institutional object

February 3–6 — the internal-access model is narrowed while it is being built

Mover. Treasury, Krause, Elez, Fiscal Service security and career staff, and plaintiffs in Alliance v. Bessent.

Institutional change. The Treasury access suit began February 3. Treasury's February 4 public letter described coded-data access as read-only and similar to an audit. On February 5, Elez was mistakenly granted read/write permissions to the Secure Payment System. Fiscal Service corrected the permission to read-only on the morning of February 6; Elez resigned that day. The court record says the initial forensic review found no use of the write permission and no unauthorized action during the supervised session.

Maturity. System access was real; the write permission was a configuration error, not a proved alteration of a payment or production record.

Counterevidence and limit. The completed forensic picture was not yet available at the February 24 hearing. Early government descriptions also required correction: Elez was a temporary Schedule C employee, not a special government employee, and his work could involve sending selected payment files to authorized State officials. The relevant question is therefore not “did DOGE own Treasury?” but “which Treasury employee had which role, dataset, permission, reporting chain and external-disclosure authority at each moment?”

February 6–21 — two courts create different operating restraints

Mover. The D.D.C. and S.D.N.Y. courts, Treasury and the plaintiff organizations/states.

Institutional change. A February 6 consent order in Alliance limited access while permitting specified read-only work. A separate states' case began February 7; a February 8 TRO broadly interrupted DOGE-linked access. On February 21, the S.D.N.Y. court replaced that emergency order with a narrower preliminary injunction requiring Treasury to document training, vetting, authority, mitigation and reporting-chain conditions before access.

Maturity. Judicially bounded operating access. Treasury could continue payment-integrity work but not treat “efficiency” as a substitute for privacy, security and administrative process.

Counterevidence and limit. On March 7, the D.D.C. court denied a separate preliminary injunction because the plaintiffs had not shown irreparable harm under that circuit's standard; the S.D.N.Y. restraint remained the operative cross-case limit. Later merits outcomes belong to later waves and must not be backdated.

Primary records. D.D.C. March 7 opinion, S.D.N.Y. TRO, and S.D.N.Y. preliminary-injunction opinion.

Plotline. The court becomes part of the control plane. It does not simply stop or bless the project; it specifies the conditions under which the observer may remain inside the money room.

1B.3 — February 19–March 19: the record moves in front of the payment

February 19–27 — a credit-card pilot precedes and then enters the EO architecture

Mover. DOGE, participating civilian agencies, agency heads and DOGE team leads.

Institutional change. The administration's own public work feed said on February 19 that it was already working with agencies to review unused or unnecessary federal credit cards. On February 27 it reported a 14-agency pilot and roughly 24,000 deactivated cards after eight days. EO 14222, signed February 26, then imposed a 30-day government-card freeze with agency-head exceptions and made centrally recorded travel and payment justifications part of the broader cost-efficiency architecture.

Maturity. Administration-reported bounded pilot plus a broader signed executive direction. The underlying agency card records were not recovered for this packet.

Counterevidence and limit. A self-reported deactivation total is not a cash-savings ledger, fraud finding or final cancellation of every account. The order exempted critical categories and allowed agency-head exceptions. Because the pilot began before the EO, the EO should be treated as formalization and expansion, not the sole cause of the operating program.

Claim surface and signed authority. The DOGE work feed is the administration's own operational claim surface, not independent proof of the reported total. EO 14222 is the primary signed authority.

February 26 — EO 14222 makes explanation a precondition of covered payment

Mover. Agency heads, approving employees, DOGE team leads, the USDS Administrator, GSA and OMB.

Institutional change. EO 14222 directed each agency to build a centralized technological system for payments under covered contracts, grants and related instruments. The system was to carry the approving employee's brief written justification, permit rapid agency-head review when justification was missing, and publish the explanation where lawful and practicable. The order also initiated contract/grant, contracting-officer, travel, card and real-property reviews on seven-, 30- and 60-day clocks.

Maturity at signature. Architecture and deadlines, not a universal completed system.

Counterevidence and limit. The order excluded direct assistance to individuals and several security, public-safety and emergency categories. It allowed written exemptions. The agency head retained the pause/review authority and the employee retained approval responsibility; the DOGE lead assisted and reported. Public posting remained subject to law and practicability.

Plotline. This is the crucial shift from payment as output to payment as an explained event. The record is supposed to exist before or alongside approval, not arrive only as an annual audit trace.

March 5–10 — bounded implementation reaches the public

Mover. Participating agencies, HHS's Program Support Center and the DOGE public-data surface.

Institutional change. The administration's work feed claimed roughly 146,000 cards deactivated across 16 agencies by March 5. On March 10 it claimed that the first 37,207 grant-payment records—three days of Payment Management System activity—had been posted publicly. The later live Defend the Spend surface makes a bounded public feed and API observable; its completeness, underlying population and reported scale are not independently established by that surface.

Maturity by March 19. A bounded public payment surface was observable. The reported counts and scale remain administration claims rather than independently substantiated operating totals. The March 10 language said justifications were coming “soon,” so the fully populated two-sided justification layer should not be assumed complete at the exact handoff. By April 13, in the following Aries wave, the administration said the HHS system was processing more than $700 million in daily payments with justifications. That later claim matures the administration's account of the seed but belongs to the next sky.

Counterevidence and limit. The feed is not the General Fund ledger, bank settlement record, universal federal payment population, blockchain or proof that a payment was lawful, efficient or nonfraudulent. Its first records stop at the federal payment edge; they do not reveal the full state, NGO, subaward, vendor, performance, correction and recovery chain.

Plotline. Public accounting begins as a centralized API and a bounded program feed. That function may later be implemented with conventional systems, cryptographic proofs or a shared ledger; this wave does not select among them.

What had changed by March 19

  • A real Treasury end-to-end payment-process engagement was underway.
  • Fiscal Service and the Treasury DOGE Team had queried, copied and partially automated review of specified foreign-aid payment files.
  • One identified payment was halted after the originating agency requested non-processing; four other reviewed files proceeded.
  • Courts had turned training, vetting, role, permission, privacy and reporting chain into operating conditions on access.
  • A multi-agency credit-card deactivation pilot was operating.
  • EO 14222 had converted payment justification, travel justification and payment-review capability into government-wide design requirements for covered spending.
  • A bounded HHS grant-payment feed was publicly visible by March 10.

What had not changed merely because these objects existed

  • Treasury had not inherited every program agency's legal payment decision.
  • The President or OMB could not treat appropriated and obligated money as an undifferentiated discretionary account.
  • Read/write permission was not proved use, alteration or fraudulent interference.
  • A risk flag, TAS match or file routed for review was not a legal fraud determination.
  • A deactivated card was not a measured cash recovery.
  • A payment line was not a complete transaction, performance or downstream-spending history.
  • A public API was not a blockchain, CBDC, tokenized Treasury account or government-wide immutable ledger.
  • The full EO 14222 architecture was not complete at the March 19 cutoff.

Astrological hearing

Governing hierarchy

The strict governor remains the fixed-rising 2024 Aries ingress; the 2024 Capricorn ingress is the nested winter room. The same geometry described in Wave 1A now appears through a different institutional organ:

  • Sun in Aries in the strict governor's 6th house, conjunct Neptune, places executive purpose inside administration and service while making clarity, image and boundary conditions central.
  • Chart-ruler Mars in Aquarius in the 4th naturally rhymes with intervention in the technical foundations of the state.
  • Jupiter and Venus form the root loop: questions of value, lawful allocation, legitimacy and relationship remain at the bottom of the hearing, even when the visible story is technical access.
  • Moon in the 10th opposite Pluto in Aquarius gives the public/political class a direct line to concentrated systems power and its visibility.
  • The nested Capricorn Sun in the 3rd emphasizes memoranda, file codes, written explanations and reporting; its Moon–Jupiter–Saturn tension holds public need, scale and institutional restraint in the same winter room.

The constructive pole is auditable stewardship: the government can finally connect payment, authority, purpose, reviewer and correction, allowing waste to be caught before release and the public to see more than annual aggregates. The shadow pole is financial telemetry as political leverage: a new observer gains sensitive cross-system access before authority, privacy and appeal rules are mature, and “fraud” becomes a rhetorical label for disfavored but lawful spending. The factual record contains evidence relevant to both poles and does not settle the final expression.

Handoffs kept open

  • March 25: EOs 14247 and 14249 add electronic payment rails, earlier payment-file submission, standardized descriptions, Do Not Pay screening and Treasury centralization. They are Wave 2 objects, not retroactive Wave 1 facts.
