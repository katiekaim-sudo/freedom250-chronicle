SEC Regulation Crypto Assets — Source Document

Evidence cutoff: 2026-08-23

Legal state: Commission-issued proposal; Public Inspection filed August 20; Federal Register publication August 21, 2026; comments due October 20, 2026

Identifiers: File S7-2026-27; Release Nos. 33-11434 / 34-106150; RIN 3235-AN38

Short answer

The SEC has proposed a federal offering regime for certain investment contracts involving crypto assets. It is not an operative crypto code and does not itself settle the whole market-structure fight. Its central move is to separate a non-security crypto asset from the investment contract through which it is offered, then create two issuance exemptions and a later safe harbor keyed to the completion or permanent cessation of the issuer's promised essential managerial efforts.

The proposal is best understood as an adapter constitution for capital formation. It governs who may raise money, what must be disclosed, how retail may participate, when state registration may be displaced, and how a sponsor asserts that the investment-contract relationship has ended. It does not define the chain's technical architecture, guarantee that the network is decentralized or functional, or approve any offering.

The three proposed routes

Route Proposed function Critical boundary
Rule 200 startup route A smaller, time-limited offering route tied to network development A four-year fundraising window is not proof that the investment contract has ended or the network is complete.
Subpart C fundraising route A larger public-offering route with prescribed disclosure and reporting It is public-offering infrastructure, not a general permission for every token sale.
Rule 400 safe harbor A filing-based route for asserting separation of the crypto asset from promised managerial efforts Filing is not SEC adjudication, factual verification, immunity from fraud law or proof of decentralization.

Seven design decisions

  1. Promises define the perimeter. The issuer's representations and promised essential managerial efforts help determine whether the asset remains tied to an investment contract.
  2. Distribution is part of construction. Token distribution can fund and create the network rather than follow a completed product.
  3. Startup is a use case, not a corporate species. The route is structured around the offering and network-development facts.
  4. The four-year clock and contract-end clock differ. Time may expire while managerial dependency remains.
  5. The larger route is public capital-formation infrastructure. Its scale, disclosure and reporting duties exceed a small private exemption.
  6. Rule 400 is a claim-and-filing mechanism. The proposed filing does not itself make the underlying facts true.
  7. State preemption depends on a current status outsiders may struggle to verify. A reliable public status and correction mechanism therefore matter.

Disclosure constitution

The proposal asks for information about the issuer or responsible group, the network and code, supply and distribution, holder rights, use of proceeds, material risks, governance, conflicts, insider holdings and sales, financial information, and the promised work still required. The source release contains 154 numbered questions, plus general, Paperwork Reduction Act, Congressional Review Act and small-entity requests.

The volume of questions matters. The Commission has supplied a proposed legal container while openly testing the evidence needed to make that container work—especially for informal groups, website-based disclosure, non-cash consideration, insider exits, network completion and state-law preemption.

Highest-value stress tests

  • Promise endogeneity: can a sponsor draft or withdraw promises to move the same economic project across the perimeter?
  • Information cliff: what continuing information remains after a smaller route ends?
  • Website evidence: how are amendments, removals, version history and authenticity preserved if disclosure lives on a website?
  • Informal responsibility: who signs, updates and answers for a project without a conventional issuer?
  • Network completion: what evidence distinguishes technical deployment from the end of essential managerial dependence?
  • Status oracle: how can a state, intermediary or buyer verify that a Rule 400 filing remains current and has not been superseded?
  • Missing intermediary stack: the proposal does not by itself resolve exchange, broker, custody, clearing, settlement, transfer-agent, bankruptcy or payment treatment.

What the proposal does not establish

  • a final rule, effective date or compliance date;
  • SEC approval of any issuer, token, network or offering;
  • that a filed safe-harbor claim is factually correct;
  • a complete SEC/CFTC market-structure allocation;
  • a custody, trading, clearing or settlement regime;
  • network functionality, decentralization, liquidity or price integrity;
  • preemption that survives stale or inaccurate status information; or
  • first operational EDGAR filing or investor reliance.

Exact return gates

  1. resolved August 21: Federal Register publication and October 20 comment deadline;
  2. opening and composition of the public comment file;
  3. rule/form text changes, especially termination, audit, current-report and state-preemption mechanics;
  4. final, withdrawn or reproposed disposition;
  5. EDGAR form availability and technical filing guidance;
  6. first claimed reliance, first challenge and first correction; and
  7. any later statute that changes the SEC/CFTC perimeter.
