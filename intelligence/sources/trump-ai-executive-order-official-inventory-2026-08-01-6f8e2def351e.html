Trump AI Executive Order Official Inventory

Bottom line

Through August 1, 2026 (ET), the official record supports a core inventory of 13 Trump executive orders that directly concern artificial intelligence or materially govern Federal AI development, use, acquisition, education, export, science deployment, cybersecurity, or data-center infrastructure:

  • 2 first-term AI orders: EO 13859 and EO 13960;
  • 11 second-term orders: EO 14148, 14177, 14179, 14277, 14318, 14319, 14320, 14355, 14363, 14365, and 14409.

This is an instrument census, not a keyword count. It therefore:

  1. counts an instrument as an executive order only when the numbered Federal Register/GovInfo record says it is one;
  2. keeps White House plans, presidential memoranda or determinations, OMB memoranda, budget guidance, fact sheets, and agency programs in separate rows;
  3. separates signature, Federal Register publication, deadline, implementation, funding, award, and operating clocks; and
  4. admits energy, cyber, quantum, or science orders only in a bounded adjacent ring when the text materially connects to AI.

The central architecture is not one omnibus AI order. It is a stack:

policy reset -> White House science advice -> Federal use/acquisition rules -> education/workforce -> compute and data-center buildout -> export packaging -> AI-enabled science -> State-law strategy -> frontier-model cybersecurity.

AI governance from policy reset to agency practice

Targeted reconciliation: September 7, 2026. The seven original Tech records ask how the AI leadership agenda becomes reviews, public input, purchasing conditions, security work and scientific collaboration. Their common policy-change lens remains visible, while each instrument answers a different question. A change in emphasis is not, by itself, evidence that every safeguard disappeared or that implementation succeeded.

This section joins those original questions to this inventory's existing order and implementation ledger. It corrects the revocation carrier, distinguishes selective cyber amendments from wholesale repeal, and follows the saved procurement-guidance and agent-consultation returns. The broader August 1 instrument census remains dated August 1. September 7 is a review of these seven records and identified source returns, not a new whole-government census.

The policy reset and its separate implementing documents

Research question: How the administration resets AI policy and turns a leadership objective into reviews, replacement guidance and a national plan.

EO 14179 was signed January 23, 2025 and published January 31. EO 14148, signed January 20, had already revoked EO 14110; this order directs review of actions taken under that revoked order, lawful revision or rescission of inconsistent actions, an Action Plan within 180 days and OMB memorandum revisions within sixty days. The saved official inventory records April 3 replacement OMB memoranda, and the White House released the Action Plan on July 23. Those are distinct returned documents, not proof every agency action changed or all safeguards disappeared. The original deregulation lens remains useful, with revocation, policy review, guidance, planning and operating results kept separate.

Clocks: EO 14179 signed January 23, 2025; filed January 30 at 11:15 a.m.; published January 31, 90 FR 8741–8742. EO 14148 signed January 20, filed January 27 at 8:45 a.m., published January 28. Nominal 60-/180-day dates from January 23 are March 24/July 22. Saved OMB replacements dated April 3; Action Plan public release July 23. Internal completion/submission instants remain unverified.

Primary evidence: EO 14179 policy and implementation direction; EO 14148 revocation carrier; July 23 public Action Plan release.

Evidence limits:

  • EO 14148 section 2(ggg) is the actual EO 14110 revocation carrier. EO 14179 does not automatically undo every action taken under EO 14110 or independent statutory authority.
  • The 180-day command is to develop and submit the plan. July 23 public release, one day after the nominal July 22 calendar derivation, does not establish when internal submission occurred.
  • The April 3 OMB memoranda are confirmed in the saved official inventory; this lane did not freshly audit their full texts or agency compliance. Claims about ideological bias are the administration's stated policy framing, not an independent finding.

Public input for the Action Plan

Research question: How a public consultation collects policy priorities for the later AI Action Plan, and what that input channel can establish.

This February 6, 2025 notice was issued by NSF's NITRD National Coordination Office on OSTP's behalf. It invited voluntary policy input through March 15 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern, covering infrastructure, competition, workforce and exports alongside assurance, cybersecurity, privacy, risks and safety standards. Responses were input to a plan, not adopted policy or contract offers. The White House's July 23 Action Plan is the later published output, organized around innovation, infrastructure and international diplomacy/security. The RFI alone does not prove which submissions determined that agenda, and publication of the plan does not establish delivery of its recommended actions.

Clocks: Notice submitted by NSF February 3, 2025; filed February 5 at 8:45 a.m.; published February 6, 90 FR 9088–9089. Comments due March 15 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern. White House Action Plan release July 23, 2025; no exact release time established.

Primary evidence: NSF/NITRD notice on behalf of OSTP; Published Action Plan return.

Evidence limits:

  • NSF/NITRD is the notice issuer and OSTP the requesting policy office; their roles should not collapse into one agency label.
  • The original March 15 deadline is preserved at its notice clock. This lane did not survey extensions, every submission, incorporation decisions or later agency delivery.
  • The RFI explicitly invites safety and security topics. It does not support a blanket claim that the new policy agenda eliminated all safety governance.

Selective changes to cybersecurity duties

Research question: How the administration reshapes AI cybersecurity work, software controls and cryptographic obligations while directing technical defensive capacity. Preserve the original build axis and policy-change question; attribute the censorship rationale to the White House fact sheet rather than declaring it proved by the predecessor text.

EO 14306 was signed June 6, 2025; June 11 is publication. It selectively amends the earlier cybersecurity order, retaining and rewriting software-security, cryptographic and AI-security duties while removing others. Its replacement AI section requires access to existing cyber-defense datasets and incorporation of AI vulnerabilities into incident processes. The prior AIxCC-dependent energy pilot, separate Defense AI-defense program and research-priority directions are absent from that replacement. The White House described this as a move away from censorship; that is the administration's stated rationale, not a finding established by the deleted technical provisions. Actual security implementation remains a separate question.

Clocks: EO 14306: signed June 6, 2025; filed June 10 at 11:15 a.m.; published June 11, 2025 (90 FR 24723–24726). Predecessor EO 14144: signed January 16, published January 17, 2025. Replacement AI duties carry November 1, 2025 deadlines. The NIST responsibility page was updated January 13, 2026, but its task list is not a completion ledger. Targeted primary review September 7, 2026 preserves saved owners' separate August cutoffs.

Primary evidence: EO 14306 amendment; EO 14144 predecessor text; White House rationale; NIST responsibility list.

Evidence limits:

  • The amendment is operative legal action, not merely a planned review, but it is not wholesale revocation of EO 14144 or EO 13694. Unamended text and other authorities require their own analysis.
  • Removing duties from this order does not prove that related research or a pilot ceased under every other authority. Conversely, elapsed deadlines do not prove datasets, vulnerability processes, standards or deployments were completed.
  • The order narrows two specified EO 13694 clauses from any person to any foreign person. That is not evidence of a particular designation, block or enforcement result.
  • The replacement PQC subsection retains the product-category list and TLS requirements but omits the predecessor's automatic category-list-to-solicitation and immediate PQC implementation clauses. Do not treat every January 2025 procurement requirement as retained.
  • The original offense/defense and pro-innovation lens is an interpretation. The operative replacement AI tasks concern cyber defense and vulnerability management; this record does not establish an offensive AI deployment.

Federal LLM purchasing conditions and issued guidance

Research question: How federal purchasing conditions shape the AI models and vendor information agencies will accept. Preserve the original procurement-side thesis and deregulate axis as historical framing, while separating policy principles, guidance, contractual remedies and measured operation.

EO 14319 was signed July 23, 2025; July 28 is publication. It establishes the administration's truth-seeking and ideological-neutrality principles for federal procurement of large language models, with disclosure options and scope limits. It does not impose one mandatory architecture on all private AI. OMB issued implementing memorandum M-26-04 on December 11, 2025, requiring terms in covered new solicitations or orders, practicable existing-contract changes and agency procedures by March 11, 2026. That is a documented guidance return. Whether a specific model complies, an agency changed its contract, or a remedy was used still requires separate evidence.

Clocks: EO 14319: signed July 23, 2025; filed July 25 at 8:45 a.m.; published July 28 (90 FR 35389–35391). Its 120-day guidance target was November 20, 2025. M-26-04 is dated December 11, 2025 and explicitly requires updated agency procedures by March 11, 2026; those are guidance and deadline clocks, not proof of completed procedures. Its two-year sunset runs from issuance unless OMB provides otherwise. September 7 review used the currently served official PDF; the original Tech record is preserved separately.

Primary evidence: EO 14319; Authenticated GovInfo text; OMB M-26-04, December 11.

Evidence limits:

  • The order's descriptions of DEI and ideological bias are the administration's policy claims, not this research's independent technical findings about a named model.
  • The neutrality principle permits ideological judgments when prompted by or otherwise readily accessible to the end user. The guidance allows documentation and disclosure paths, technical latitude and protection of sensitive model information; it is not a categorical ban on every ideological output.
  • M-26-04 excludes national security systems, with voluntary application encouraged. Its footnote does not require application to LLMs acquired under a free, open-source license, while recommending due diligence. Agency-built and non-LLM models have additional applicability factors rather than an automatic universal scope.
  • The memo requires minimum transparency requests and permits enhanced requests depending on use. Neither the requested documents nor the policy labels establish that a vendor/model has passed a specific evaluation.
  • The memo provides a two-year sunset unless OMB directs otherwise. This bounded review does not establish later extension, every agency's compliance, vendor-paid decommissioning or observed model behavior.

Specific regulatory barriers enter consultation

Research question: How does the AI Action Plan turn its regulatory-reform agenda into specific candidate changes? Preserve the original red-tape lens while retaining the notice’s own distinction between lawful modernization and complete deregulation.

OSTP opened a voluntary consultation on federal statutes, regulations, guidance, forms and administrative processes that unnecessarily hinder AI. Its six questions seek specific legal authorities, underused lawful flexibility, revisions, clarification and organizational remedies. The notice expressly says complete deregulation cannot deliver AI benefits and preserves public-interest safeguards and valid regulatory objectives. Its five barrier categories distinguish mismatch, structural incompatibility, uncertainty, direct hindrance and institutional factors. This is an intake mechanism for possible agency or legislative action; it does not repeal a rule, confer a waiver or fund a project.

Clocks: Notice dated September 24, 2025; filed September 25 at 8:45 a.m.; published September 26. Comments invited through October 27, 2025 at 11:59 p.m. ET under OSTP-TECH-2025-0067. September 7, 2026 review checks that original instrument, not later implementation across sectors.

Primary evidence: OSTP regulatory-reform RFI.

Evidence limits:

  • The original comment deadline is historical; this bounded review did not certify later docket disposition or implementation of individual suggestions.
  • The notice makes no binding commitment to pursue submitted ideas and accepts no applications for financial assistance or incentives.
  • An identified statutory barrier may require legislation; the RFI does not grant agencies new waiver authority.

Agent-security questions receive a stakeholder analysis

Research question: How does CAISI’s standards-and-innovation framing shape practical measurement of autonomous-agent security? Preserve the original reorientation thesis as a lens; the notice itself also invokes safety, robustness and public safety, so it does not establish abandonment of those objectives.

CAISI’s January notice asks how to measure and improve security of autonomous AI systems that can make persistent changes outside themselves. It separates adversarial attacks, deliberately inserted backdoors and harmful behavior of uncompromised models. Five question groups cover threats, controls, assessment, deployment environments and research/government support. General chatbot or non-autonomous retrieval security and offensive misuse are outside scope unless directly connected to agent-system security. The saved Scientific Trust source ledger already points to NIST AI 800-5, published May 18, 2026, which summarizes stakeholder responses: a completed consultation analysis, not a binding standard or demonstrated security assurance.

An existing consortium changes its research scope

Research question: What changed when federal AI collaboration was reframed around measurement, innovation and adoption? Preserve the original institutional-reorientation lens while distinguishing a renamed ongoing consortium, revised research priorities, participant selection and actual standards or technical outcomes.

NIST’s May notice retitles the existing AISIC, revises its research scope and reissues invitations to technically contributing organizations. Research began in 2024 and is described as ongoing; this is not the first launch of all consortium activity. New applicants submit letters of interest for selection and normally a CRADA, with a discretionary non-CRADA route for entities legally unable to enter one. Existing members need not reapply. The notice includes security, risk management, post-deployment monitoring and measurement goals as well as innovation. Separate national-security groups retain NIST-determined eligibility and research-security requirements. Invitation or membership does not establish adopted standards, funding or assurance of a member’s AI products.

Clocks: Original consortium formation notice November 2, 2023; collaborative work began in 2024 as stated in the May notice. New notice filed May 28, 2026 at 8:45 a.m. and published May 29. Letters accepted on an ongoing basis, reviews likely biannual, first review to begin within 60 days of publication (a review-start clock, not an application or admission deadline). Linked LOI page, updated August 4, says first review June 2026. Neither establishes actual completion of selections. September 7 is this bounded document review.

Primary evidence: NIST consortium notice; Linked letter-of-interest page; NIST announcement.

Evidence limits:

  • The notice says existing members may be asked to amend agreements; the accompanying agency news summary uses stronger must-sign wording. Preserve the notice’s formulation rather than flattening them.
  • Ongoing intake and a planned review period do not prove particular admissions, executed agreements or participation in restricted groups.
  • The linked LOI page identifies a June 2026 first review period but contains inconsistent displayed OMB control numbers and an August 31, 2026 expiration. This check does not certify present form approval or resolve that metadata discrepancy.
  • No new membership census, executed CRADA review, funded-award search or performance assessment was performed.

AI diffusion: nonenforcement and the unfinished rescission

Research question: Did the administration dismantle the Biden global AI diffusion framework to favor innovation and diplomatic access, and what changed in the actual export-control mechanism?

BIS's May 13, 2025 announcement directed nonenforcement of the AI Diffusion Rule and initiated its rescission; it did not itself finish the promised regulatory repeal. The linked PDF is dated May 12. The original rule was effective January 13, 2025, with most compliance deferred to May 15. GAO's May 12, 2026 decision distinguished the unfinished rescission from the definitive nonenforcement policy and found that policy subject to Congressional Review Act submission requirements.

Clocks: Original interim final rule: effective January 13, 2025; published January 15; most compliance May 15, with specified Data Center VEU provisions delayed to January 15, 2026. Linked announcement PDF: May 12, 2025; BIS webpage and original record: May 13. Commerce response to GAO: January 30, 2026; GAO cited April 17 CFR and accessed release April 28; decision May 12, 2026. Review of these exact objects: September 7, 2026.

Primary evidence: BIS announcement PDF, dated May 12; BIS May 13 web release; Original rule, DATES on page 1; GAO B-337935, May 12, 2026.

Evidence limits:

  • The original phrase 'killed the Biden diffusion regime' overstates completed legal rescission. Nonenforcement had practical consequences, but formal repeal and a replacement rule were separate promised actions.
  • GAO's decision reports Commerce's January 30, 2026 response and cites the CFR as of April 17; its May 12 decision is a dated legal finding, not a September 7 verification of later rulemaking or CRA compliance.
  • This review establishes neither an individual export license nor an actual shipment, and does not imply that other EAR controls ceased. The release's three separate chip guidance documents are outside this annotation's substantive coverage.

Entity List: restrictions depend on the exact actor and item

Research question: Did targeted national-security chip restrictions continue alongside the administration's export-liberalization measures, and which actors and foreign-product mechanisms actually changed?

BIS's final rule took effect September 12, 2025 and was published September 16. It describes 32 Entity List additions, including 23 under China, and 27 existing-entry corrections. Shanghai Fudan Microelectronics Co., Ltd. and Sino IC Technology Co., Ltd. specifically received footnote-4 treatment for foreign-produced items meeting the EAR's section 734.9(e)(2) conditions. This is evidence of targeted national-security restrictions alongside the separate liberalizing actions, not a uniform rule for all chip trade.

Clocks: Final rule effective September 12, 2025; filed September 12 at 4:15 pm; Federal Register publication September 16, 2025. Its savings clause covered qualifying shipments already en route on September 12 only if completed by October 14, 2025. Exact rule reviewed September 7, 2026; no later Entity List census.

Primary evidence: September Entity List final rule; Authenticated rule PDF.

Evidence limits:

  • Footnote 4 was not assigned to every addition, every Fudan affiliate, or all foreign-produced chips. The Entity List requires attention to the exact legal name, applicable EAR scope, entry-specific license requirement and review policy.
  • The stated count includes cross-destination entries: Shanghai Fudan Microelectronics (HK) appears under China, Singapore and Taiwan; Smart Mail Services under Iran and the UAE. Do not describe 32 as an independently deduplicated corporate-group count.
  • The rule also addresses biotechnology, aerospace, Russia/Iran procurement and other conduct. Its legal controls do not establish actual denied applications, disrupted shipments or measured industrial effects; subsequent list changes were not surveyed.

Advanced computing: a conditional licensing pathway

Research question: Did the administration concretely ease access to advanced U.S. AI chips for China and Macau, and which licensing conditions constrain that opening?

The January 15, 2026 final rule opened conditional case-by-case review for direct U.S. exports of qualifying advanced-computing commodities to end users in China or Macau. Its thresholds are TPP below 21,000 and total DRAM bandwidth below 6,500 GB/s; H200 and MI325X are examples, not the legal test. Applicants must supply the required certifications, including U.S. supply protection, an aggregate-TPP export ratio, end-user and remote-access safeguards, and shipment testing. This changes the licensing gate without granting a license or proving delivery.

Clocks: Filed January 13, 2026 at 4:15 pm; published and effective January 15, 2026. The rule's preamble identifies chips commercially available in the United States at publication; its certifications and testing apply at application and before export as specified. Exact instrument reviewed September 7, 2026; this is not a refreshed approval or shipment count.

Primary evidence: January advanced-computing final rule; Authenticated rule PDF.

Evidence limits:

  • The pathway does not generally extend to reexports or in-country transfers. Other applications retain the rule's presumption-of-denial treatment; all applicable controls and transaction-specific requirements still matter.
  • The codified 50% condition compares aggregate TPP exported to China or Macau with aggregate TPP shipped for U.S. end use for the same commodities, from first commercial U.S. shipment to application. It is not a 50% cap on dollar revenue or all national chip exports.
  • A qualified lab reviews every shipment before export on an approved license; the preamble permits representative batch sampling, not necessarily testing every chip. A rule, application, approval, test certificate and shipment are distinct evidence states. No licensing or shipment census was performed.

The original policy-change lens is useful precisely because these instruments can pull in different directions. An opening for one commodity and transaction can coexist with restrictions on a named entity. Keep the exact actor, item, destination, end use, instrument and evidence date attached to every conclusion.

Coverage: complete BIS announcement and GAO decision, complete September Entity List and January licensing-rule texts; the original Diffusion rule was checked only for its first-page legal clocks. No substantive review of the three separate chip-guidance documents or later approval/shipment census is claimed.

Scope, method, and confidence

Admission test

A numbered Trump order enters the core only if its operative text does at least one of the following:

  • establishes or resets national AI policy;
  • directs Federal AI research, development, deployment, governance, inventory, acquisition, or procurement;
  • creates an AI education, workforce, export, scientific-computing, health, or frontier-model program;
  • directly governs infrastructure expressly defined around AI training, inference, simulation, or synthetic-data generation; or
  • changes the operative legal baseline by expressly revoking an AI order.

An order enters the adjacent ring only if its operative text materially connects energy, cyber, quantum, workforce, or Federal scientific-integrity requirements to AI. Mere technological relevance is insufficient.

Official-source ladder

  1. Federal Register HTML record and GovInfo PDF for exact instrument text, number, signature date, publication date, citation, and document number.
  2. White House presidential-actions page for same-day signing/release context.
  3. White House, OMB, OSTP, Commerce, DOE, NIH, NSF, CISA, NIST, and other agency records for observed implementation.
  4. Official government domain census for bounded discovery negatives.

No press article, vendor release, think-tank summary, or secondary legal database is used as evidence here.

Clock rule

Unless an order expressly provides otherwise, its commands take effect when the President signs it. Later Federal Register publication authenticates and publishes the order; it does not silently move the signature-date deadline clock. Derived dates below are calendar-day calculations from signature and are labeled as such. Where text uses a relative relationship such as “within 90 days after guidance,” the second clock cannot start until the carrier appears.

Detailed command and deadline ledger

EO 13859 — American AI Initiative

Operative commands. EO 13859 established the American AI Initiative and directed:

  • NSTC’s Select Committee on AI to coordinate the initiative;
  • agencies to treat AI R&D as a budget priority and report plans annually;
  • agencies to improve access to Federal data and models while protecting security, privacy, and confidentiality;
  • OMB to solicit input on data/model access, identify barriers, and issue regulatory guidance;
  • NIST to prepare a Federal technical-standards engagement plan;
  • agencies to prioritize AI access to high-performance computing;
  • Education and NSF to develop AI-education and workforce recommendations; and
  • national-security agencies to prepare an action plan protecting the United States AI advantage.

Main clocks from signature. 90 days for the OMB data/model request and workforce recommendations; 120 days for data/source-code guidance and the national-security action plan; 180 days for agency data improvement plans, the cloud-computing report, OMB regulatory guidance, and the NIST standards plan; plus annual budget reporting.

Implementation/legal limit. OMB’s later AI-regulation memorandum and NIST’s AI standards work are implementation carriers, not amendments to the order. The National AI Initiative Act later supplied a statutory program layer; statutory codification should not be back-described as another executive order.

EO 13960 — trustworthy AI in Federal Government

Operative commands. For covered non-defense and non-intelligence agency uses, EO 13960 applied nine principles to the design, development, acquisition, and use of AI; directed OMB to prepare a policy roadmap; required public AI use-case inventories and remediation or retirement of inconsistent uses; and directed CIO Council/OPM workforce and rotational mechanisms.

Main clocks. 30 days for agency responsible officials; 45 days for CIO coordination and OPM inventory work; 60 days for use-case inventory criteria; 90 days for an AI Presidential Innovation Fellows track; 180 days for the OMB roadmap; annual inventories after the CIO criteria; and 120 days after an inventory for remediation or retirement.

Revocation clock and limit. EO 14110 expressly revoked EO 13960. EO 14148 later expressly revoked EO 14110. The 2025 revocation does not say EO 13960 is revived. Current Federal governance should therefore be sourced to current OMB memoranda and active orders, not to presumed common-law revival.

EO 14148 — AI legal-baseline rescission carrier

The AI-relevant operation is the express revocation of EO 14110. That action took effect on signature, January 20, 2025. EO 14148 did not replace EO 14110’s many agency instructions one-for-one. EO 14179 separately created the review, plan, and OMB-revision process.

EO 14177 — PCAST

The order establishes PCAST with no more than 24 members, places the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology and the Special Advisor for AI and Crypto among its presidentially selected leadership/membership structure, authorizes advice on science, technology, education, and innovation, and makes DOE the support agency. It revokes EO 14007 as amended by EO 14109. The order creates an advisory body; PCAST advice is not an agency rule or appropriation.

EO 14179 — policy reset and plan

Operative commands. The order states a policy of sustaining and enhancing United States AI dominance, requires a national AI Action Plan within 180 days, orders review of agency actions taken under revoked EO 14110, authorizes suspension, revision, or rescission of inconsistent actions, and requires OMB to revise M-24-10 and M-24-18 within 60 days.

Observed carriers. OMB issued M-25-21, Accelerating Federal Use of AI through Innovation, Governance, and Public Trust and M-25-22, Driving Efficient Acquisition of Artificial Intelligence in Government on April 3, 2025. The White House released Winning the Race: America’s AI Action Plan on July 23, 2025. Those are implementation documents, not executive orders.

EO 14277 — AI education

Operative commands and clocks. The order creates the White House AI Education Task Force, chaired through OSTP, and directs:

  • a Presidential AI Challenge plan within 90 days, with the challenge to occur within 12 months after the plan;
  • public-private K-12 AI education resources and a readiness review 180 days after the first partnerships;
  • Education guidance and research measures within 90 days;
  • Education, NSF, and USDA teacher-training/research action within 120 days;
  • Labor apprenticeship, WIOA, certification, and secondary-school credential measures within 120 days; and
  • agencies with education grants to treat AI education as a priority within 120 days.

Claim limit. A task force, challenge plan, partnership, grant priority, or training eligibility rule is not evidence that a particular school received money or that a national curriculum was imposed.

Observed challenge operation. The White House launched the nationwide Presidential AI Challenge on August 26, 2025. On June 9, 2026, it recognized six National Champion teams and reported participation by more than 20,000 students across all 50 States, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and 49 Department of War Education Activity schools in 10 countries. That establishes program launch, participation, judging, and national awards—not universal school participation, a mandate, or a grant to every participant.

EO 14318 — AI data-center infrastructure

Operative architecture. The order expressly defines data-center use around AI inference, training, simulation, or synthetic-data generation and covers supporting power, chips, networking, data storage, and cloud infrastructure. A “qualifying project” meets an order-defined threshold such as at least $500 million in committed capital expenditure, more than 100 MW of new load, national-security designation, or Secretarial designation.

Commands and clocks. Commerce is to establish a support initiative and agencies are to identify existing financial-support tools. Relevant agencies have 10 days to identify available categorical exclusions to CEQ; after a relevant agency identifies a project to FPISC, the FPISC Executive Director may designate and publish it as a transparency project within 30 days; EPA has 180 days for reuse-site guidance; and the Army has 180 days to review nationwide permits. Interior, Energy, and Defense receive site/authorization/lease directions.

Revocation and limit. The order revokes EO 14141. It accelerates and coordinates lawful pathways; it does not itself issue a site permit, interconnect a facility, secure water, finance construction, or commence operations.

EO 14319 — Federal LLM procurement

The order directs OMB guidance within 120 days on “truth-seeking” and “ideological neutrality” principles for agency procurement of large language models. Contracts entered after the guidance must include compliance terms and a vendor-paid decommissioning remedy after an opportunity to cure. Agencies are to revise existing contracts when practicable and establish implementation procedures within 90 days after guidance. National-security exceptions remain.

Dependency clock. The agency-procedure clock starts only when the required OMB guidance issues. The order text is not itself the procurement guidance or a finding that a vendor/model complies.

Observed guidance. OMB issued M-26-04, Increasing Public Trust in Artificial Intelligence Through Unbiased AI Principles on December 11, 2025. That starts the order’s 90-day agency-procedure clock; the memorandum explicitly requires updated procedures by March 11, 2026. The official PDF’s first footnote appears to print “EO 14139”; the authenticated Federal Register order is EO 14319, so the footnote is preserved as a source defect rather than propagated as a new instrument.

EO 14320 — American AI Exports Program

Commerce must establish and implement the American AI Exports Program within 90 days and issue a public call for full-stack proposals. Proposals are due 90 days after that call. Commerce, State, Defense, Energy, and OSTP are to select priority packages; the Economic Diplomacy Action Group may then mobilize Export-Import Bank, DFC, technical-assistance, and diplomatic tools.

Observed program clocks. Commerce/ITA announced program implementation on October 21, 2025, beginning with an RFI. It opened the formal call for proposals on April 1, 2026, with submissions through June 30. On July 6, 2026, ITA reported 78 applications and said each remained subject to review and interagency consultation before final designation. The Federal domain census also identifies aiexports.gov as a live Commerce/ITA surface. Application is not designation; designation is not an export-control authorization, financing commitment, disbursement, shipment, or foreign deployment.

EO 14355 — pediatric cancer AI

The MAHA Commission, HHS, APST, and Special Advisor for AI and Crypto are to use AI through the Childhood Cancer Data Initiative, prioritize existing Federal funds, seek private engagement, improve interoperability, and finalize patient- data standards capable of handling structured and unstructured information in a safe and privacy-compliant way.

The order contains no new appropriation and no fixed completion deadline. NIH Bio Genesis and the broader Genesis Mission may supply relevant infrastructure, but a broader AI-science portfolio is not automatically implementation of every pediatric-cancer command.

September 7 bounded source clarification: The order was signed September 30, 2025 and published October 7. HHS separately announced on September 30 that CCDI funding would rise from $50 million to $100 million. This announced budget response is distinct from a new appropriation in the order, award-level spending, deployed models or clinical outcomes. The order retains patient/parent control and privacy requirements. For the different question of regulatory evidence and manufactured-product acceptance, read the existing Wave 3 biotech map’s “Biomedical mission, regulatory evidence and manufacturing acceptance” section. This addition does not extend the thirteen-order census into a new government-wide survey.

EO 14363 — Genesis Mission

Operative architecture. DOE must establish the American Science and Security Platform integrating high-performance and cloud computing, AI agents and models, Federal data, and autonomous laboratories. APST/NSTC coordinate the cross-agency mission.

Clocks. From November 24, 2025: 60 days for at least 20 national science and technology challenges; 90 days for the compute/resource inventory; 120 days for data/model assets and an integration plan; 240 days for robotic-facility review; 270 days to seek initial operating capability; and one year for the first report, annually thereafter.

Observed July 22, 2026 implementation. Official White House and agency records describe a whole-of-government challenge portfolio and more than $5 billion in heterogeneous Federal commitments. The component clocks remain separate:

  • DOE selected 278 projects for award negotiations, not 278 completed awards or operating projects;
  • NIH described more than $1.2 billion as a mixture of obligated FY2026 and planned FY2027 Bio Genesis support;
  • NSF described $380 million for 20 teams over four years, plus up to $20 million in philanthropy;
  • DOE described more than $800 million in private consortium commitments mixing compute credits, models, cloud services, expertise, funding, and other value; and
  • Department of War amounts were described as planned contributions, which should not be converted into appropriations, obligations, or completed data integration.

These records show a live portfolio and resource mobilization. They do not yet prove every EO milestone, every award, or full platform initial operating capability.

EO 14365 — national AI policy framework

Commands and clocks. The order directs the Attorney General to create an AI Litigation Task Force within 30 days; Commerce to evaluate State AI laws within 90 days; NTIA to address certain BEAD conditions within 90 days; agencies to assess discretionary-grant conditions; FCC to consider a proceeding within 90 days after Commerce’s publication; FTC to issue an AI policy statement within 90 days; and APST/Special Advisor to recommend Federal legislation.

Legal limit. The President directs executive litigation, grant, regulatory, and legislative-recommendation strategies. The order does not by itself erase a State law or confer jurisdiction that a statute withholds. Each lawsuit, grant condition, agency action, and congressional enactment has its own legal clock.

Observed implementation. The Attorney General’s January 9, 2026 memorandum established the AI Litigation Task Force one day before the nominal 30-day date and named the Attorney General or designee as chair and Associate Attorney General as vice chair. The White House published national AI legislative recommendations in March 2026. DOJ then filed a complaint in intervention in United States v. Philip Weiser and Colorado on April 24, 2026 challenging Colorado SB 24-205. Those objects establish task-force creation, legislative recommendations, and one litigation action; they do not establish a judgment, congressional enactment, or blanket invalidation of State AI law.

EO 14409 — frontier-model innovation and cybersecurity

Thirty-day lane. CNSS/Department of War and CISA are to advance AI-enabled cyber defense for national-security and civilian systems; Treasury is to lead a cybersecurity clearinghouse with NSA, CISA, industry, and critical- infrastructure participants; OMB is to address grant funding; and OPM is to support technology-force cyber hiring.

Sixty-day lane. Treasury, NSA, CISA, the White House, OSTP, and NIST are to develop classified benchmarking and a threshold for “covered frontier models,” and establish a voluntary framework for up to 30 days of pre-release Federal access with developers and trusted partners. The order expressly disclaims mandatory model licensing or preclearance. DOJ is to prioritize applicable cybercrime statutes.

Cutoff status. The principal public deadline dates derive to July 2 and August 1, 2026. On July 14, the White House launched GOLD EAGLE, the EO-linked vulnerability-coordination clearinghouse built with Treasury, DHS/CISA, Department of War, industry, open-source, and critical-infrastructure partners. That establishes a named operational coordination object, not a complete audit of every 30-day command. No separately titled public voluntary pre-release frontier-model framework was isolated by cutoff. That remaining public-record negative is not proof of missed classified, internal, or nonpublic work; the benchmark lane is expressly classified.

Claim corrections this inventory supports

  1. “Trump signed three AI executive orders in July 2025” is true only for the July 23 trio. It is not the full two-term Trump AI-order inventory.
  2. A White House plan is not an executive order. The AI Action Plan is an EO 14179 implementation report; OMB memoranda are agency-management directives; neither should be assigned invented EO numbers.
  3. Revoking EO 14110 is not the same as reviving EO 13960. Revival requires text or an independently operative carrier, not assumption.
  4. EO 14318 does not “approve AI data centers.” It creates and accelerates support/permitting pathways; every site retains distinct money, land, grid, water, environmental, construction, and operating clocks.
  5. EO 14319 is bounded to Federal procurement of LLMs. It does not directly regulate all private AI expression or certify that any model is neutral.
  6. EO 14320 creates an export-program pipeline, not completed exports. A proposal, priority designation, finance eligibility, export license, shipment, and foreign operating deployment are different objects.
  7. Genesis commitments are heterogeneous. Selections for negotiation, appropriated funds, obligations, planned future funds, philanthropy, compute credits, expertise, datasets, integrations, and operating capability cannot be summed as if they are the same state.
  8. EO 14365 does not itself preempt State AI law. It directs executive strategies that remain subject to statutes, agency jurisdiction, procedure, litigation, and judicial adjudication.
  9. GOLD EAGLE is implementation evidence, not the whole of EO 14409. Its launch establishes the named clearinghouse, while classified benchmarks, voluntary frontier-model access, CISA directives, and workforce/grant carriers retain separate evidence clocks.
