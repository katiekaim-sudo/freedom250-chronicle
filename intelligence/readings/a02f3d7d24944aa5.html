Short answer
The SEC has proposed a federal offering regime for certain investment contracts
involving crypto assets. It is not an operative crypto code and does not itself
settle the whole market-structure fight. Its central move is to separate a
non-security crypto asset from the investment contract through which it is
offered, then create two issuance exemptions and a later safe harbor keyed to
the completion or permanent cessation of the issuer's promised essential
managerial efforts.
The proposal is best understood as an adapter constitution for capital
formation. It governs who may raise money, what must be disclosed, how retail
may participate, when state registration may be displaced, and how a sponsor
asserts that the investment-contract relationship has ended. It does not define
the chain's technical architecture, guarantee that the network is decentralized
or functional, or approve any offering.
September 25 Corporation Finance FAQ overlay
Corporation Finance issued nine staff answers applying the March Commission
interpretation to classification and investment-contract questions. The FAQs
do not amend this proposal, activate Rule 400 or Form TR, bind the Commission
or create new legal obligations. They do refine the factual framework this
proposal uses:
Commission-defined functionality and decentralization govern asset
classification, while the issuer's own promised thresholds govern whether it
fulfilled its representations;
another party's assumption of the promises does not create separation;
after a system is functional, securing, maintaining, improving or enhancing
it and facilitating network effects do not involve essential managerial
efforts under the cited Commission view;
marketing existing utility and indefinite non-profit-focused aspirations
likely are not, without more, promises of essential managerial efforts;
a post-functionality buyback announcement is not such a promise, while a
pre-functionality buyback promoted as yield or return may be;
a pure receipt requires continuing depositor ownership, unchanged rights, no
added economics and no issuer transfer, lending, pledge, rehypothecation,
other use or third-party exposure; and
secondary listing does not make a platform a promoter unless Securities Act
Rule 405 is satisfied.
The resulting lifecycle is:
asset classification
→ issuer promise inventory
→ investment-contract period, if formed
→ issuer-defined milestone state
→ successor-promise continuity
→ post-functionality conduct
→ possible separation
→ later transaction analysis
The authority ladder remains: March Commission interpretation; September
Corporation Finance staff application guidance; proposed Regulation Crypto
Assets. Staff guidance is useful but does not convert the proposal into law.

The three proposed routes
Route
Proposed function
Critical boundary
Rule 200 startup route
A smaller, time-limited offering route tied to network development
A four-year fundraising window is not proof that the investment contract has ended or the network is complete.
Subpart C fundraising route
A larger public-offering route with prescribed disclosure and reporting
It is public-offering infrastructure, not a general permission for every token sale.
Rule 400 safe harbor
A filing-based route for asserting separation of the crypto asset from promised managerial efforts
Filing is not SEC adjudication, factual verification, immunity from fraud law or proof of decentralization.
Seven design decisions
Promises define the perimeter. The issuer's representations and promised
essential managerial efforts help determine whether the asset remains tied
to an investment contract.
Distribution is part of construction. Token distribution can fund and
create the network rather than follow a completed product.
Startup is a use case, not a corporate species. The route is structured
around the offering and network-development facts.
The four-year clock and contract-end clock differ. Time may expire while
managerial dependency remains.
The larger route is public capital-formation infrastructure. Its scale,
disclosure and reporting duties exceed a small private exemption.
Rule 400 is a claim-and-filing mechanism. The proposed filing does not
itself make the underlying facts true.
State preemption depends on a current status outsiders may struggle to
verify. A reliable public status and correction mechanism therefore matter.
Disclosure constitution
The proposal asks for information about the issuer or responsible group, the
network and code, supply and distribution, holder rights, use of proceeds,
material risks, governance, conflicts, insider holdings and sales, financial
information, and the promised work still required. The source release contains
154 numbered questions, plus general, Paperwork Reduction Act, Congressional
Review Act and small-entity requests.
The volume of questions matters. The Commission has supplied a proposed legal
container while openly testing the evidence needed to make that container
work—especially for informal groups, website-based disclosure, non-cash
consideration, insider exits, network completion and state-law preemption.
Highest-value stress tests
Promise endogeneity: can a sponsor draft or withdraw promises to move the
same economic project across the perimeter?
Information cliff: what continuing information remains after a smaller
route ends?
Website evidence: how are amendments, removals, version history and
authenticity preserved if disclosure lives on a website?
Informal responsibility: who signs, updates and answers for a project
without a conventional issuer?
Network completion: what evidence distinguishes technical deployment from
the end of essential managerial dependence?
Status oracle: how can a state, intermediary or buyer verify that a Rule
400 filing remains current and has not been superseded?
Missing intermediary stack: the proposal does not by itself resolve
exchange, broker, custody, clearing, settlement, transfer-agent, bankruptcy or
payment treatment.
What the proposal does not establish
a final rule, effective date or compliance date;
SEC approval of any issuer, token, network or offering;
that a filed safe-harbor claim is factually correct;
a complete SEC/CFTC market-structure allocation;
a custody, trading, clearing or settlement regime;
network functionality, decentralization, liquidity or price integrity;
preemption that survives stale or inaccurate status information; or
first operational EDGAR filing or investor reliance.
Exact return gates
resolved August 21: Federal Register publication and October 20 comment deadline;
opening and composition of the public comment file;
rule/form text changes, especially termination, audit, current-report and
state-preemption mechanics;
final, withdrawn or reproposed disposition;
EDGAR form availability and technical filing guidance;
first claimed reliance, first challenge and first correction; and
any later statute that changes the SEC/CFTC perimeter.
