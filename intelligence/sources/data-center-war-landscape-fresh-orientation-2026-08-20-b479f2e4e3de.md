# Data-center “war” landscape — a fresh legal orientation

## Controlling reframe

The conflict is not best understood as a single pro-data-center coalition
fighting a single anti-data-center coalition. It is a contest over **which
government can exercise which gate, at what stage, and who must absorb the
resulting cost or risk**.

The most useful questions are therefore:

1. Who can allow or withhold land use?
2. Who can connect, delay, curtail or condition electric service?
3. Who pays for generation, transmission, distribution and stranded capacity?
4. Who sets air, water, wetlands, species and environmental-review conditions?
5. Who grants or withdraws tax advantages?
6. Who can sue, intervene, vacate, remand or preserve a project approval?
7. Which sovereign—federal, state, local or Tribal—owns the relevant decision?

This is a **representative orientation**, not a fifty-state census. It selects
operative objects that reveal the architecture of the field. Every example
retains its own legal and project state.

## The landscape in one matrix

| Power being contested | Federal | State / utility commission | Local / Tribal | Courts and enforcement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accelerate buildout | EO 14318; federal land; FAST-41; NWP 39 | State economic-development programs and utility-service pathways | Rezoning, special exceptions, development agreements, Tribal lease/energy authority | Expedited review proposals; limits on vacatur remain mostly proposed federally |
| Pause or stop | Direct federal moratorium bills remain proposed | New York EO 62 creates a bounded permit abeyance; Illinois paused new incentive applications | Moratoria, zoning denials, special-exception votes, Tribal resolutions | Rezonings or permits can be challenged; relief is project-specific |
| Allocate grid cost | FERC section 206 proceedings across six RTO/ISOs | Texas SB 6; Oregon HB 3546; Virginia GS-5; Georgia PSC tariffs/orders | Municipal/co-op contracts and local utility agreements | Commission orders and tariffs can be reviewed; no headline substitutes for the operative tariff |
| Control environmental effects | EPA/USACE/FWS/NEPA framework | State air, water, SEQRA and delegated permit programs | Local noise, water, stormwater, zoning and health powers | Citizen suits, government intervention, injunction requests and permit appeals |
| Subsidize or recapture value | Federal financing/tax proposals; no consolidated EO §3 program located | Sales/use-tax exemptions, credits, qualification agreements, application pauses or repeal proposals | PILOTs, abatements, community-benefit agreements, infrastructure commitments | Clawback and contract enforcement depend on exact instrument |
| Define the community bargain | Federal labor/community proposals remain mostly bills or guidance | State community-investment frameworks and wage/benefit conditions | Host-community agreements, public hearings, rezoning proffers; Tribal ownership/revenue architecture | Courts police authority and procedure, not the political slogan surrounding the project |

## Front 1 — federal acceleration is real, but it does not occupy every gate

EO 14318 supplies an operative federal acceleration policy. Its returned
objects include federal-land solicitations and selections, a publicly confirmed
Portsmouth DOE lease, FAST-41 coverage, EPA implementation resources and a
reissued Army nationwide-permit framework. The order cannot itself create a
state utility rate, local rezoning, state water right or completed project
permit.

This produces the first structural tension: **federal agencies can accelerate
their own procedures and property, while many decisive retail-power, water and
land-use decisions remain state or local.**

Primary federal spine: [EO 14318](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/07/28/2025-14212/accelerating-federal-permitting-of-data-center-infrastructure); Wave 1 return audit.

## Front 2 — the grid front is becoming the operative center of gravity

### FERC: regional tariff reform, not one final national code

On June 18, 2026, FERC opened six separate Federal Power Act section 206
proceedings covering every RTO/ISO under its jurisdiction. Each grid operator
and its transmission owners received 60 days to justify existing tariffs or
file reforms. The common issues include:

- cost-recovery agreements and protection against speculative load;
- large-load study and interconnection procedures;
- transmission-service and cost-shifting rules;
- co-located generation and behind-the-meter arrangements;
- flexible or interruptible service; and
- forecasting and public transparency.

These are **orders to justify or reform**, not final uniform tariff provisions.
FERC deliberately selected region-specific section 206 cases rather than a
single proposed final rule. The earlier RM26-4 ANOPR remains a pre-proposal
record that helped build the evidentiary base.

Primary routes: [FERC June 18 action](https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/ferc-launches-aggressive-targeted-action-speed-large-load-integration); [RM26-4](https://www.ferc.gov/rm26-4).

### Texas: reliability, financial commitment and curtailment

Texas **SB 6 became law and took effect June 20, 2025**. It directs PUCT to
create ERCOT large-load interconnection standards, using a 75 MW threshold
unless the Commission sets a lower one. The statute includes:

- recovery of utility interconnection costs from the large load;
- disclosure of duplicative service requests;
- on-site backup-generation disclosure;
- possible emergency deployment of backup generation or curtailment;
- an initial study fee of at least $100,000;
- site-control proof and uniform financial commitments;
- ERCOT study and PUCT review of specified co-location arrangements; and
- a demand-management service for qualifying large loads.

The statute preserves applicable emissions and environmental limits. It also
delegates significant implementation to PUCT and ERCOT, so enactment does not
mean every standard and protocol was complete on the signature date.

Primary routes: [Texas SB 6 enrolled text](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB00006F.HTM); [official history](https://capitol.texas.gov/billlookup/History.aspx?Bill=SB6&LegSess=89R).

### Oregon: a statutory “beneficiary pays” retail framework

Oregon **HB 3546 became Chapter 323** and took effect on passage. It directs
the Oregon PUC to establish a separate service classification for large energy
use facilities, allocate serving costs to that class, mitigate risks to other
retail customers and require qualifying electric-service contracts. The act
sunsets January 2, 2035.

This is enacted state law, but the operative retail terms still depend on PUC
tariff and contract implementation.

Primary route: [Oregon HB 3546](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2025r1/Measures/Overview/HB3546).

### Virginia: commission-made rate safeguards

The Virginia State Corporation Commission approved Dominion's **GS-5** class
for customers at or above 25 MW, effective January 1, 2027. The approved package
includes a fourteen-year take-and-pay obligation for specified new contracts,
minimum monthly payment requirements equal to 85 percent of contracted
transmission/distribution demand and 60 percent of generation demand, plus
credit/collateral protections and future cost-allocation work.

This is an operative commission order with a future effective date—not merely a
bill, company proposal or pledge.

Primary route: [Virginia SCC data-center initiatives](https://www.scc.virginia.gov/about-the-scc/scc-facts/).

### Georgia: tariffs, contract visibility and generation backstops

Georgia PSC records show a sequence rather than one “data-center law”:

- a January 2025 large-load rule allowing minimum billing and longer contracts
  for new customers above the applicable threshold;
- Commission access to new data-center contracts before execution;
- April 2025 large-load price-structure changes;
- a July 2025 base-rate freeze through 2028; and
- a December 2025 generation agreement that includes financial backstop and
  remediation mechanisms if forecast data-center contracts do not materialize.

The exact tariffs and orders control. The PSC's summary is a router, not a
substitute for those underlying instruments.

Primary route: [Georgia PSC March 2026 fact sheet](https://psc.ga.gov/site/downloads/datacenterfactsheet.pdf).

## Front 3 — New York is the clearest operative state pause

New York **Executive Order 62**, signed July 14, 2026, creates a real but
bounded moratorium while a Generic Environmental Impact Statement is prepared.

The order applies to defined data centers capable of consuming **50 MW or
more**. It directs DEC to hold in abeyance applications for discretionary state
permits, approvals or licenses for construction or expansion when the
application:

1. is or later becomes pending before DEC; and
2. had not been determined complete before July 14, 2026.

The order does **not** directly suspend local permits. It excludes facilities
primarily used for manufacturing, research—including specified quantum and
biomedical research—education or medical care. The pause lasts until DPS submits
the final GEIS report and findings statement, rather than an unconditional fixed
calendar year.

It also creates a much broader implementation program:

- DPS large-load proceeding Case 26-E-0045;
- GEIS public process under SEQRA;
- ESD Community Investment Framework within 60 days;
- consideration of a Grid Acceleration Fund and dedicated clean generation;
- Data Center Interconnection Working Group within 60 days;
- transmission-owner review and DPS report within 90 days; and
- DEC water-withdrawal assessment/report within twelve months.

The state's separately discussed repeal of sales-tax exemptions and possible
Grid Acceleration Fund remain proposals or delegated consideration until the
relevant legislation, regulation or commission order issues.

Primary authority: [New York Executive Order 62](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops).

## Front 4 — local land-use power can still decide whether the site exists

### Loudoun County: removing the by-right route

On March 18, 2025, Loudoun County adopted plan and zoning amendments that made
data centers conditional or Special Exception uses in areas where they had been
allowed by right. A separate grandfathering resolution protects qualifying
applications accepted before the stated cutoff, subject to its conditions.

This is a procedural-power shift: the project must obtain a discretionary public
approval rather than only demonstrate compliance with ministerial standards.
It is not a countywide moratorium.

Primary route: [Loudoun Data Center Standards and Locations](https://www.loudoun.gov/5990/Data-Center-Standards-Locations).

### Prince William Digital Gateway: plan approval and rezoning can separate

The Virginia Court of Appeals' July 29, 2026 order left the Digital Gateway
Comprehensive Plan amendment in place but rendered the challenged rezonings
void, returning the parcels to their prior zoning. Any new rezoning effort must
run through the ordinary application, staff-review and public-hearing process.

This is not a general judicial ban on data centers. It is a project-specific
example of how a plan-level policy can survive while the parcel-level legal
authorization fails.

Primary route: [Prince William County project and court-status page](https://www.pwcva.gov/department/planning-office/pw-digital-gateway).

## Front 5 — environmental law is both a permit system and a litigation system

Wave 1 already establishes that NWP 39's express data-center coverage does not
replace individual CWA permitting for larger impacts. Project Skye's individual
permit notice is the clean example: an application and comment period are not an
issued permit.

The litigation front adds another clock. In the Southaven, Mississippi xAI/MZX
Tech case, private plaintiffs allege Clean Air Act permitting violations. On
June 15, 2026, DOJ moved to intervene and dismiss. That filing establishes the
federal government's litigation position; it does **not** establish that the
court granted dismissal or that the facility is exempt from the Clean Air Act.

Primary route: [DOJ intervention and dismissal filing announcement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-files-intervene-and-dismiss-lawsuit-would-hamper-americas-ai-innovation).

The environmental front therefore contains at least four distinct objects:

`permit application -> agency permit decision -> administrative/judicial challenge -> final disposition`

No earlier object inherits the later result.

## Front 6 — incentives are regulatory gates, not background economics

The buildout landscape is shaped not only by prohibitions and permits, but by
conditional tax law:

- Virginia's sales-tax exemption currently requires qualifying Virginia
  location, investment, job/wage and VEDP MOU conditions.
- Georgia's high-technology data-center equipment exemption requires statutory
  investment and job evidence and a state application/certificate process.
- Illinois' statutory program offers state/local tax exemptions and an
  underserved-area construction-wage credit, but DCEO states that, under a June
  5, 2026 gubernatorial directive, it stopped processing new applications on
  July 1, 2026.

The Illinois action is an **incentive-application pause**, not a construction or
environmental-permit moratorium. New York's announced effort to repeal sales-tax
exemptions is not operative until legislation is enacted.

Primary routes: [Virginia Tax](https://www.tax.virginia.gov/sales-tax-exemptions); [Georgia Department of Revenue](https://dor.georgia.gov/high-technology-data-center-equipment-exemption); [Illinois DCEO](https://dceo.illinois.gov/expandrelocate/incentives/datacenters.html).

## Front 7 — Tribal sovereignty is a separate authority system, not a loophole label

The existing Sovereign Compute package shows why “move it to Tribal land” is
legally unusable shorthand. The controlling objects may include:

- trust, restricted-fee, fee-simple or allotted parcel status;
- BIA-approved HEARTH leasing regulations or an individual Part 162 approval;
- a TERA, TEDO or Section 17 corporation;
- Tribal council authorization, citizen procedure and any waiver terms;
- federal rights-of-way, environmental review and cultural consultation;
- utility/interconnection agreements and state/federal environmental permits;
  and
- lease, power, tax, ownership and revenue-sharing instruments.

Some Tribes operate compute or digital infrastructure; some are studying or
marketing projects; some oppose adjacent development; and some reported
moratoria or proposals still lack the primary instrument. Those states cannot
be collapsed into “Tribes support” or “Tribes oppose” data centers.

Controlling workbench route: Tribal Data Center Field Registry.

## What the “war” is actually about

Across the selected objects, the repeated disputes are:

| Dispute | Legal expression |
|---|---|
| Speed versus deliberation | FAST-41 and federal land on one side; GEIS, special exceptions and public hearings on the other |
| Who bears speculative-load risk | Study fees, site control, security, minimum bills, take-or-pay terms, collateral, cost-recovery agreements and insurance pools |
| Whether generation stays public | Co-location studies, behind-the-meter rules, islanded-generation interpretation, emergency curtailment and dedicated-power proposals |
| Who controls the parcel | Federal lease/ROW, state environmental approval, local zoning, Tribal land/lease authority and judicial review |
| Who controls scarce water and air capacity | State withdrawal rules, CWA/CAA permits, SEQRA/NEPA, local health/noise controls and citizen suits |
| Who captures public value | Tax exemptions, clawbacks, community funds, local proffers, prevailing-wage/PLA terms and Tribal revenue architecture |
| Who can say “not yet” | A legislature, governor, commission, locality, Tribal government, permit agency or court—but only within its authority |

## A better research architecture from here

The broad landscape should proceed in four linked but independent books:

1. **Grid and rate book** — FERC's six proceedings, each RTO/ISO tariff, state
   PUC large-load classes and named service agreements.
2. **State legal book** — enacted statutes, executive orders, GEIS/rulemaking,
   water/air regimes and tax-incentive changes, state by state.
3. **Local and judicial book** — zoning text, moratoria, rezoning/CUP/SPEX,
   court cases and project-specific permit appeals.
4. **Sovereign and federal-land book** — federal realty instruments, Tribal land
   status, lease authority, power/revenue architecture and consultation.

They should join only through a named project or a named legal object. A national
headline should never overwrite a state tariff, a local parcel or a Tribal
authority record.
