Data centers where tribal, state and federal power meet

Cherokee Nation's full task-force report now supplies a sovereign policy model: use land status, consultation, power, water and enforceable community benefits to govern each project, while favoring workforce and supplier gains over tax-arbitrage or direct hyperscale investment.

What we found

  • Cherokee Nation published a 37-page report based partly on 1,593 citizen survey responses. It identifies known projects but says fragmented permits, NDAs and missing registration rules prevent a complete census.
  • The report says direct hyperscale investment and tax-arbitrage are not strong fits for the Nation's current posture; workforce training, supplier work and enforceable community benefits offer lower-risk gains.
  • Cherokee authority depends on land status: it can govern tribally owned and trust land directly, while using consultation, advocacy and intergovernmental influence for projects on non-Tribal land within the Reservation.
  • Pocatello's October 1 agenda prepared a decision upholding denial of the former Hoku-site permit. The final adopted decision, its treatment of Shoshone-Bannock concerns and judicial review remain separate.
  • DOE estimates Tribal-energy selections around November 20, 2026; the August nuclear webinar still names no Tribal data-center/microreactor project.

Why it matters

The Cherokee report shows Tribal sovereignty operating as a practical project-governance layer: land authority, consultation, resource stewardship, workforce capture and community terms can shape development even when the Nation does not own the hyperscale facility.

What to watch

  • 2026-11-20 · DOE's estimated selection date for the $50 million Tribal-energy funding round; look for a named data-center-serving power, transmission or site project.
  • pending · Pocatello's adopted written denial/reconsideration decision and any judicial-review filing, including treatment of Shoshone-Bannock treaty or consultation concerns.
