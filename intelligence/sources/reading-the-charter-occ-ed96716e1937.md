# Reading the Charter — OCC

The OCC is the referee Congress built for its own bank-chartering scheme, then handed a leash instead of independence, then in 2010 half-cut the leash and gave it a wallet the President can't touch. It's not a bank, it's not the Treasury, it's a "bureau... in the Department of the Treasury" (§1(a)) that examines and licenses national banks — and since 2010 it pays for itself entirely out of the pockets of the banks it polices. Read below section by section.

## 1. Immunities & liability shields

Nothing in this chapter grants the OCC a "sue or be sued" clause, sovereign-immunity language, or asset-protection shield of the kind Freddie Mac or the IMF got. The OCC's power runs the other direction — it does the suing/enforcing (bank examinations, enforcement actions) rather than needing shielding from suits itself. §1(b)(1) does something notable in the opposite direction: it bars the Secretary of the Treasury from intervening in "any matter or proceeding before the Comptroller of the Currency (including agency enforcement actions)" — an immunity the OCC has from its OWN nominal boss, not from outside litigants.

## 2. Tax exemptions

None appear in chapter 1. The OCC is a regulator, not an issuer of securities or a holder of taxable assets on the scale of a GSE — this checklist item is essentially a null result here, which is itself informative: OCC's power isn't property-based, it's rule-making and licensing power.

## 3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause — the control map

- **Appointment:** President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate (§2). Five-year term.
- **Removal — the load-bearing clause:** §2, "unless sooner removed by the President, upon reasons to be communicated by him to the Senate." This is NOTICE, not consent — the President can remove the Comptroller at will, so long as he tells the Senate why. Compare the 1863 original (National Currency Act, §1), which required "the advice and consent of the Senate" for removal too, not just appointment — a stronger check that survived exactly one year before the 1864 re-enactment (National Bank Act §1) dropped it to the current notice-only standard. That's a real weakening of independence, done quietly, one year into the office's existence.
- **The Dodd-Frank independence sentence — the one everyone quotes and nobody reads closely:** §1(b)(1): "The Comptroller of the Currency shall perform the duties of the Comptroller of the Currency under the general direction of the Secretary of the Treasury. **The Secretary of the Treasury may not delay or prevent the issuance of any rule or the promulgation of any regulation by the Comptroller of the Currency, and may not intervene in any matter or proceeding before the Comptroller of the Currency (including agency enforcement actions), unless otherwise specifically provided by law.**" Read the two sentences together and the contradiction is right there in black and white: the Comptroller acts "under the general direction of" the Secretary — Treasury boss language — and yet the Secretary is statutorily barred from touching rules, regulations, or enforcement actions. "General direction" with no power over rules or enforcement is direction over almost nothing that matters day-to-day. This is functional independence wearing a nominal-subordinate's uniform.
- **The pre-2010 version was blunter and older:** before Dodd-Frank rewrote §1 in 2010, the statute just said the Comptroller "shall perform his duties under the general directions of the Secretary of the Treasury" with a single added sentence barring delay of rules — no bar on intervening in enforcement actions. The 2010 rewrite is the moment the "don't touch enforcement" language got added — a direct legislative response to pre-2008 complaints that Treasury/political pressure could and did influence bank exams.
- **Deputy Comptrollers:** appointed by the Secretary of the Treasury (not the President), up to four, one designated First Deputy, in a statutory succession order (§4). One Deputy Comptroller (§4b, added 2010) must specifically handle federal savings-association supervision — a structural fossil of the 2010 OTS merger.
- **Conflict-of-interest bar:** §11 — Comptroller/Deputy Comptroller may not "hold an interest in any national bank or any Federal savings association," directly or indirectly. Simple, absolute, no materiality threshold.

## 4. Money — self-funding vs. appropriations

**§16 is the single most important clause in this charter.** Added by Dodd-Frank in 2010: the Comptroller "may collect an assessment, fee, or other charge" from every entity it supervises, sized however the Comptroller determines appropriate (asset size, condition, "any other factor... as the Comptroller... determines is appropriate" — that's a blank check on the pricing formula). Then the kill clause: **"Such funds shall not be construed to be Government funds or appropriated monies, and shall not be subject to apportionment for purposes of chapter 15 of title 31 or any other provision of law."** Translate: Congress's power of the purse — appropriations, apportionment, the whole budget-control mechanism — does not reach the OCC. It taxes the banks it regulates, keeps the money, and Congress has zero appropriations leverage over it. The OCC has been fully industry-funded and off the congressional-appropriations grid since 2010; before that, bank examination fees existed but the express "not Government funds" disclaimer and apportionment exemption are Dodd-Frank additions. Note also the second paragraph of §16: the Comptroller has "sole authority to determine the manner in which the obligations of the Office... shall be incurred and its disbursements and expenses allowed and paid" — total internal control over its own spending, checked only by Title 5 civil-service pay rules.

## 5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs

No GAO-audit or FOIA-exemption language appears anywhere in chapter 1 — a real contrast with, e.g., Freddie Mac's billed-GAO-audit clause. The only disclosure hook in this chapter is affirmative and recent: **§14a (2022)**, requiring OCC to adopt data standards for information "regularly filed with or submitted to" it and to publish public data assets in open, machine-readable, API-accessible form. But §14a's own rule-of-construction note (Pub. L. 117-263 §5843) caps the reach: nothing in it requires OCC to collect or publish *new* categories of information beyond what it already gathered before Dec. 23, 2022 — the open-data mandate is a format requirement, not a new-transparency requirement. **§14** requires only a bare annual report to Congress, and the 1946 amendment specifically stripped out any statutory dictation of what that report must contain — Congress used to require detailed exhibits; since 1946 it doesn't. The report requirement has gotten thinner over time, not thicker.

## 6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses

No standalone severability or dissolution clause exists in chapter 1 itself — the OCC's existence and structure sit entirely at ordinary statutory amendment, the same as any bureau. There is no special supermajority, no entrenchment. Whatever Congress can do by simple legislation, it can undo the same way. This is a much weaker self-protection posture than a chartered GSE with a "dissolved only by Act of Congress" perpetuity clause — though in practice, a bureau nobody has any real appetite to abolish enjoys de facto permanence anyway.

## 7. Emergency powers

None appear in chapter 1 as a discrete "unusual and exigent circumstances" trigger (contrast the Federal Reserve Act §13(3) emergency-lending clause, or Treasury's Exchange Stabilization Fund). OCC's power is the everyday supervisory/enforcement kind, not an emergency-activated switch — its emergency-adjacent authority (e.g., over troubled-bank interventions) lives in the separate bank-supervision statutes referenced by §9/§10, not in this chapter.

## 8. Definitions section — scope traps

Chapter 1 has no dedicated definitions section (unlike, e.g., the FFB Act's §2282). The one quietly load-bearing cross-reference is in **§16**: the assessable universe is "any entity described in section 1813(q)(1) of this title" — a pointer into the FDIC Act's definition of "appropriate Federal banking agency" jurisdiction, which (post-2010) folds in not just national banks but also federal savings associations, absorbed from the abolished Office of Thrift Supervision. That single cross-reference is how OCC's fee base quietly doubled in scope in 2010 without this chapter's own text growing much at all.

## 9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution

Chapter 1 contains no explicit perpetuity or dissolution clause — the Office simply exists as a bureau "established in the Department of the Treasury" (§1(a)) for as long as the authorizing statute stands. There is no charter to expire and no shareholder-style wind-up mechanism; ending the OCC would require Congress to repeal the chapter outright, same as abolishing any other agency.

## 10. Anything strange

- **The genealogy hiding in the section numbers.** Nearly every operative section of this modern bureau — §2 (appointment/term), §3 (oath), §4 (deputy), §8 (clerks), §11 (conflict bar), §12 (seal), §13 (rooms/vaults) — traces its "R.S. §" citation straight back to Section 1, 2, or 3 of the National Bank Act of June 3, 1864. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, as an institution, is 162 years old this year and has never been re-chartered from scratch — only amended, in place, continuously, since the Civil War. The building blocks (oath within fifteen days, a seal "judicially noticed," fireproof vaults for engraved plates) are Civil-War-era plumbing still technically on the books, word for word.
- **The 1863→1864 removal downgrade, one year apart.** The National Currency Act of 1863 required Senate advice-and-consent to REMOVE the Comptroller — a genuinely strong tenure protection. The very next year's re-enactment, the National Bank Act of 1864, quietly dropped that to presidential-removal-with-Senate-notice-only. Congress gave this office a stronger independence protection and then took it back within twelve months, before the office had even finished its first term-in-office. Nobody talks about this because the 1864 Act is the one that "stuck" and is treated as the origin story; the 1863 version's stronger clause is a documented, forgotten first draft.
- **Dodd-Frank's split personality on subordination.** §1(b)(1) is the strangest single sentence in the file: it keeps "general direction of the Secretary of the Treasury" as the governing phrase and, in the same breath, statutorily forbids the Secretary from touching the two things that actually constitute directing a regulator (rulemaking, enforcement). Congress didn't remove Treasury's nominal authority over OCC in 2010 — it hollowed it out while leaving the label on. That's the OCC's whole "independent bureau inside a cabinet department" trick in one sentence.
- **The stablecoin trigger clause (2025).** §1(b)(3), added by Pub. L. 119-27, doesn't take effect on a fixed calendar date — it activates on "the earlier of the date that is 18 months after July 18, 2025, or the date that is 120 days after" final stablecoin regulations are issued. A federal statute whose effective date is defined relative to when regulators finish their own homework is an unusual drafting choice — it makes "when does this law start" itself a function of administrative behavior, not a fixed point Congress controls.

## Layer map — how we got here

1. **1863 — National Currency Act (ch. LVIII, 12 Stat. 665).** First creation of the currency bureau and the Comptroller's office, inside Treasury. Comptroller appointed by President with Senate advice-and-consent for BOTH appointment and removal — the strongest independence protection this office ever had.
2. **1864 — National Bank Act (ch. 106, 13 Stat. 99).** Re-enacts the office in nearly identical language, but weakens removal to presidential-removal-with-Senate-notice (the standard that survives, unchanged, to today). This 1864 text — not the 1863 text — is the direct textual ancestor of current §§1-4, 8, 11-13.
3. **1913 — Federal Reserve Act §10.** Subordinates the Comptroller's Federal Reserve note duties to the general supervision of the newly created Federal Reserve Board — first time another federal body, not just the Treasury Secretary, gets a supervisory hook into the office.
4. **1935 — Banking Act of 1935 §§203(a), 209.** Strips "on the recommendation of the Secretary of the Treasury" from the appointment clause (leaving pure presidential nomination) and raises the salary — a small independence gain on appointment, paired with the removal weakness already locked in since 1864.
5. **1959, 1966, 1972 — administrative modernization.** Multiple Deputy Comptrollers instead of one (1959), minor technical amendments (1966), and the full removal of bond/surety requirements for the Comptroller and Deputy Comptrollers (1972) — the office stops being personally bonded against misconduct, relying instead on ordinary federal civil/criminal liability.
6. **1980 — delegation power (§4a).** Comptroller can delegate any statutory power to staff — administrative flexibility, not a control-map change.
7. **2010 — Dodd-Frank (Pub. L. 111-203).** The big one. Rewrites §1 to (a) formally establish OCC as "a bureau" with an explicit safety/soundness/fair-access/fair-treatment mission statement, (b) absorb the Office of Thrift Supervision's federal-savings-association jurisdiction (new §4b, amended §11), and (c) most importantly, add the assessment/self-funding regime at §16 that takes OCC fully off congressional appropriations, plus the enforcement-independence sentence in §1(b)(1). This is the year OCC stopped being a bureau Congress could squeeze by the purse strings.
8. **2022 — data standards/open-data (§14a)**, and **2025 — stablecoin-issuer regulatory authority (§1(b)(3))**, added by Pub. L. 119-27 — the newest layer, extending OCC's jurisdiction into a genuinely new asset class (payment stablecoins) on a delayed, rulemaking-triggered effective date.

## Entity Theory read

This is the referee that regulates the game Congress itself set up in 1864 and never rebuilt from scratch — same bones, patched continuously for 162 years, wearing a "Treasury bureau" name tag that stopped meaning much the moment Dodd-Frank barred the Secretary from touching its rules or its enforcement actions. It survived the 1863-to-1864 flip that weakened its own removal protection, survived absorbing an entire dead sister agency (OTS) in 2010, and in that same year cut itself loose from congressional appropriations entirely by taxing the very banks it polices — money that by statute is declared not to be government money at all. Change of control at the top (a new President, a new Comptroller) doesn't touch this creature's plumbing; the only thing that could actually kill or reshape it is Congress rewriting the chapter, and Congress hasn't done that in any fundamental way since the Civil War.
