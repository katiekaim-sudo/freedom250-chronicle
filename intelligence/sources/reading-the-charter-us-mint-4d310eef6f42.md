# Reading the Charter — US Mint

## The 10-point hunt

**1. Immunities & liability shields.** None in 1792. Current 31 U.S.C. §5111(c)(3)(A) is the closest thing: the Secretary's "domestic control of coinage" procurement determinations are declared "not... reviewable in any administrative proceeding or court of the United States" — a narrow but real judicial-review shield, carved out specifically for national-security-flavored coin-production sourcing decisions.

**2. Tax exemptions.** None appear in either instrument — the Mint isn't a tax-exemption vehicle, it's a manufacturing operation.

**3. Who appoints, who removes, and for what cause.** The 1792 Act creates five offices (Director, Assayer, Chief Coiner, Engraver, Treasurer) but is silent on appointment mechanics beyond §4's oath requirement and §5's surety-bond requirement ($10,000 bond for the assayer, chief coiner, and treasurer) — appointment ran through the ordinary Appointments Clause with the Director answering to the President via the Secretary of the Treasury. Current law doesn't even name a "Director of the Mint" position in the sections captured here — by the 1980s recodification, the Mint had been folded fully into Treasury's chain of command with the Secretary directly exercising minting authority (§5111(a): "The Secretary of the Treasury... shall mint"). The Mint has zero independence from Treasury today — a sharp contrast to the OCC, which sits in the same building but keeps its own removal-protected chief.

**4. Money: self-funding vs. appropriations.** The 1792 Act pays officer salaries from ordinary appropriations (§8: "shall be defrayed from the Treasury of the United States, out of any monies which from time to time shall be therein, not otherwise appropriated") but *also* invents seigniorage on day one: §14's "deduction of one half per cent... shall constitute a fund towards defraying the expenses of the said mint" — the Mint was charging a fee on bullion brought in for coining and keeping the difference to run itself, 233 years before "self-funding agency" became a term of art. Current §5111(b) formalizes this completely: the "coinage profit fund" captures "the amount by which the nominal value of the coins minted from the metal exceeds the cost of the metal" — i.e., seigniorage — with only the *excess* swept to Treasury as miscellaneous receipts. The Mint today runs substantially off its own seigniorage, not congressional appropriations.

**5. Audit & disclosure carve-outs.** 1792 §7 requires quarterly Treasury settlement and an annual report to Congress, certified by the Comptroller — actually more disclosure discipline than the Mint has been subject to at any later point in this capture; nothing in current §5111/§5112 requires public Mint financial reporting beyond ordinary agency practice.

**6. Amendment & withdrawal clauses.** No entrenchment; ordinary legislative amendment, as the decades of §5112 rewrites for commemorative coin programs make obvious.

**7. Emergency powers.** §5111(d) lets the Secretary "prohibit or limit the exportation, melting, or treatment of United States coins" whenever "necessary to protect the coinage of the United States" — an executive-discretion emergency lever with real teeth: knowing violation carries a $10,000 fine and up to 5 years imprisonment, and violating coins/metal are forfeited to the government.

**8. Definitions section quietly load-bearing.** None explicit, but §5112(a)'s "may mint and issue **only** the following coins" is doing definitional work by exclusion — it is the boundary fence around what counts as lawful U.S. coinage at all, and subsection (k) then blows a hole straight through that fence (see #10).

**9. Succession/perpetuity & dissolution.** No dissolution clause in either instrument — the Mint, like Treasury, doesn't "wind up," it only gets reorganized.

**10. Anything strange.** Two things, one from each era. From 1792: §19's felony-death-penalty clause for any Mint officer who debases the coinage or embezzles metal or coin "for the purpose of profit or gain" — the Founders treated currency debasement as capital treason against the monetary system itself, a punishment harsher than most violent crimes then on the books. From the current code: §5112(k), the platinum-coin clause — "The Secretary may mint and issue platinum bullion coins... in accordance with such specifications, designs, varieties, **quantities, denominations**, and inscriptions as the Secretary, in the Secretary's discretion, may prescribe from time to time." Unlike every gold/silver/copper coin in §5112(a), which is capped by law at specific enumerated denominations, platinum coins have **no denomination ceiling written into the statute at all** — this is the literal statutory basis for the "trillion-dollar coin" debt-ceiling workaround: nothing in the text stops the Secretary from minting a single platinum coin and declaring it worth whatever face value he chooses.

## Layer map

- **1792 — Coinage Act (1 Stat. 246):** creates the Mint as a five-officer shop with its own seigniorage fund and a death penalty for debasement/embezzlement.
- **1873 — Coinage Act of 1873 ("Crime of '73"):** demonetizes silver dollar coinage, not captured verbatim here but the pivot point for the entire late-19th-century Free Silver political fight.
- **1965 — Coinage Act of 1965:** eliminates silver from dimes/quarters, converts half-dollar/dollar coinage to clad composition — the ancestor of current §5112(b)'s clad-coin specifications.
- **1982 — Title 31 recodification (Pub. L. 97-258):** restates the Mint's authority as current §§5111-5112, folding Mint operations fully under the Secretary with no separate Director office named in the operative text.
- **1985-2020 — decades of §5112 amendments:** American Eagle gold/silver bullion coins (1985), American Buffalo/palladium coins, the platinum-coin clause (added by Pub. L. 104-208, 1996, expanded thereafter), the 50-State Quarters program (1997) and its successors.

## Entity Theory read

The Mint is pure factory, no independence — it makes what the Secretary tells it to make, funded mostly by its own seigniorage rather than Congress's purse. It survives any change of political control completely unbothered because there is no leash to fight over; the Secretary already holds it outright. Its only real teeth are historical: a Founding-era willingness to execute its own officers for cheating the currency, and a modern accident of drafting — the platinum-coin loophole — that hands the Secretary of the Treasury a mathematically unlimited denomination with zero statutory ceiling, sitting quietly in the code next to the gram-and-millimeter specifications for a dime.
