OPM is being rebuilt as the government's central HR machine

Beyond job cuts, the Office of Personnel Management was being turned into the central hub for federal hiring and ranking, a shared HR platform, a digital records and data utility, and a payment-checking node. Many of the rules and systems are real; many of the results are not yet proven.

What we found

  • Retirement went digital: OPM reported that by June 30, 2026 more than 95% of applications were electronic and more than 155,000 had been processed over the prior year. It marked its last day of paper processing on July 1, 2026.
  • Federal HR 2.0: a December 10, 2025 OPM–OMB directive told agencies to stop duplicate HR system projects. Oracle won the governmentwide HR platform on June 10, 2026, and a fee-based OPM HR Shared Service Center was set up March 17, with transitions expected in FY2027. Migration and savings are not proven.
  • An August 27, 2026 memo encourages AI across hiring, including résumé screening, ranking and selection, while keeping veterans' preference, human review and reconsideration duties. A July 7, 2026 final rule removed the Level 2 rating, allowed standardized rating distributions, and starts a new rating cycle October 1, 2026.
  • A new OPM–Treasury Do Not Pay match for retirement payments legally starts September 25, 2026, after OPM's Inspector General found mostly manual controls and left 12 recommendations open. No match, adverse decision or recovery had happened by the cutoff.
  • In AFGE v. OPM, a federal court ruled on September 12, 2025 that OPM lacked power to order other agencies' probationary firings and entered a permanent injunction. The Ninth Circuit heard the appeal August 28, 2026; no decision by cutoff.

Why it matters

A clear example of the thesis: the federal personnel system is being rebuilt from inside, through OPM rules, memos, contracts and data matches rather than new statutes. The court case shows where that route hits a legal limit.

  • 2026-09-25 · First legal start of the OPM–Treasury Do Not Pay retirement-payment match.
  • 2026-10-01 · Start of the new standardized performance-rating cycle under the July 7 rule.

Emptying an agency is not the same as abolishing it

These maps separate five things that often get blurred: reclassifying career jobs, removing top officials, whether an agency still legally exists, moving or stopping its work, and a proposed replacement. An agency can lose its staff and work while the law that created it still stands.

What we found

  • Education: the statute (§ 3411) still names the Department. Interagency agreements move administration to other agencies, but those agencies collect the work without taking legal ownership.
  • USAID: the law (§ 6563) still names the agency even though State emptied it. P.L. 119-28 deals with money, not the agency's charter.
  • HHS and SAMHSA still legally exist. The proposed new entity, AHA, is only a request, and P.L. 119-75 still funds HRSA and SAMHSA.
  • Converting jobs to Schedule Policy/Career changes who can be fired without amending any charter. For independent agencies, FTC for-cause protection fell, while a Fed Governor's protection stands for now on the stay.
  • The maps keep four clocks apart: legal existence, staffing, transfer of functions, and money. A press release is not a repeal, and a House-passed bill is not law.

Why it matters

This is the thesis mechanism in plain view: government is being reshaped through staffing rules, funding and agreements between agencies, while the laws that create agencies mostly stay on the books. Because the legal shell remains, what happens next still runs through Congress and the courts.

What to watch

  • pending · Any enacted law that actually repeals or amends the statutes naming Education, USAID or HHS units, as opposed to transfers, cuts or proposals.
  • pending · Merits rulings in the Cook removal case and in Kennedy, which is so far only a preliminary injunction.

The government rooms hiding between the boxes

Our agency map is strong, but we have not consistently tracked the recurring public rooms where officials hear requests, coordinate methods, choose projects and turn advice into later action.

What we found

  • The Source Monitor has 1,077 official routes across 398 entities, but its route vocabulary has no first-class category for meeting indexes, minutes, recommendations, agency responses, stakeholder contacts, board actions or implementation dashboards.
  • The Federal Reserve quietly publishes a current stakeholder-contact ledger with participants, topics and attachments, while its statutory Federal Advisory Council publishes substantial quarterly records. These show what was requested or reported before later action, but never prove influence or agreement by themselves.
  • ACUS has an active fall 2026 pipeline on permits, regulatory warnings and frontline decisions. Draft, committee approval, plenary adoption, publication and agency use are separate clocks.
  • GSA's Federal Secure Cloud Advisory Committee publishes unusually rich minutes and recommendation records. July minutes reported 3,320 active agency authorizations and incomplete lifecycle reporting; a November vote on adoption and reuse is still future.
  • The Technology Modernization Fund portfolio exposes project selection, transfers, obligations, delay and cost-overrun flags. It is a rare bridge from policy priority to implementation state, though it is not independent proof of outcome.

Why it matters

These are the connective tissues of procedural reconstruction. Tracking a few material surfaces can reveal requests, shared methods, funding choices and implementation bottlenecks before a final rule, deployment or audit appears—without pretending that advice is authority or that a meeting caused the later decision.

What to watch

  • October 1–20, 2026 · ACUS fall committee meetings and any revised drafts, votes or committee dispositions.
  • October 20, 2026 · TMF initial proposal date for the 2026 permitting-technology and AI call.
  • November 2, 2026 · FSCAC meeting and planned vote on FedRAMP adoption-and-reuse recommendations.
  • December 10–11, 2026 · ACUS plenary disposition and TMF's stated Board-consideration boundary.

DOJ's complaint against Minnesota federal judges

DOJ has opened a judicial-conduct process over judges' public comments, but the filing itself changes no ruling, case assignment or judge's status.

What we found

  • The September 30 complaint names Chief Judge Patrick Schiltz, Judge John Tunheim and five unidentified District of Minnesota judges. It asks the Eighth Circuit to investigate and asks the judges to recuse from DHS matters.
  • The narrow public-comment claim has a real legal hook: Canon 3A(6) and Conduct Rule 4 can reach comments about pending or impending matters, and at least one cited case remained open.
  • The filing is not a finding. Chief Judge Steven Colloton must first screen it under Rule 11 and may dismiss it, conclude it, make a limited inquiry or appoint a special committee.
  • Earlier DOJ complaints against Judges Ana Reyes and James Boasberg were dismissed in 2025. Those orders said disputes over rulings and case-specific recusal normally belong in motions, appeals or mandamus, not the conduct process.
  • This complaint has better proof of public statements than those earlier ones. It is weaker where it seeks blanket DHS recusal, attacks rulings as wrong or groups five unnamed judges without individual quotations.

Why it matters

DOJ is asking the judiciary's internal discipline system to police judges' public speech during active litigation involving the executive branch. The key questions are whether the remarks addressed the merits of cases, whether they created a reasonable appearance of prejudice, and whether any remedy belongs in the conduct process or in individual cases.

What to watch

  • pending · An Eighth Circuit complaint number, chief-judge order, transfer, dismissal, conclusion or special-committee appointment.
  • pending · Any case-specific Section 455 motion, recusal order, reassignment or appellate ruling tied to the reported comments.
  • 2026-10-19 · The current response deadline on the Supreme Court docket in Avila v. Noem, one of the cases cited by DOJ.

The four Trump indictments were four different legal stories

The famous 91-count total was a real peak snapshot, but only New York reached a verdict; the other three cases ended before a jury resolved the allegations.

What we found

  • New York's 34 counts were the same first-degree business-record offense repeated across 11 invoices, 12 ledger entries and 11 checks or check stubs. The felony theory depended on an alleged election-law conspiracy, and the jury convicted on all 34.
  • The federal classified-documents superseding indictment had 42 total counts across three defendants but 40 against Trump. It was dismissed before trial on Special Counsel appointment and funding grounds, not after an evidentiary acquittal.
  • The federal election case charged four conspiracy or obstruction offenses, not insurrection or sedition. Supreme Court immunity and obstruction rulings changed the available conduct and legal route before DOJ dismissed without prejudice under its sitting-President rule.
  • Georgia began with 13 Trump counts. Courts quashed five, and the replacement prosecutor abandoned the remaining eight after identifying serious problems with intent, proof, venue, precedent, RICO fit, delay and resources.
  • The clean record is one conviction under appeal, three cases ending before merits verdicts, and no acquittal in any of the four cases.

Why it matters

Aggregate headlines flattened four sovereigns, four charging structures and several distinct legal clocks into a single number. The primary documents show both more legal specificity and much more procedural qualification than the usual shorthand.

What to watch

  • pending · A merits decision in New York Appellate Division Case 2025-00648 or later New York Court of Appeals review.
  • pending authorization or release · Publication of Special Counsel Volume II concerning the classified-documents investigation.

When the Fed is not the expert owner

The Fed OIG turned one construction failure into an agency-wide rule: for major work outside its core expertise, the Board needs imported delivery skill, independent challenge and controls that survive into the next project.

What we found

  • The Eccles-1951 finding is a capability boundary, not a claim that the Federal Reserve is generally incompetent: monetary, regulatory and supervisory expertise does not automatically create construction or enterprise-technology delivery expertise.
  • The OIG's seventh recommendation calls for an agency-wide framework with full-scope budgets, stated cost limits, KPIs, accountability, materiality-based escalation, oversight criteria and independent senior members.
  • Chairman Warsh accepted the program and directed GSA project leadership, an independent awarded-cost audit, GMP negotiations, fixed metrics, contract reconciliation and permanent standards, but those are directions rather than completed results.
  • The independent audit can connect contractor and subcontractor awards to invoices, cost codes, construction in process and eventual fixed assets; the public financial statements currently show only aggregate construction in process.
  • The decisive durability test is first reuse: whether the framework actually governs planned IT modernization or another named major initiative before costs and schedule drift become irreversible.

Why it matters

This could change how an unusually autonomous, self-funded institution governs work outside its core mandate. The Board would keep legal authority while deliberately separating ownership, specialized execution, independent assurance and senior oversight—a portable control constitution rather than a one-project repair.

What to watch

  • 2026-12-31 or earlier on publication · An operative GSA project-executive instrument, named independent auditor, audit protocol or completed cost-validation result.
  • pending · A signed GMP or enforceable substitute, fixed original/current baselines, fee cap, service reconciliation and any reimbursement or project credit.
  • 2027-03-31 or earlier on publication · An issued agency-wide significant-initiative policy and the Board's 2026 construction-in-process and placed-in-service accounting.
  • pending · First documented use of the framework on IT modernization or another major non-core initiative, followed by measurable outcome evidence.

Agency strategic plans are a legal cycle, and many still are not out

Federal agency strategic plans are a required, recurring cycle that ties goals to yearly plans, budgets and results reports. A stated goal is not funding, legal authority or a result, and as of September 2026 several cabinet and small-agency plans were still missing.

What we found

  • Under 5 U.S.C. § 306, covered agencies owed a new plan on the first Monday in February after a presidential term began. For this cycle that was February 2, 2026, covering at least FY 2026 to FY 2030. The CIA, GAO, USPS and Postal Regulatory Commission are left out of this duty.
  • The Federal Agency Performance Act of 2024 (Public Law 118-190) added yearly strategic reviews. A June 2026 GAO report (GAO-26-108516) found OMB and the four sampled agencies had not fully put the new rules in place.
  • The President's Management Agenda, issued December 8, 2025 as OMB memo M-26-03, groups its goals under: shrink government and cut waste, ensure accountability, and deliver results and buy American.
  • A September 18, 2026 refresh found seven newly final agency-wide plans and three newly located component plans, along with missed publication forecasts. The study works from fixed lists of 392 entities and 226 congressional committee bodies and finished 18 first-wave reviews, without claiming a full census.
  • On the SEC's EDGAR filing system, the current work order sits under the OneIT multi-award contract. No matching open notice was found, so outside technology most likely enters through a listed prime contractor, a later on-ramp or a narrow unsolicited-proposal channel.

Why it matters

Strategic plans are the paperwork through which an administration writes its direction into every agency's goals, budgets and reports. They are a procedural lever, but the study is clear that a plan is not proof anything changed.

What to watch

  • pending · The still-open queue of cabinet and small-agency FY 2026–2030 plans that had not been published by September 18, 2026.
  • 2026 · Whether DHS, Treasury and GSA carry out the 2026 strategic reviews that GAO said they planned.
  • pending · Any EDGAR on-ramp, successor competition or new award showing which outside technology gets in.

New law puts 'other transaction' deals on USAspending, but slowly

Public Law 119-106, signed September 11, 2026, counts 'other transaction agreements' (OTAs) as federal awards that must be reported on USAspending. The legal change is done, but Treasury has until September 11, 2029 to build the reporting system, and nothing new was live at the cutoff.

What we found

  • GAO found about $77.5 billion in Defense Department OTAs for FY2021-FY2025 that were recorded in SAM.gov but missing from USAspending. That is a gap in public reporting, not money missing from all government records and not proof of fraud.
  • The law does not ban or cut OTAs, cancel awards, declassify anything or prove fraud. CBO estimated it costs under $500,000 to administer over FY2026-FY2031, and it comes with no dedicated funding.
  • By September 11, 2027: Treasury must post a yearly total of unreported spending with reasons, Treasury and OMB must publish the list of agencies that must report, inspectors general file first reviews, and GAO recommends updates to FAR clause 52.204-10. If the system is not done, Treasury must publish an interim OTA list (2027) and give Congress a plan (2028).
  • At the September 15 cutoff, USAspending showed no separate OTA award type and no Treasury or OMB implementation notice had been found.
  • Path to law: introduced March 2025 (S. 872 by Sen. Joni Ernst; H.R. 2069 by Rep. Barry Moore); Senate passed S. 872 June 10, 2026; House passed H.R. 2069 July 20; Senate passed it August 7; signed September 11. The law calls for automatic data feeds but does not mention blockchain or smart contracts.

Why it matters

Congress changed the legal meaning of a federal award at once, while the data pipes, agency lists and audits run on separate clocks over three years. The spending record is being rebuilt inside existing law and agencies, not replaced.

What to watch

  • 2027-09-11 · First unreported-spending report, first list of agencies that must report, first inspector-general reviews, GAO's FAR recommendation, and an interim OTA list if the system is not done.
  • 2028-09-11 · Treasury implementation plan to Congress if the OTA system is still incomplete.
  • 2029-09-11 · Deadline for automatic OTA data transmission and a centralized OTA view on USAspending.

The tax law stays put; who collects it and handles the data is changing

Congress still creates taxes and the IRS still assesses and enforces them. What is moving is the outer layer: border collection, private collection and reporting duties, contractors, electronic payment rails and wider use of tax data by other agencies.

What we found

  • The External Revenue Service is still only a proposal. A January 20, 2025 memo ordered a study and S.175 was introduced, but no agency, funding, staff or transfer of collection authority exists.
  • Customs is still small: under 4% of fiscal-year receipts as of June 2026, while income and payroll taxes supplied about 92%. A February 2026 Supreme Court ruling against IEEPA tariffs started a refund clock.
  • A 1% tax on certain cash-like transfers abroad took effect January 1, 2026. Private remittance providers must collect it and can be liable if they don't. The implementing rules are still proposed.
  • The statutory IRS Commissioner office is vacant; a new CEO runs daily operations and reports to the Treasury Secretary. TIGTA found a 28% net staff drop from January 2025 to January 2026. Direct File was suspended, and four Zero Paper contractors got contracts worth about $2.3 billion through possible extensions to 2030.
  • Tax data is spreading: a 2025 IRS–DHS/ICE agreement led to one large address-data transfer and lawsuits, and TIGTA found 1,124 known tax-data sharing agreements with no complete central list.

Why it matters

The legal core of taxation stays with Congress and the IRS while management, collection and data use shift to a new CEO, private intermediaries, contractors and other agencies. The change runs through delegation and contracts, not new tax law.

What to watch

  • pending · Final regulations for the 1% remittance-transfer tax.
  • pending · Monthly customs refunds after the IEEPA ruling, and the legal status of the Trade Act section 122 surcharge after July 24, 2026.
  • pending · Any IRS Commissioner nomination, or the document that defines the CEO's authority.

Two orders rename AI and set up America.gov as a front door

On September 29, 2026 the President signed two orders: one tells agencies to call AI 'Super Intelligence' in their own documents without changing what it means, and one makes America.gov a single front door to federal services while agencies keep their records and decisions.

What we found

  • The Super Intelligence order tells the executive branch to use 'Super Intelligence' or 'SI' instead of 'AI' in non-statutory documents. It borrows the existing legal definition of AI (15 U.S.C. 9401(3)) and sets no test for superhuman ability. Statutes, past regulations, contracts and grants do not change.
  • The America.gov order has GSA, the National Design Studio and OMB run the site, link it to Login.gov, and require agencies to expose existing services used by more than 100,000 people a year. IRS tax filing, the Department of War and intelligence services are excluded.
  • The originating agency keeps custody of records and the final decision. America.gov's own terms say it does not submit, decide or approve filings.
  • Today the site works as a question-and-answer search tool. The White House fact sheet says passport renewal and Medicare enrollment come 'later this year', while the site's Coming Soon page says early 2027.
  • At cutoff neither order had an Executive Order number or Federal Register citation. The study's wider census of AI plans across eleven federal entities is frozen at August 23 and was not updated.

Why it matters

This is the government changing its own vocabulary and building a shared front door on top of existing agencies, through orders and OMB memos rather than new law. The study is clear that the front door is direction for now, not working cross-agency transactions.

What to watch

  • ~2026-11-28 · The legislative proposal on a durable Super Intelligence definition, due within 60 days. It would still be only a proposal.
  • ~2026-12-28 · OMB's America.gov implementation memo, due within 90 days.
  • early 2027 · Whether passport renewal and Medicare enrollment actually work through America.gov.

The National Archives sets rules for keeping federal AI records

On August 21, 2026 the National Archives told agencies when AI prompts, outputs, logs, code and training copies count as federal records that must be kept under approved schedules. At the same time it is building its own AI tools on Google, Microsoft Azure and Amazon cloud services.

What we found

  • Guidance AC 11.2026 says AI material becomes a federal record when it is used in official business, relied on for a decision, shared, built into an agency system, under agency control, or required to be kept. It is records guidance only: not a keep-everything order and not an AI-use, privacy or FOIA rule.
  • Much AI material can still be destroyed under existing schedules: short-term items needed under 180 days, and drafts once the final product exists, unless they are needed as evidence of a decision. AI policy documents and AI training data have no general schedule yet; NARA is considering one.
  • AI output kept only on a vendor's platform, and never captured or used by the agency, is not necessarily a federal record.
  • NARA's February 2026 inventory lists 14 AI uses: 4 deployed (including Gemini in Google Workspace and Amazon Kendra search), 2 pilots and 8 in development. NARA rates all 14 'not high-impact', which is its own label.
  • NARA asked for $419.67 million for FY2027. Its electronic-records funding drops from $30 million enacted in FY2026 to $20 million requested, none identified as AI money, while its ERA 2.0 archive system is still being built and on-site storage nears capacity.

Why it matters

An old law, the Federal Records Act, is being applied by guidance to set the paper trail of AI-assisted government. What agencies capture or delete now decides what anyone can later prove about how they used AI.

What to watch

  • pending · A proposed or final general records schedule for AI training data and AI policy records.
  • pending · Whether NARA launches public AI search or an archival chatbot on Archives.gov; both were still pre-deployment.

Do Not Pay flags payments, but each program makes the call

Treasury's Do Not Pay is a shared screening network that checks payees against death, debt, exclusion and other records before money goes out. A match is only a signal; the agency or state program must check it and make its own legal decision.

What we found

  • Treasury runs the system, its data links, portal, API, state hubs and a payment-verification checkpoint. Treasury's own help pages say a match does not stop a payment by itself.
  • States can use it for federally funded programs through PARIS (Medicaid, TANF, SNAP, child care and more) and a Labor Department hub for unemployment insurance. That does not reach purely state-funded spending.
  • OMB memo M-25-32 (August 20, 2025) gave a four-year waiver from separate computer-matching agreements. Public Law 119-77 (February 10, 2026) strengthens state death-data sharing, effective December 27, 2026.
  • A May 29, 2026 OMB proposed rule would require states to screen before paying out federal funds. It was still proposed, not final, at the August 29 cutoff.
