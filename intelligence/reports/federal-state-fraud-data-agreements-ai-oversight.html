Federal and Florida Fraud Agreements — AI Oversight Interlock

Research contract

  • Question: What do the published federal-State fraud agreements and Florida instruments actually build, and how do they intersect with the federal transition to agency AI governance?
  • Scope: the public Ohio model MOU; Florida Secretary of State and State Treasury agreements; Florida fraud authorities and task-force lanes; the National Fraud Detection Center; DOJ data and AI-governance records; OMB M-25-21; and the September 14 Heartland expansion.
  • Exclusions: no claim that the Ohio form is identical to either Florida agreement; no inference that Florida recipient-level benefit records are shared; no claim that the National Fraud Detection Center uses AI, Palantir, or any other named platform absent a direct system record; no conversion of a lead, risk score, suspension, charge, intended loss, restitution order or demand letter into collected recovery.
  • Frozen cutoff: September 14, 2026, Eastern Time.
  • Lifecycle boundary: Workbench synthesis only. It creates no Chronicle promotion, canonical watch, records request, automation or app shipment.

Answer first

The agreements are building the permission and data-delivery layer of the new fraud architecture before they build a public result ledger. The clearest published specimen—the June 4 Ohio Secretary of State MOU—does not obligate or transfer funds. It authorizes specified public corporate data for fraud and financial-crime analysis, leaves schedules and ingestion schemas to a later technical annex or service-level agreement, assigns security and retention rules, and requires DOJ to validate records before investigative, regulatory or enforcement action.

That strongly supports a groundwork-first reading of Florida. Florida has two different announced data instruments:

  1. a Secretary of State corporate-registration route; and
  2. a State Treasury payment-transaction route.

These sit on top of an already mature State enforcement edge: the Office of Statewide Prosecution, the Department of Financial Services Division of Public Assistance Fraud, AHCA, DCF, the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit, the Florida IG Council and other State and local investigative bodies. The September 9 Florida Anti-Fraud Task Force adds the explicit handoff from National Fraud Detection Center leads to Florida prosecutors and investigators. It does not itself move money or prove that one lead has produced a payment hold, charge, judgment or recovery.

The AI interlock is real at the governance boundary, but the NFDC's own AI system is not yet publicly identified. OMB M-25-21 presumes AI used as a principal basis for detecting fraudulent use of government services, adjudicating benefits or penalties, and several law-enforcement decisions to be high-impact. DOJ already has Department-wide AI governance, an inventory, a central data catalog, AI-readiness work and adjacent accredited analytics platforms. More importantly, PRAC—an inaugural NFDC member—publicly says it is bringing its existing cross-agency data and advanced-analytics capabilities into the Center; PRAC's PACE platform uses machine learning, graph analytics and predictive risk models. The NFDC release itself, however, says only analytical capabilities, shared technology and generate criminal leads. No NFDC-specific AI inventory row, PIA, SORN, ATO, model documentation, vendor record or system diagram was located.

The best current ruling is:

The agreements are the data constitution for an AI-ready fraud network, not proof that an AI system is already making fraud, benefit or payment decisions.

September 14 statutory turn: permanence with broad executive discretion

The clearest new overhaul signal is legislative rather than financial. H.R. 9576, introduced July 2, would place the National Fraud Enforcement Division in title 28, make it a permanent DOJ division, and install a presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed Assistant Attorney General. The introduced text also enumerated seven duties: leading federal fraud enforcement, overseeing multi-district and multi-agency investigations, directing and assisting U.S. Attorneys, coordinating across agencies, setting national priorities, proposing legislative and regulatory reforms, and advising DOJ leadership on high-impact cases and policy.

The Rules Committee print posted September 10 keeps the statutory division and Assistant Attorney General but replaces those enumerated duties with a single clause: the official performs duties prescribed by the Attorney General. It also adds the Division to the title 5 political-activity restriction that already covers the Criminal Division and other named enforcement components.

As of the 5:44 p.m. EDT cutoff on September 14, the Rules Committee page still showed only the 4:00 p.m. meeting and the proposed print; it did not yet show a reported rule or House action. The print is therefore a live proposal, not enacted law. Its structural meaning is nevertheless important: Congress is considering converting April's administrative reorganization into a statutory division while leaving its operating portfolio unusually open to Attorney General direction. That supports a durable-overhaul reading, but it does not create appropriations, authorize an NFDC AI system, or prove operational use.

The federal agreement layer is at least two different instrument families

Further source work identifies an NFDC Charter in addition to the data- sharing agreements. SSA OIG, DOL OIG, HUD OIG, SBA OIG, VA OIG and USDA OIG each describe themselves as Charter signatories and say they committed dedicated analysts and investigators to the prosecutor-led Center. This proves a personnel and governance instrument, not merely an exchange of datasets. A separate clause-level deep dive found no public copy of the signed Charter, signature schedule, effective date, amendments or implementing appendices through the 6:04 p.m. EDT cutoff.

The public record therefore supports two separate federal instrument families:

Instrument family What is publicly proved What remains unavailable
NFDC Charter Named OIG signatories; dedicated analysts and investigators; prosecutor-led centralized collaboration Charter text, voting/command rules, data-access roles, recusal, retention and dispute provisions
Data-sharing agreements DOJ says fifteen federal-agency agreements exist; PRAC and OIG releases describe cross-program analytical collaboration Complete party list, agreement texts, source datasets, matching authority, refresh/correction rules and technical annexes

The fifteen-agreement statement should not be silently converted into fifteen charter signatories or fifteen data feeds. Public releases establish overlap, but not identity, between membership, personnel commitments and data-sharing instruments.

PRAC supplies the strongest proved AI/analytics bridge into NFDC

PRAC is an inaugural NFDC member and says it will continue bringing its data and analytical capabilities to the Center. Its existing public operating record is unusually concrete:

  • PACE combines public, non-public and commercial data in a secure cross-agency platform and supports OIG and law-enforcement work.
  • PRAC reports more than sixty data sources, more than one billion records, more than fifty federal partners and support for more than 1,320 investigations as of August 2026.
  • PRAC describes machine learning, predictive risk models, graph analytics, social-network analysis and automated processes as existing tools.
  • In the PBGC example, the model highlighted anomalies for auditor review and supported later administrative and civil recovery; it did not itself issue a benefit determination or collect funds.
  • Congress extended PRAC through September 30, 2034 and supplied $88 million in the 2025 reconciliation law, giving this analytic capacity a life beyond the original pandemic sunset.

This changes the thesis carefully. We no longer need to infer that every NFDC participant arrives with only conventional spreadsheets. A named member is bringing a mature AI/ML-capable analytics stack. But the evidence still does not show whether PACE is the NFDC platform, whether Florida records enter PACE, which models touch those records, or whether an NFDC-specific high-impact AI determination has been made.

What the public Ohio MOU proves

Ohio is the usable instrument specimen because the Ohio Secretary of State published the signed six-page MOU linked from its official June 4 announcement. It is evidence of the agreement family, not a substitute for the unavailable Florida texts.

Clause Operative content Why it matters
Parties and purpose Ohio Secretary of State and DOJ National Fraud Enforcement Division establish a framework to share public business-entity data for prevention, detection, investigation, financial-crime analysis and prosecution This is an intergovernmental data-use instrument, not an appropriation or payment-control order
Authority Each party acts under existing legal authority; the MOU does not expand or limit constitutional, statutory or regulatory power The agreement opens a route; it does not create new substantive jurisdiction
Data Corporate entity types, names, registration numbers, status, reports, filing history and available agent/office/control-person fields These fields can support entity-resolution and link analysis, but filings are not verified beneficial-ownership findings
Extensibility Additional public data elements may be added by written addendum The published object is a framework, not necessarily the complete future dataset
Permitted use Identify, audit, evaluate, investigate and prosecute fraud and related crimes; Computer Matching Act programs are excluded unless an exemption applies Broad analytical use is allowed, but statutory matching rules remain a live boundary
Delivery Periodic extracts and file-transfer protocols are contemplated The architecture is designed for repeatable ingest, not occasional manual lookup
Hidden implementation layer Automation schedules, data-ingestion schemas, transmission frequencies and technical parameters go in a separate annex or service-level agreement The most important operating object is not public in the specimen
Security NIST-compliant safeguards, DOJ policy, FOUO handling, need-to-know access and training Public source records can become a controlled analytical corpus after transfer
Retention DOJ retains only as long as necessary; incorporated federal records follow NARA schedules The source record and DOJ analytical/case record can have different custodians and clocks
Accuracy Ohio disclaims completeness, accuracy, timeliness and investigative fitness; DOJ must independently validate before action A match or network edge is expressly not enough for action
Costs No obligation or commitment of funds and no transfer of funds; each party bears its own costs unless a later agreement says otherwise This directly separates groundwork from the money clock

The MOU's deepest significance is therefore not DOJ now has a list. Public business records were already obtainable. The instrument normalizes frequency, format, ingestion, permitted use, custody and responsibility so that records from many jurisdictions can be analyzed as one recurring input family.

Florida has two distinct announced instruments

1. Secretary of State: corporate-registration data

DOJ says Florida's Secretary of State joined the Southern-State agreements that give the Fraud Division and NFDC access to publicly available corporate registration data. The exact Florida agreement was not located.

Florida's public Sunbiz service already publishes weekday data files through a public data portal and SFTP, including corporate filings and events, federal tax liens, fictitious names and partnerships. This means the likely operational gain is a standardized, timely, no-cost or machine-ready feed and agreed use, not the first legal visibility into Florida corporations.

The source quality limit is unusually important. Florida's Division of Corporations describes itself as an administrative filing agency that accepts compliant filings at face value and says filing does not establish the correctness or validity of the information. A graph edge from a Sunbiz address, agent or officer field is therefore a lead for corroboration, not proof of ownership, control or fraud.

2. CFO / State Treasury: public payment-transaction data

Florida CFO's August 3 announcement says the signed MOU allows sharing and use of publicly available payment transaction data maintained by the Florida State Treasury for fraud detection, investigation and financial-crime analysis. It does not publish the MOU text or identify exact tables and fields.

This is narrower and more precise than DOJ's later description of Florida's public benefits payment data. Public Florida surfaces include bulk State vendor-payment files and FLAIR-derived disbursement, object and vendor data. The available evidence does not establish that the MOU transfers person-level SNAP, Medicaid, TANF or other recipient records. Until the text or technical annex is published, public State payment transactions and individual public-benefit records must remain different hypotheses.

That distinction changes the likely analytic object:

If the feed is... Likely useful join What it still does not establish
Public vendor/disbursement data payee ↔ State agency ↔ object/category ↔ amount ↔ date ↔ corporate registrant Beneficial ownership, federal award lineage, recipient eligibility or fraud
Contract/grant payment data contractor/grantee ↔ agreement ↔ State payment ↔ corporate network Downstream expenditure, performance, intent or collected recovery
Person-level benefit data recipient/provider ↔ program ↔ claim/payment ↔ identity attributes This remains unproved and would require a more specific legal/privacy account

The exact Florida Treasury feed is therefore the highest-value State return.

Florida is a prebuilt State edge

The September 9 federal-State Florida Anti-Fraud Task Force is not Florida's first fraud body. It connects several already-authorized lanes:

Florida object Existing authority or function New federal interlock
Office of Statewide Prosecution Florida Statute 16.56 authorizes investigation and prosecution of fraud and related offenses spanning or affecting two or more judicial circuits and permits State-agency assistance A statewide prosecutorial owner can receive or coordinate multijurisdictional leads
DFS Division of Public Assistance Fraud Florida Statute 414.411 directs DFS to investigate public assistance and examine records, including EBT records; DPAF already works with DCF, AHCA, Education, USDA, SSA and HHS NFDC can add cross-program and cross-jurisdiction lead context without replacing program or prosecutorial authority
State Public Assistance Fraud Task Force Announced April 15 under the Attorney General with a Special Prosecutor, multi-circuit case review and warrant/affidavit support Predates the September federal-State task force; no public instrument proves the two bodies merged
Florida Anti-Fraud Task Force Convened by three U.S. Attorneys and the Florida Attorney General; includes federal OIGs, Florida enforcement bodies and local inspectors general DOJ says it will use NFDC-generated leads
Sunbiz and State Treasury feeds Corporate-registration and public payment-transaction source families Recurring data inputs can be normalized for national entity and payment-pattern analysis

The resulting topology is more specific than Florida joined a task force:

State source records → NFDC analytical lead → Florida federal/State task-force triage → investigative corroboration → agency or prosecutor action → court or administrative disposition → collection/recovery

Every arrow is a separate authority, record owner and clock.

The AI oversight interlock

Confirmed federal control environment

  • OMB M-25-21 requires agency Chief AI Officers, inventories, high-impact use classification, monitoring, independent review, human oversight, remedies and appeals where applicable.
  • M-25-21 specifically presumes AI serving as a principal basis for fraud detection in government services, benefits adjudication and penalties to be high-impact. Human review does not by itself remove the classification.
  • DOJ's January 30, 2026 public inventory contains 315 entries and describes a Department-wide review process involving the Chief AI Officer, component leaders and governance bodies. It predates the NFDC.
  • DOJ's April 2026 Open Data Plan says its internal Justice Data Catalog uses standardized metadata and is intended to promote sharing and reuse; it explicitly joins data governance to enterprise AI readiness.
  • DOJ already publishes adjacent examples of governed analytics: EOUSA's Palantir data platform can integrate multiple data formats, perform link and outlier analysis and use AIP summaries to inform investigative and litigation strategy; DOJ OIG's ODAS uses statistical and, in some tools, AI/ML analysis to identify anomalies and possible investigative leads.

These examples prove that DOJ knows how to place analytics inside PIAs, ATOs, access controls, audit logging, retention schedules and AI inventory review. They do not prove that either platform is used by the NFDC.

What is inferred, not proved

The combination of recurring State extracts, fifteen announced federal-agency agreements, embedded inspector-general analysts, shared technology and a cross-program lead mission is structurally suited to:

  • entity resolution across business names, addresses, agents and payees;
  • graph/link analysis across programs and jurisdictions;
  • anomaly and outlier detection;
  • risk ranking and lead prioritization; and
  • natural-language summarization of supporting records.

Calling that AI-ready is a supported architectural inference. Calling it an operating NFDC AI system would outrun the evidence.

Where oversight must attach

The crucial boundary is not simply is AI present? It is how far an output travels toward a consequential decision.

Stage Record needed Control owner to identify High-impact pressure
Source ingest agreement, data dictionary, provenance, correction and refresh rules source agency data steward + DOJ receiving owner data quality and privacy; not yet a fraud decision
Entity resolution match method, confidence, ambiguous-link handling NFDC/system owner false association risk
Risk/lead generation model or rules documentation, threshold, version and explanation NFDC analytical owner + DOJ CAIO if AI may become high-impact if the output is a principal basis for law-enforcement or benefit action
Human triage reviewer identity, corroboration standard, override and disposition prosecutor/OIG/investigative supervisor human involvement does not erase AI risk
Investigative opening lead-to-case audit trail and legal predicate investigative agency or OIG law-enforcement impact becomes concrete
Administrative/payment action authoritative program rule, notice, contest and reinstatement path program agency or payment owner benefits/payment detection and adjudication are expressly presumed high-impact when AI is a principal basis
Charge or civil filing evidence independent of raw score and prosecutorial review DOJ/USAO/State prosecutor allegation remains distinct from adjudication
Judgment and collection final order, debt record, payment and correction records court, agency, Treasury or State collection owner only here can an amount move into adjudicated and collected states

The emerging governance problem is federated: if an NFDC-generated analytic output is used by another department to block a benefit or payment, DOJ may own the model while the program agency owns the adverse action. The public record does not yet show which CAIO classifies the use, which agency accepts risk, how an affected person reaches the source record, or how corrections propagate from Florida through the federal analytic derivative.

September 14 expansion: architecture before recovery

DOJ's same-day Heartland announcement adds three more federal-State cooperation agreements—with the Missouri Secretary of State, Nebraska Treasurer and Kansas Treasurer—and reports a 44-USAO SBA enforcement surge. This shows that the agreement pattern is being replicated beyond the Southeast.

The release reports nearly 80 defendants charged with about $100 million in intended loss, about 43 guilty pleas associated with $44 million in intended loss, and about 40 sentencings associated with nearly $100 million in intended loss. It rolls these into more than 160 defendants and about $245 million in intended loss. Those figures are procedural and allegation/sentencing cohorts, not one additive recovery ledger. The release also reports 870,000 suspended borrowers tied to $39 billion in suspected PPP/EIDL activity; suspension and suspected activity are not adjudication or collected recovery.

The announcement attributes Operation No Doze to the National Fraud Enforcement Division, but it does not say the cases originated in NFDC leads or the new State agreements. The strongest proved same-day change is replication of the institutional and data-sharing model, not a newly observed money movement.

Exact next returns

These are research-return objects, not active watches or authorized requests:

  1. The executed Florida Secretary of State agreement and any addenda.
  2. The executed Florida State Treasury MOU, including exact datasets, fields and whether the feed is vendor/disbursement, grant/contract or recipient-level.
  3. Any Technical Implementation Annex or Service Level Agreement defining automation schedule, schema, transfer method, access roles and correction.
  4. The complete fifteen-federal-agency agreement list and the legal instrument for each source family.
  5. The NFDC Charter, including command, personnel, data-access, records, correction and dispute provisions.
  6. NFDC PIA, SORN mapping, ATO/system identifier and records schedule.
  7. DOJ AI inventory addition or high-impact determination covering NFDC fraud analytics, including any written determination that a presumed category is not high-impact.
  8. Model/rules card, thresholds, validation, subgroup/error testing, versioning, reviewer protocol, override and appeal/remedy route.
  9. One case ledger linking source record → analytic lead → corroboration → investigative opening → charge/action → disposition → collected amount.

The DOJ Fraud Division FOIA page currently says its public FOIA library has no documents. A focused request for the Florida agreements, federal agreement list, technical annexes and NFDC privacy/system records is therefore a viable future route, but nothing has been filed or authorized.
