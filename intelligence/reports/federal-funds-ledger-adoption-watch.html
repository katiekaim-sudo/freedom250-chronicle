Federal Funds Ledger — Grants, State Pass-Throughs, and Public Auditability

Completed: 2026-07-23
Status: current Workbench watchboard; no automatic Chronicle watch or promotion
Evidence cutoff: 2026-07-23
Inherited thesis: The On-Chain State — The EO Trail and the Quantum Layer
Registered forecast: MASTER PREDICTION — The Sovereign Ledger, M7

Short answer

Katie's theory is already a registered Observatory thesis and forecast:

fraud and improper-payment pressure
  → transaction-level federal controls
  → conditions on new federal awards
  → state and subrecipient flow-down
  → public or permissioned spending ledger

The theory has materially strengthened, but the exact legal state matters:

The United States is building a government-wide payment-justification, eligibility-screening and machine-readable reporting architecture. It is not yet operating a government-wide public blockchain for federal spending, and no located authority requires all States to transmit federal funds on-chain.

The most important new evidence is OMB's May 29, 2026 proposed Uniform Guidance rule. It would require States to review relevant eligibility data before disbursing federal award funds; require federal agencies to screen payments through Do Not Pay; and require agency payment systems to record a brief written justification for every payment request. OMB expressly says the State-screening rule would not require a particular payment system or technology. That technology-neutrality is not contrary evidence. It is the insertion seam through which a later Treasury-approved ledger, cryptographic receipt or public-proof standard could enter without first rewriting the policy rationale.

The strongest current read is therefore:

  • the control architecture is moving;
  • a publicly queryable federal grant-payment feed is already live in a bounded HHS Payment Management System implementation;
  • the ledger specification is still open;
  • new discretionary grants and slower disaster-reconstruction awards are the cleanest first State-adoption lanes;
  • a permissioned authoritative system with a public API or public cryptographic anchor is more likely than raw PII-bearing transactions on a permissionless chain;
  • a conventional append-only database remains a live competing branch and must not be mislabeled blockchain.

1. What changed the evidence grade

1.1 Executive direction already created an every-payment record

Executive Order 14222, signed February 26, 2025, directed each agency to build a centralized technological system covering payments under covered contracts and grants. The system is to record a responsible employee's written justification, permit pause and review, and publish justifications to the maximum extent practicable and permitted by law.

That is already a transaction-level control ledger in the generic sense. The order does not select blockchain, distributed consensus, a token, a wallet or a public chain.

The order also contains exclusions for direct assistance to individuals and for certain immigration, law-enforcement, military, public-safety, intelligence, critical and acute or emergency spending. That makes immediate-response disaster checks a weaker first blockchain candidate than post-emergency Public Assistance, reconstruction, reimbursement and subaward chains.

Primary source: EO 14222 — Implementing the President's “Department of Government Efficiency” Cost Efficiency Initiative

1.2 Treasury is centralizing the payment rail without selecting a chain

Executive Order 14247, signed March 25, 2025, directed a transition from paper checks and cash to electronic funds transfer for federal disbursements and receipts. Its payment-method language includes Treasury-centralized systems, real-time payment systems, digital wallets and other modern electronic methods. It does not create a CBDC and does not choose a blockchain.

Executive Order 14249, signed the same day, supplies the transaction-control graph: Treasury pre-certification and Do Not Pay review; standardized payee, appropriation, contract and financial-assistance identifiers; earlier payment data; consolidation of agency core financial systems; movement of disbursements to Treasury; and daily reporting by remaining non-Treasury disbursing offices. Payment Automation Manager already rejects files missing valid Treasury Account Symbol / Business Event Type Code data. That is a live centralized gate, not DLT.

Primary sources: EO 14247 — Modernizing Payments To and From America's Bank Account · EO 14249 — Protecting America's Bank Account Against Fraud, Waste, and Abuse · Fiscal Service PAM FAQ

1.3 OMB proposed the State and agency flow-down seam

OMB's May 29, 2026 proposed rule, OMB-2026-0034, RIN 0348-AB88, closed its comment period on July 13. OMB proposed an October 1, 2026 effective date for fiscal-year 2027 awards, but this is still a proposed rule at cutoff.

The key objects are:

Proposed provision Proposed control Why it matters Limiter
2 CFR 200.303(g) States review relevant data before disbursing federal award funds to verify payee eligibility and prevent improper payments, using Do Not Pay or alternative screening Moves the control obligation below the federal prime-payment gate OMB expressly disclaims requiring a specific payment system or technology
2 CFR 200.305(a) Federal agencies review Do Not Pay eligibility information before federal payments Places screening immediately before release A risk match is not a fraud adjudication
2 CFR 200.305(c) Federal agency payment systems record a brief written justification for each payment request; non-State recipients and subrecipients use the capability as systems become available Creates the transaction-level explanation field required by EO 14222 The proposed text does not impose the same justification-system requirement on States

The proposal is the strongest located bridge between federal payment control and State-administered money. It is not yet a blockchain requirement and not yet a final rule.

Primary sources: Federal Register proposed rule · permanent GovInfo PDF

1.4 A live public grant-payment feed now proves the function

The administration's live Defend the Spend feed begins with HHS Payment Management System grant payments. It publishes approved or disbursed payment items with recipient and agency justifications, exposes a public API, updates weekly, and states an intention to expand coverage and converge toward real-time reporting.

This is the strongest current evidence for Katie's open-source / publicly queryable / real-time accounting function. Its current architecture is a centralized federal system with an open API, not a blockchain. It therefore does two things at once:

  1. confirms that transaction-level public accounting is no longer only a forecast; and
  2. creates a competing architecture that may satisfy policymakers without a public distributed ledger.

Primary sources: Defend the Spend payment feed · public API documentation

1.5 Treasury already tested a permissioned grant ledger

In October 2021, Treasury's Bureau of the Fiscal Service announced a task order for a blockchain payment-request prototype whose stated goal included executing a federal grant payment using blockchain. Treasury described the possible benefits as transparency, reduced reporting burden, stronger internal controls, and reduced fraud and improper payments. It also identified unresolved questions about node ownership, wallets, cryptocurrency standards and cross-agency authority.

The controlling closeout is the December 2023 JFMIP report. The Fiscal Service/GAO prototype represented a tokenized grant moving from grantor to grantee to subgrantee on a private, permissioned Ethereum Proof-of-Authority network. It was illustrative, not prescriptive, made no policy recommendations, and did not involve or affect an actual grant transaction. The report also states the decisive limitation: a blockchain can preserve an input without proving that the off-chain event occurred or that the transaction was legal, authorized or nonfraudulent.

This is the cleanest primary-source proof that “blockchain for a federal grant payment” is not merely an outside theory. It remains prototype evidence, not proof of a successor program, a production transaction or a government-wide deployment.

Primary sources: Fiscal Service Testing Blockchain to Streamline Grant Payment Processes · JFMIP Blockchain Initiative · JFMIP-24-01 final report

2. The present funds stack

The missing domestic map is not one system. It is an authority and reconciliation chain:

appropriation
  → apportionment / allotment
  → obligation
  → prime award
  → payment or draw authority
  → State / recipient account
  → subaward
  → provider / vendor / household transaction
  → outlay
  → correction / reversal / deobligation
  → recovery
  → public disclosure

Current systems occupy different parts of that chain:

Surface Current job What it does not prove
GTAS Treasury platform for standardized government-wide financial reporting Not a public transaction ledger and not a blockchain
ASAP Treasury payment system through which agencies authorize and recipients draw federal funds, with limits, approvals and current reports Does not expose every lower-tier expenditure to the public
Do Not Pay Prepayment eligibility and risk-data matching A match is not a legal fraud finding
USAspending / Data Broker Machine-readable award publication, validation and standardization under the DATA Act/FFATA model Publication data are not the authoritative bank/payment record; lower-tier visibility and data quality remain incomplete
Defend the Spend Public grant-payment justifications and API for a bounded subset of HHS Payment Management System activity, with a stated real-time target Currently centralized and incomplete; does not prove blockchain or government-wide coverage
Agency and State systems Program eligibility, accounting, payment administration and supporting records Fragmented records do not create one reconciled public chain

ASAP already supplies much of the operating skeleton a future grant ledger would need: agency authorization, recipient enrollment, withdrawal limits, payment requests, approval/rejection and reporting. Treasury reported that ASAP handled about $725 billion for 68 agencies and 26,000 organizations in fiscal year 2023.

Primary sources: ASAP payment-justification page · ASAP brochure · USAspending API · USAspending API source code · DATA Act Broker source code

GAO's current work makes the authority gap explicit. In June 2026, GAO asked Congress to give Treasury clear authority over government-wide recipient eligibility data standards and interoperability across the award lifecycle. Separately, GAO began a series on GTAS and continued to document weaknesses in USAspending completeness and consistency.

Primary sources: GAO-26-107466 — recipient eligibility data · GAO-26-107961 — GTAS · GAO-24-106214 — USAspending data · GAO-24-106164 — grants data standards

4. Likely adoption waves

Wave 0 — machine-readable publication and standardization

State: operational but incomplete.

USAspending, the DATA Act model, Treasury reporting and open-source publication tools create the data vocabulary. This is the substrate, not an on-chain system.

Wave 1 — federal every-payment justification

State: directed, with a bounded live HHS grant-payment feed; broader implementation evidence must be collected agency by agency.

EO 14222 creates the record-and-review requirement. EO 14247 centralizes electronic payment methods. Defend the Spend shows the public-feed function in operation for part of HHS PMS. The OMB proposal would regularize related controls in Uniform Guidance.

Wave 2 — new discretionary and milestone-based grants

State: most plausible first mandatory adoption lane.

A new NOFO or award can provide clear advance notice, define the reporting object, attach additional conditions based on risk, require flow-down to subrecipients, and connect payment to milestone acceptance. Research, technology, infrastructure and targeted resilience grants are cleaner candidates than legacy entitlement systems.

Wave 3 — State-administered reconstruction and reimbursement

State: plausible early case study, not yet located as a ledger mandate.

Disaster money has urgency, duplication-of-benefits risk, multiple payors, project milestones, contractor chains and clawbacks. Yet EO 14222's acute or emergency exclusion makes immediate relief a poor first test. FEMA Public Assistance and other slower reconstruction/reimbursement flows are better: projects, eligible costs, drawdowns, supporting documents, subawards, vendors, insurance proceeds, duplication checks and recoveries can be represented as linked objects.

GAO's 2026 work supplies the need: EDA could not efficiently reconcile awards and disbursements, while FEMA and SBA still needed a real-time applicant-data interface to identify duplication of benefits.

Primary sources: GAO-26-107742 — EDA disaster awards · GAO-25-107608 — FEMA/SBA duplication of benefits · FEMA Public Assistance process

Treasury and FEMA also used Digital Payout with FedNow for instant disaster-recovery payments in 2025. That is a live real-time payment precedent, not a blockchain precedent. It shows why a later ledger may attach as provenance and reconciliation around an existing dollar payment rail rather than replace the rail.

Primary source: Fiscal Service — FedNow available through Digital Payout

Wave 4 — State program flow-down

State: policy pressure is visible; mandatory ledger term not located.

The likeliest mechanism is not “all States convert everything or lose all funding.” It is a phase-in:

  1. new program or new fiscal-year award;
  2. clear data and transaction schema in the notice and award;
  3. Treasury-approved interface or payment system;
  4. pass-through terms to subrecipients;
  5. payment hold, corrective action or disallowance for noncompliance;
  6. federal matching funds or implementation grants to reduce conversion cost.

SNAP and Medicaid illustrate the pressure because administrative accuracy is becoming fiscal exposure. Their scale, entitlement structure, State accounting rules and due-process obligations also make them later and harder adoption targets than bounded discretionary grants.

Wave 5 — government-wide statutory ledger standard

State: no located enactment.

This would require Congress to settle Treasury's authority over award-lifecycle data standards, protected-data lanes, public disclosure, correction and government-wide applicability. This is the cleanest legal path to uniformity and the least developed current object.

State public-chain laboratory — Wyoming

Wyoming is the strongest located State-level public-chain precedent, but it does not yet prove open statewide accounting. The State launched its FRNT/WYST stable token across interoperable public networks and published a public-agency reserve attestation. State materials have proposed vendor payments, tax and fee receipts, social-service payments and disaster uses. Those government use cases must remain labeled proposed or tested, not general production accounting.

Wyoming matters because it can supply a future interoperability partner for a federal award pilot. It also supplies a falsifier: public-chain infrastructure can exist without being adopted by State agencies as the authoritative spending ledger, and its continued operation depends on funding and economics.

Primary sources: Wyoming 2025 factbook · May 2025 Commission update · proposed Wyoming Token Expansion Program

5. Legal pressure map

Existing law provides substantial technology-neutral authority:

  • 2 CFR 200.302 requires State financial-management systems to track federal expenditures and demonstrate compliance while generally using State laws and procedures;
  • 2 CFR 200.303 requires effective internal controls and safeguarding of sensitive information;
  • agencies can attach award-specific conditions under 2 CFR 200.208 when the statutory and risk basis is present;
  • pass-through entities must transmit award terms and monitor subrecipients under 2 CFR 200.332;
  • payment holds, disallowed costs, suspension, termination and other remedies exist for noncompliance;
  • 2 CFR 200.336 favors open, machine-readable award information when practicable and controls against alteration;
  • the Spending Clause permits conditions on federal funds when Congress gives clear notice and the condition is related to the federal program.

But the current law does not equal a universal public-blockchain power:

  • ordinary financial reporting generally cannot be demanded more frequently than quarterly unless a valid specific condition supports it;
  • the current machine-readable rule retains a paper-on-request fallback;
  • States retain their own accounting procedures, subject to federal standards and program rules;
  • Cash Management Improvement Act agreements govern important State/federal cash-timing relationships;
  • nonfederal supporting records do not automatically become public records;
  • formula, block, entitlement and disaster programs have different statutory and termination constraints;
  • threatening all legacy funds can become unconstitutionally coercive even where a clearly stated condition on a new award would survive.

Primary sources: 2 CFR 200.302 · 2 CFR 200.303 · 2 CFR 200.208 · 2 CFR 200.328 · 2 CFR 200.332 · 2 CFR 200.336 · 2 CFR 200.338 · 2 CFR 200.339 · 31 CFR part 205 — CMIA rules · Constitution Annotated — spending conditions · clear-notice doctrine · anti-coercion limit

Subrecipient and vendor lanes must also remain separate. Award terms can flow from a State pass-through entity into subawards under 2 CFR 200.332. A vendor is a procurement object, so transaction-event duties need a procurement clause; they do not automatically bind every downstream contractor merely because the prime award contains them.

6. Architecture branches

Branch A — public-chain transaction or public anchoring

Government or its payment agent posts a transaction or cryptographic commitment to a public network. Public verification is strongest, but metadata leakage, fees, chain governance, sanctions, correction and public-record rights become first-order problems.

Strong evidence: a NOFO, award term, task order or rule names a public chain, wallet, transaction identifier, on-chain schema and authoritative-record rule.

Branch B — permissioned DLT with a public proof surface

Treasury, agencies, States and authorized recipients share a controlled ledger. Protected records remain inside the permissioned perimeter; public users receive redacted transaction data, proofs, anchors or an API. This is the branch most consistent with the SWIFT/DTC institutional pattern and current privacy duties.

Strong evidence: node/admission rules, consensus or notary design, signed records, correction/reversal process, operator, data tiers and legal finality are published or procured.

Branch C — conventional append-only database plus open API

Agencies implement EO 14222 and the OMB rule through ordinary centralized systems, event logs and USAspending-style APIs. This can provide near-real-time open accounting without distributed consensus. Defend the Spend is the clearest live federal example of this branch.

Meaning: the open-accounting thesis advances, but M7 is not satisfied.

Branch D — fragmented compliance

Agencies add justification fields and State screenings to disconnected legacy systems. Publication remains delayed; subawards and lower-tier transactions remain hard to reconcile.

Meaning: policy direction exists, but the interoperable ledger thesis stalls.

7. The minimum viable federal funds ledger

The useful object is not a list of wallet transfers. Each transaction needs linked authority and correction state:

Object Minimum fields
authority appropriation, program statute, account, fiscal year, availability
award agency, recipient, UEI, assistance listing, award terms, amount, dates
subaward pass-through entity, subrecipient, flow-down terms, amount, purpose
payment/draw requestor, approver, amount, time, payment rail, justification
eligibility/control screening performed, source class, result class, human review; no public PII
performance milestone, acceptance record, invoice/cost category, supporting-record hash
accounting obligation, outlay, accrual, classification, authoritative system
correction superseding entry, reason, approver, dispute/appeal state
recovery questioned cost, debt, offset, refund, settlement
disclosure public/redacted/protected/classified lane and legal basis

The controlling rule must be:

Append-only does not mean “the first entry is eternally true.” It means every correction, reversal, deobligation, refund and dispute remains linked and inspectable while the authoritative current state is explicit.

Pre-registered positive triggers

  1. A federal instrument names a public or permissioned distributed ledger for spending, payments or award reporting.
  2. A new federal award requires a State or pass-through entity to submit transaction-level records through that ledger.
  3. Treasury publishes a common transaction identifier joining award, draw, payment, subaward, outlay, correction and recovery.
  4. A disaster-reconstruction payment is publicly traceable from federal award to State draw to subrecipient/vendor, with protected data handled separately.
  5. A public proof can be independently verified while Treasury or the agency identifies the authoritative legal record and correction process.

Falsifiers and downgrades

  1. The final OMB rule deletes the State prepayment-review or payment-justification provisions.
  2. EO 14222 implementation remains fragmented and produces no usable public record.
  3. The 2023 JFMIP closeout remains the last federal grant-DLT object, no successor reaches an actual transaction, and future procurement remains non-DLT.
  4. By mid-2027, modernization is entirely chain-agnostic and no federal pilot specifies a public or permissioned ledger: this falsifies M7 as written even if open accounting improves.
  5. Courts, Congress or privacy reviews bar the necessary linkage without a viable public-proof/private-record design.
  6. State conditions are rejected as unclear, unrelated, unauthorized or coercive, forcing the project back to voluntary or federally operated pilots.

9. Ranked next research

Priority 1 — OMB proposed-rule closeout

Recover the docket comments, identify State, tribal, recipient, auditor, technology and privacy objections, then compare the final rule line by line. This is the nearest hard clock.

Priority 2 — EO 14222 implementation census

For each high-spend department, locate the system, payment-justification field, public output, data model, responsible office, procurement, exemptions and pause workflow. The executive order is only as important as its operating objects.

Priority 3 — Fiscal Service authoritative-record map

Map GTAS, ASAP, CARS/GWA, Payment Automation Manager, Do Not Pay and USAspending: which record controls, what transaction identifier passes between them, when a record becomes final, and how a correction propagates.

Priority 4 — Treasury blockchain prototype lineage

Recover the task order, vendor, code, test participants, final report, actual payment evidence, closeout state and any successor procurement. This could move the theory from precedent to lineage—or prove the prototype ended.

Priority 5 — one disaster reconstruction trace

Choose one FEMA Public Assistance or EDA award and trace:

appropriation → award → State receipt → draw → subaward → vendor/provider
→ insurance/duplication offset → correction → recovery → USAspending record

This will show precisely which joins a ledger must solve.

Priority 6 — legal award-condition model

Build model clauses for new discretionary, disaster and formula awards, then test clear notice, program relatedness, administrative burden, reporting frequency, State accounting rules, CMIA timing, subrecipient flow-down, remedies, records access and public disclosure.

Priority 7 — public-proof/private-record specification

Design the minimum privacy architecture: protected PII and pricing remain off-chain; public transaction records contain authority, amount, purpose, timestamps and cryptographic commitments; authorized auditors can open the supporting record; correction and appeal rights are first-class objects.

10. Claim hygiene

Safe:

  • “The federal government is building transaction-level payment-justification, eligibility-screening and machine-readable award controls.”
  • “OMB has proposed, but not finalized, a State pre-disbursement review rule.”
  • “A bounded federal grant-payment justification feed and public API are live; current architecture is centralized.”
  • “Treasury and GAO tested a private permissioned grant ledger; the documented prototype did not affect an actual grant transaction.”
  • “A public or permissioned spending ledger is now a sourced, testable forecast.”

Do not say:

  • “The federal government has mandated blockchain accounting.”
  • “States must send federal funds by blockchain.”
  • “The OMB rule requires a ledger.”
  • “Treasury's prototype entered production.”
  • “Defend the Spend proves that the federal government chose blockchain.”
  • “A blockchain entry proves eligibility, honest performance or absence of fraud.”
  • “Public blockchain requires publishing PII or classified transactions.”
  • “Real-time public accounting and blockchain are the same technical object.”

Bottom line

The project should now watch for the connector instrument, not repeat the general theory:

current policy/control spine
  = payment justification + prepayment screening + electronic rails
    + public transaction feed + award-data standardization
    + State fiscal pressure

missing connector
  = named ledger technology + common transaction ID + award flow-down term
    + authoritative-record rule + correction/privacy constitution

The control spine is no longer speculative. The connector remains the forecast.
