Stop Secret Spending Act — Legal and Implementation Deep Dive

Question

What did the Stop Secret Spending Act actually change, what spending problem does it address, and what remains unimplemented after enactment?

Bottom line

The Stop Secret Spending Act of 2025 is now Public Law 119-106, approved September 11, 2026, at 140 Stat. 1014. It is a federal spending-data and oversight law, not a spending cancellation, OTA ban, declassification law or finding of fraud.

The central move is real: the law adds other transaction agreements to the FFATA definition of a Federal award. It then requires Treasury, working with OMB and agencies, to build automatic OTA data transmission and a centralized USAspending view by September 11, 2029. Interim reports begin in 2027 if the system is not already complete.

The title needs a factual correction. GAO's strongest current measurement was not $77.5 billion that had vanished from every federal record. GAO found about $77.5 billion in Defense Department OTAs recorded in SAM.gov for FY2021–FY2025 but absent from USAspending.gov. The defect was fragmented and inconsistent public reporting in the government's intended central spending site. That is a serious transparency gap; it is not by itself proof that the awards were classified, unlawful, fraudulent or unknown to the government.

What an OTA is

An OTA is a legally binding agreement other than a standard procurement contract, grant or cooperative agreement. Congress has given particular agencies tailored authority to use OTAs, often for research, prototypes, technology development and follow-on production. Their attraction is flexibility: they generally are not governed by the full Federal Acquisition Regulation framework and can be tailored to participants or projects that do not fit ordinary procurement well.

That flexibility is not itself misconduct. It does, however, make the reporting and oversight layer especially important because familiar procurement terms, clauses, competition rules and data flows may not apply in the same way.

The problem Congress addressed

GAO had identified three related defects:

  • FFATA did not expressly include or exclude OTAs, and Treasury and OMB read the reporting obligation differently. Treasury told GAO that OTAs were outside the express Federal-award definition; OMB had not issued OTA-specific guidance and advised agencies to report according to the award type an OTA most closely resembled.
  • Agencies therefore used inconsistent methods. GAO's March 2026 testimony found $77.5 billion in DOD OTAs in SAM.gov for FY2021–FY2025 that were not in USAspending.
  • Spending-data coverage and quality problems were broader than OTAs. GAO's November 2023 review identified 49 agencies that did not report to USAspending, including 25 executive-branch agencies with more than $5 billion in FY2022 net outlays, while responsibility for deciding who must report was unclear. Earlier DATA Act inspector-general review deadlines had also run their course.

The law answers those defects with an express OTA definition, a named Treasury and OMB control layer, recurring agency-list determinations, data-quality duties and a renewed IG cycle.

What Public Law 119-106 does

1. OTAs become Federal awards for FFATA purposes

Section 2(a) amends FFATA's Federal-award definition to include other transaction agreements. No separate delayed effective date is stated for this definition change.

This legal inclusion is not the same thing as a functioning data pipeline. The law separately allows up to three years for automatic transmission and the centralized public view.

2. Treasury must build the OTA reporting path

By September 11, 2029, the Treasury Secretary must ensure that OTA data is automatically transmitted to USAspending (or its successor) and that a centralized view of the data is available there.

The statute does not prescribe every field, schema, system interface or validation rule. Those operational details remain to be built and evidenced.

3. Interim OTA disclosure is required if the final system is not ready

  • If Treasury has not completed the final OTA system by September 11, 2027, it must publish a report on USAspending listing and describing all OTAs entered into by relevant agencies during the preceding fiscal year. OMB determines which agencies possess OTA authority for this purpose.
  • If the final system is still incomplete by September 11, 2028, Treasury must give Congress an implementation plan stating the current status and the actions planned to reach full incorporation by the 2029 deadline.

These are fallback duties, not evidence that Treasury must wait until the deadline to publish OTA data.

4. Treasury must account for spending that remains unreported

Beginning no later than September 11, 2027, and annually thereafter, Treasury, in consultation with OMB, must post a report giving:

  • the total amount of Federal-award spending for which data was not posted; and
  • the reason it was not posted, including whether it involved national-security-related or classified spending, a legislative- or judicial-branch grant or contract, or a subaward below the primary subaward.

This is an accounting for nonpublication. It does not direct the government to declassify protected details. The text requires a total and reasons; it does not promise transaction-level public identification for every excluded award.

5. The law creates a clearer data-quality control chain

Treasury and OMB, consulting agency heads, must establish requirements for complete and accurate information. Agency heads are responsible for the data their agencies or components post. Treasury and OMB may verify that posted data is complete, accurate and consistent, and Treasury sets display standards.

By September 11, 2027, and at least every two years thereafter, Treasury and OMB must determine and publish which federal agencies and components are required to post the covered information, then notify each listed head and inspector general. The amendments that tie section 3(b) reporting duties to that list take effect when the first list is published. The list is therefore an operational legal gate, not clerical housekeeping.

6. Recurring inspector-general review returns

Inspectors general of agencies covered by 31 U.S.C. 901(b)(1) and (2)—the CFO Act agency classes—must submit and publish a first report by September 11, 2027, then report at least once every two years over the statutory ten-year window. The underlying FFATA review uses a statistically valid sample and assesses completeness, timeliness, quality, accuracy, and implementation and use of data standards.

The enacted text contains awkward cadence language. Until an authoritative codified treatment resolves any ambiguity, the safe operational reading is the minimum Congress repeatedly stated: first report within one year and no less frequently than biennially during the ten-year window.

7. GAO must address the FAR subaward-reporting clause

By September 11, 2027, GAO must recommend any advisable updates to FAR 52.204-10 to incorporate FFATA requirements. This is a recommendation duty. It does not itself amend the FAR clause.

What the law does not do

It does not:

  • prohibit, reduce or rescind OTA authority;
  • cancel an award, recover money, make an appropriation or prove a payment;
  • make classified award details public;
  • establish that an unreported award was wasteful, fraudulent or illegal;
  • audit every award rather than a statistically valid IG sample;
  • require publication of every lower-tier detail regardless of legal limits;
  • create a demonstrated recipient-to-payment-to-performance reconciliation;
  • itself amend FAR 52.204-10; or
  • prove that USAspending's OTA schema, automated feed or centralized view is live.

The law makes missing records more visible. It does not decide whether the underlying spending was wise or whether the recipient performed.

  • no Treasury/OMB implementation notice, first agency list, annual unreported- spending report, first IG cycle or GAO recommendation required by this law was located.

The justified state is law enacted; implementation not yet demonstrated. The current endpoint supports the narrower statement that no distinct OTA type or centralized OTA view is exposed there—not the broader claim that no OTA record of any kind appears anywhere in USAspending.

Legislative path

  • March 5, 2025: Senator Joni Ernst introduced companion S. 872 with Senators Gary Peters, James Lankford and Bernie Moreno.
  • March 11, 2025: Representative Barry Moore introduced H.R. 2069 with Representatives Jimmy Panetta and Maggie Goodlander.
  • March 18, 2026: House Oversight and Government Reform favorably reported H.R. 2069 after a committee markup.
  • June 10, 2026: the Senate passed S. 872 by unanimous consent.
  • July 20, 2026: the House passed H.R. 2069 under suspension of the rules by voice vote.
  • August 7, 2026: the Senate passed the House bill by unanimous consent.
  • September 11, 2026: the President signed H.R. 2069.
  • By September 14, 2026: NARA's current-session list identified it as Public Law 119-106, 140 Stat. 1014.

The enacted vehicle is H.R. 2069. The same bipartisan project appeared in the 118th Congress as S. 3926 and H.R. 8690 but did not become law then.

Cost and funding boundary

CBO estimated that the House-reported bill would increase federal administrative costs by less than $500,000 over FY2026–FY2031, subject to appropriation, and that any net direct-spending effect would be negligible. That estimate concerns administration of the reporting law; it is not a measurement of the OTAs newly made visible or savings the law will produce.

The statute supplies duties but no dedicated appropriation. Enactment therefore does not establish that Treasury, OMB, GAO or each IG has received a separate funding increment for implementation.

Why it matters to Freedom 250

This is a clean example of the difference between a legal transparency mandate and an operating public ledger. Congress changed the legal perimeter of the award record immediately, while the data model, source-system bridge, responsibility map, audit cycle and visible public interface remain separately timed.

It also shows why USAspending is not a cash ledger. An OTA record can establish an award or obligation and identify a recipient and purpose; it does not by itself establish settlement, final payment, delivery, acceptance, performance, fraud, recovery or observed public benefit.

Blockchain and smart-contract signal test

The enacted text contains a digital reporting architecture signal, but not a blockchain or smart-contract adoption signal.

The strongest adjacent language requires Treasury to ensure that OTA data is "automatically transmitted" to USAspending and that a "centralized view" is available there. The law also requires complete and accurate information, permits Treasury and OMB to verify that posted data is complete, accurate and consistent, and gives Treasury responsibility for display standards. Those requirements favor structured, interoperable and increasingly automated data flows. They could later supply data to a conventional database, an append-only audit layer, a permissioned ledger or a public-proof system.

They do not, however, select among those architectures. The law does not use the terms blockchain, distributed ledger, smart contract, token, wallet, cryptographic proof, immutable record, consensus, on-chain or real time. It does not require contract terms, payment approvals, performance milestones, acceptance events, Treasury settlement or recovery actions to be encoded as executable rules. "Other transaction agreement" is a federal-award category; the word transaction is not a reference to a blockchain transaction.

The "centralized view" is also a public display requirement, not a command to replace agency source systems or Treasury's payment and accounting systems with one ledger. "Automatically transmitted" can be satisfied by ordinary APIs, scheduled data feeds or other conventional system integrations. The annual unreported-spending report and the one-, two- and three-year implementation deadlines further show that the statute is not a mandate for transaction-level, real-time accounting.

The bounded inference is therefore:

  • Supported: Congress is moving more federal-award data toward automatic, standardized and centrally visible publication, with named accountability for completeness and accuracy.
  • Architecturally compatible but not evidenced: Treasury could later place a cryptographically verifiable or ledger-based layer underneath or alongside that public view.
  • Not supported by this law: government-wide public-blockchain settlement, real-time public accounting, or smart contracts that automatically release, halt, claw back or audit federal payments.

A genuine adoption signal would require a later law, rule, Treasury/OMB system specification, procurement or production pilot that names the ledger or cryptographic mechanism and connects legal authority, award conditions, payment authorization, Treasury settlement, accounting treatment, correction and remedy. A public website or automated feed alone would not cross that gate.
