C. Authority response

F. Six-lane worksheet

Lane Proposed grade One-sentence reason Primary evidence + vintage Persistence evidence Shock evidence Strongest alternative Confidence Legal/event link Data gap
L1 Domestic anchor B The 2 percent anchor and long-run expectations remain credible and activity remains positive, but inflation has persisted moderately above target without yet constituting material credibility failure. BLS June 2026 CPI: −0.4% m/m SA, +3.5% y/y; core 0.0% m/m, +2.6% y/y. The Executive Director statement reported February PCE 2.8% and core PCE 3.0%, while most longer-term expectations remained consistent with 2%; CR26/76 pp. 91–92. CR26/76 Table 1, p. 50: core PCE q4/q4 3.3% in 2023, 3.0% in 2024 and 2.9% in 2025; real GDP growth 2.9%, 2.8% and 2.1%, respectively. The deviation is persistent but bounded, with no disproportionate activity loss. Tariff-related goods inflation and the 2026 energy shock complicate the path. June’s headline fall was energy-led. Section 122 cannot be isolated; its duty-exclusive import-price context is not landed-cost evidence. A is plausible if recent core disinflation and anchored long-term expectations establish that the above-target interval is purely transitory; three consecutive above-target core-PCE years keep the sealed call at B. HIGH Article IV BOARD-CONCERN; TP-US-2026-SECTION122 Current official PCE and expectations vintages after June; duty-inclusive landed-cost/pass-through decomposition; evidence separating Section 122 from energy, FX and other tariff measures.
L2 Underlying order B Banking, funding, payment and settlement functions remain resilient with strong reported capital and liquidity, but fiscal/Treasury-market tail risk and incomplete visibility into NBFIs are material bounded strains. CR26/76 Table 5, p. 54: 2025Q3 regulatory capital/RWA 16.86%, NPLs 0.94% of loans, liquid assets 30.90% of assets; pp. 3–4 and 49 identify elevated valuations, NBFI visibility and supervisory gaps. Table 5 shows capital rising from 15.54% in 2022 to 16.86% in 2025Q3, NPLs staying below 1%, and liquidity near 29–31%; household and corporate leverage measures generally declined. Public debt and short-maturity exposure nevertheless rose persistently. U.S. authorities report that core markets and payment, clearing and settlement systems remained operational through the short April 2025 volatility episode; CR26/76 pp. 91–92. C is plausible if the rising sovereign-debt trajectory, market concentration and opaque leveraged NBFIs are treated as a present systemic impairment; the frozen evidence describes tail vulnerability, not fracture or extraordinary-support dependence. HIGH Article IV BOARD-CONCERN; event link NONE for the primary grade 2026 FSAP findings; current NBFI leverage/liquidity and Treasury-market concentration; current mid-sized-bank and commercial-real-estate stress; updated 2026 FSIs.
L3 Exchange order A The freely floating USD operates without a parallel market, reserve-defense pressure or evidenced exchange-market fracture; ordinary reserve-adequacy tests are inapposite to the global reserve issuer. De jure and de facto free floating; rate determined freely in the FX market, CR26/76 p. 87. Fed broad dollar index rose 2.2% from 117.9463 on 2026-02-24 to 120.5315 on 2026-07-17 without evidence of disorder. The report records free-floating operation and no MCP; its external annex notes more than 10% REER appreciation over five years but identifies no exchange-market dysfunction or unsustainable intervention pressure, p. 68. During the Section 122 window the broad dollar appreciated, but no official source attributes the move to the surcharge or identifies disorder. The CIT/Federal Circuit clocks concern collection authority, not the FX regime. B/C could be argued from the assessed REER overvaluation, but that is an external-adjustment deviation assigned to L4, not exchange-market disorder. MEDIUM Manipulation NO-ADVERSE-FINDING-LOCATED; TP-US-2026-SECTION122 context only Consistent official spot/forward intervention disclosures and high-frequency volatility/liquidity measures through cutoff; attribution of the observed dollar move.
L4 External adjustment C A material, persistent current-account/REER deviation leaves the external position moderately weaker, while reserve-currency funding capacity and a clear fiscal/private-saving adjustment route prevent a D call. Final 2025 ESR assessment of 2024: “moderately weaker,” CA-gap midpoint −1.4% GDP, REER overvaluation midpoint 11.9%, NIIP −89.9% GDP. Separately, the preliminary 2025 Article IV assessment gives CA −3.7%, gap midpoint −1.1%, REER overvaluation midpoint 10%, and NIIP −88.8% at 2025Q3; CR26/76 p. 68. CR26/76 Table 1, p. 50: current account −3.3% GDP in 2023, −4.0% in 2024 and −3.7% in 2025; NIIP −73.4%, −90.6% and −90.4%. The 2024 ESR and preliminary 2025 assessment independently retain the “moderately weaker” classification. BEA 2026Q1 national clock: deficit $226.8bn/2.9% GDP and NIIP −$21.27tn. May trade deficit widened to $77.6bn, while Jan–May remained narrower y/y. These later national data do not update the IMF finding or isolate Section 122. B is plausible because dollar-denominated liabilities, foreign-currency assets, deep markets and reserve status mitigate funding risk, and 2026Q1 CA was below the 2025 annual ratio; valuation effects and one quarter do not erase the persistent assessed gap. HIGH Article IV BOARD-CONCERN; TP-US-2026-SECTION122 Final 2026 ESR; full 2026 current-account components; saving/investment decomposition; revised NIIP valuation effects; a completed pre/post-Section-122 real-trade and causal attribution window.
L5 Current-payment usability A Bona fide current international payments remain usable without an IMF-identified MCP, general exchange restriction, arrears or transfer delay; notified security measures are a bounded legal caveat rather than evidenced operating impairment. Fund Relations as of 2026-02-28: Article VIII §§2/3/4 accepted; system free of MCPs and current-payment/transfer restrictions except security measures; freely determined FX rate, CR26/76 p. 87. The 2026 annex records continuing Article VIII status and refers to the security-notification process, with the last printed notification dated 2024-02-26; no persistent operating arrears or generalized transfer impairment is identified. Section 122 is a customs surcharge and remains legally separate from exchange availability or transfer delay; no current-payment impairment is promoted from the event. B/NR could be argued because the security measures are not itemized and the public packet contains limited transaction-level delay evidence; the official annex is sufficiently direct for a grade and contains no evidence of material impairment. MEDIUM VIII §2 NONE-LOCATED; VIII §3 NONE-LOCATED; VIII §4 NO-FUND-CONCERN-LOCATED; TP-US-2026-SECTION122 firewall only Current AREAER/itemized security-measure scope and approval status; direct official transfer-delay and external-arrears series.
L6 Systemic service A The USD continues to perform its actual reserve, funding, vehicle and hedging roles at dominant scale with deep markets and no observed service failure. IMF COFER: USD 57.13% of world FX reserves in 2026Q1, up from 56.42% in 2025Q4. BIS 2025 Triennial Survey: USD on one side of 89.2% of April 2025 FX trades. CR26/76 p. 68 identifies dominant reserve status, strong institutions and deep, liquid, diverse asset markets. BIS reports the USD FX-turnover share rising from 88.4% in 2022 to 89.2% in 2025. COFER describes the reserve share as broadly stable into 2026Q1. April 2025 volatility caused temporary tightening but core Treasury, funding, payment, clearing and settlement functions remained operational; Section 122 produced no evidenced loss of international monetary service. B is plausible because fiscal tail risk, secular reserve diversification and settlement risk could weaken future service; at the frozen cutoff they are vulnerabilities, not a material impairment of the role. HIGH Article IV BOARD-CONCERN is contextual; TP-US-2026-SECTION122 has no promoted role transmission Comparable longitudinal invoicing, payments and cross-border dollar-credit shares; valuation-adjusted COFER decomposition; current swap-market and FX-settlement-risk measures; evidence of whether fiscal risk is changing user behavior.
