Question
What is an interest rate; what does “the Fed’s interest rate” actually mean; who sets the other rates people see; how does a Federal Reserve decision travel through markets and contracts; and may a private or public lender charge less than a prevailing market rate?
Bottom line
There is no single interest rate and no single rate setter.
Interest is the price attached to the use of money over time. An interest rate is the rule that converts a principal balance and a period of time into interest owed or earned. But a coupon, yield, annual percentage rate, benchmark, policy target and realized return are not interchangeable.
The FOMC sets a target range, not every borrowing rate. The target is for the overnight federal funds rate. The effective federal funds rate is then calculated from actual reported transactions; it is an observed market rate, not the number voted into existence.
The Federal Reserve implements the target with several separate administered rates and balance-sheet operations. The Board sets interest on reserve balances; the FOMC directs the New York Fed’s Desk on overnight reverse-repo, standing-repo and securities operations; and Reserve Bank directors establish discount-window rates subject to Board review and determination.
Other rates have their own setters. Treasury auction yields are produced by accepted bids. SOFR is calculated from Treasury-repo transactions. Banks set their own prime and customer rates. Bond yields are produced by price trading. Mortgage, auto, business and credit-card rates are contractual lender offers built from a reference or funding baseline plus term, credit, liquidity, capital, expense, optionality and profit components.
The Fed influences this structure strongly but incompletely. Its greatest direct control is near overnight money. Longer rates reflect the expected future path of short rates, expected inflation and term/risk premiums. A long rate can move before an FOMC meeting, fail to move after it, or move in the opposite direction.
Charging below “market” is generally possible, because “market rate” is not a universal legal minimum. Zero-percent promotions, family loans, employee loans, relationship pricing and public credit subsidies all exist. The consequences depend on the lane: tax law may impute interest; bank-insider rules may prohibit preferential terms; fair-lending law may prohibit discriminatory pricing; bank supervisors may question unsafe pricing; and public lenders need program authority and budget treatment for subsidy costs. Usury rules ordinarily cap rates from above rather than establish a floor.
Credit unions require their own lane. Returning cooperative value to members through lower loan rates is part of the credit-union model, not automatically an improper below-market transfer. But federal and state charters, field-of-membership rules, official-preference restrictions, fair lending, tax characterization and safety-and-soundness still control the particular loan.
The clean research rule is:
Never record “the interest rate” without naming the instrument, actor, principal, term, compounding convention, security/collateral, reset rule, fees, observation time and legal purpose.
1. What an interest rate actually is
At its simplest:
interest = principal × rate × time
That simple-interest formula is only a starting point. Real instruments may compound daily or monthly, amortize principal, reset from an index, capitalize unpaid interest, include points and fees, or trade at a price different from face value.

For Treasury notes and bonds, the distinction is visible. Treasury sets the security’s coupon from the auction result, but once the security trades, its market price changes. A lower price produces a higher yield for the same promised cash flows; a higher price produces a lower yield. The old coupon remains fixed while the current yield changes.

The current live example
The September 16, 2026 FOMC statement raised the federal funds target range by 0.25 percentage point to 3.75%–4.00%, effective September 17. The separate implementation note recorded these operating decisions:
IORB: 3.90%;
standing overnight repo: 4.00%;
overnight reverse repo: 3.75%;
primary credit: 4.00%; and
Desk operations as needed to maintain the federal funds rate inside the target range and reserves at an ample level.
Those are five related settings or instructions, not five names for one rate. The EFFR for the new regime will be calculated from qualifying transactions after the settings take effect.
This also shows the separate institutional clocks:
FOMC vote and announcement → effective date → Board/Desk/Reserve-Bank implementation → overnight transactions → published reference rate → private repricing → contract reset/payment
The announcement is not the trade; the trade is not the customer quote; the quote is not a signed contract; the contract rate is not cash paid until the payment clock arrives.
3. How the FOMC chooses its target
Congress has instructed the Federal Reserve to pursue maximum employment, stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates. The FOMC uses economic data and forecasts, risk judgments and its monetary-policy strategy to choose a stance it considers appropriate. It usually meets eight times a year, announces its decision and vote, and publishes an implementation note. Meeting participants may also publish projections of what each considers an appropriate future policy path.
Three boundaries matter:
The target is a policy judgment, not a formula result. Inflation, employment, output, financial conditions and risks inform the vote; none mechanically dictates it.
The Summary of Economic Projections is not a Committee promise. Individual participants’ appropriate-policy projections are conditional assessments, not an adopted future rate schedule.
The target decision and implementation are related but legally distinct. The FOMC sets the target and directs Desk operations. The Board sets IORB and acts on discount rates. Reserve Bank directors have the statutory establishment role for discount rates, subject to the Board.
4. How a target becomes an observed overnight rate
The current framework operates with ample reserve balances. In simplified form:
Banks and other eligible institutions hold reserve balances at Reserve Banks.
The Board offers IORB on eligible reserve balances. An eligible bank generally has little reason to lend overnight materially below what it can earn risk-free at the Fed, although balance-sheet costs, counterparty access and market segmentation prevent perfect arbitrage.
Eligible money-market counterparties that cannot earn IORB may have access to ON RRP, placing another administered return beneath parts of the money market.
The standing repo facility and discount window can supply liquidity against collateral, limiting pressure at the upper side without guaranteeing that every private rate stays below a number.
The Desk changes reserve supply or conducts operations under the FOMC directive as needed.
Institutions transact. The New York Fed calculates and publishes EFFR from the specified reported trades.
The Fed therefore steers a market outcome using administered alternatives, access rules, collateralized backstops and balance-sheet operations. It does not type the next day’s EFFR directly into existence.
5. Who sets the rates outside the Fed
Overnight benchmarks
SOFR is a broad measure of the cost of borrowing cash overnight against Treasury securities. The New York Fed calculates it from qualifying repo transaction data. It is secured; federal funds are unsecured. The two rates can be close but need not be identical because the collateral, participants and transaction sets differ.
A contract may say, for example, SOFR + 2.00 percentage points. The New York Fed administers the benchmark; the lender and borrower set the margin and other contract terms; the contract determines the reset and payment dates.
Treasury rates
Treasury decides what securities to offer, their terms and offering amount. Competitive bidders specify the return they require. Treasury accepts competitive bids from the lowest yield/rate upward until the offering is placed, and successful bidders receive the single auction price produced by the highest accepted rate, yield or discount margin.
After issuance, holders trade the security in the secondary market. Buyers and sellers then set the price; the price implies the market yield. Treasury did not administratively decree that secondary-market yield.
Prime
Prime is set by individual banks. The Federal Reserve’s H.15 release reports the rate posted by a majority of the 25 largest insured U.S.-chartered commercial banks. Many banks link prime partly to the FOMC target, but the Fed does not set prime. Prime is itself often only a base: a customer may pay prime plus or minus a contractual spread.
Customer loan and deposit rates
The lender or deposit-taking institution sets an offered rate subject to law, program rules, risk governance and competition. A useful conceptual stack for a loan is:
reference/opportunity cost + expected credit loss + term and liquidity premium + capital and operating cost + optionality + profit margin − subsidy/relationship value
Not every lender literally calculates one additive formula, but the stack identifies the economic jobs hidden inside the quote.
Reference/opportunity cost: What could the institution earn or pay elsewhere over the relevant horizon?
Expected credit loss: What probability and severity of nonpayment does underwriting assign?
Term and liquidity: How long is money committed, and how easily can the asset be sold or funded?
Capital and operating cost: What capital, liquidity, servicing, compliance and acquisition resources does the exposure consume?
Optionality: Can the borrower prepay when rates fall, draw at will, or extend the exposure?
Profit/relationship value: What return does the lender require, and what deposits, fees, product sales or strategic value offset it?
Subsidy or guarantee: Is a seller, employer, government or guarantor absorbing some cost or risk?
Mortgage pricing therefore responds to expected policy, Treasury and mortgage-backed-security markets, borrower credit, down payment/loan-to-value, loan type, term, points, prepayment risk, guarantee/insurance and lender competition. A credit-card rate may reset from prime plus a margin. An auto manufacturer may subsidize a zero-percent loan to sell a vehicle. A deposit rate reflects the bank’s funding need and competitive strategy, not simply IORB passed through to a depositor.
6. Why other rates do not move one-for-one with the Fed
The transmission chain is not a wire. It is a set of repricing decisions.
Short rates
Overnight and short-maturity market rates usually track the expected path of FOMC settings relatively closely because alternatives can be compared and arbitraged over short horizons. Even here, access, collateral, balance-sheet costs, reserve supply and market segmentation matter.
Long rates
A useful decomposition is:
long nominal yield ≈ expected average future short real rates + expected inflation + term/risk premium
That means:
a future rate change can be priced before the meeting;
a current hike can coincide with a falling long yield if markets infer weaker future growth or fewer future hikes;
a current cut can coincide with a rising long yield if inflation or fiscal-risk expectations rise;
Treasury supply, dealer capacity, hedging demand, foreign demand, liquidity and risk appetite can move term premiums independently of the current target; and
borrower rates can diverge further because credit spreads, collateral values, guarantees and lender margins change.
The accurate claim is “Fed policy influences the rate structure.” The inaccurate shortcut is “the Fed sets interest rates.”
7. Is a lender allowed to charge below market?
General private-law answer
Usually, yes. A willing lender can commonly offer a low or zero rate, forgive interest, accept a lower return for a relationship, or receive compensation from a third party. “Market” is evidence of an arm’s-length price, not ordinarily a universal statutory minimum.
But below-market pricing can change the legal or accounting identity of the transfer. The real question is not only “is the note enforceable?” It is also “who supplied the economic benefit, why, and how must it be recorded?”
Credit unions: lower rates can be the mission, but the member and preference rules still matter
Credit unions are not-for-profit, member-owned cooperatives. NCUA describes lower loan rates, higher savings rates and reduced fees as ways a credit union may return economic value to its members. A credit-union rate below a bank average therefore does not by itself establish a subsidy, loss, improper preference or non-arm’s-length transaction. It may be the ordinary product of cooperative ownership, a different funding mix, lower required profit, a different borrower pool or a deliberate allocation of member benefit.

The Federal Credit Union Act makes the institutional geometry unusually visible. It authorizes an FCU to lend to members under criteria established by its board, places a ceiling on interest inclusive of finance charges, and permits NCUA to raise that ceiling temporarily when money-market rates and credit-union safety-and-soundness conditions satisfy the statutory test. NCUA’s 2026 analysis explicitly tied the ceiling decision to liquidity, capital, earnings and growth. The ceiling is designed to stop excessive rates while allowing the cooperative to remain viable; it is not a benchmark of the “correct” member price.
NCUA’s separate earnings guidance supplies the other half. A credit union must generate enough revenue to cover service costs and maintain safe and sound net worth, but it need not maximize return for outside stockholders. Officials balance current member benefit—including lower loan rates—against retained earnings needed for future service, capital and resilience. A low loan rate can therefore be a lawful allocation of cooperative surplus, while a portfolio priced too low for its losses, funding and capital needs can still become a supervisory problem.
For comparison work, do not ask whether the credit union is simply “below banks.” Ask whether the compared products have the same:
borrower credit and income profile;
collateral and loan-to-value ratio;
term, amortization and prepayment option;
guarantee or insurance;
fees, points and required memberships/accounts;
fixed or adjustable reset rule;
geography and observation time; and
loss, capital, liquidity and servicing responsibilities.
The clean credit-union sequence is:
charter and membership → board product/risk policy → lawful ceiling and terms → member underwriting → comparable-member/official test → fair-lending and tax treatment → funding/capital sufficiency → contract → servicing and loss performance
Public-depository eligibility, federal share insurance or tax-exempt status does not by itself authorize a particular below-market loan or prove who bore the discount. A government-insured or guaranteed member loan may instead follow the rate and term authority of its own program; that is a separate government-credit lane.
Tax: the Applicable Federal Rate is a statutory proxy, not the Fed target
Internal Revenue Code §7872 applies to specified below-market loans, including gift, compensation-related, corporation-shareholder and tax-avoidance loans. In broad terms, it may treat the lender as transferring the forgone interest to the borrower and the borrower as retransferring it to the lender as interest. The first deemed transfer may be a gift, compensation, dividend/distribution or another object depending on the relationship.
The tax benchmark is the Applicable Federal Rate (AFR):
short-term for terms not over three years;
mid-term for terms over three and not over nine years; and
long-term for terms over nine years.
Treasury/IRS determines AFRs monthly from market yields on outstanding U.S. obligations. AFR is therefore not the federal funds target, EFFR, IORB, SOFR, prime or a universal fair-market lending rate.
IRS guidance also recognizes exceptions where the arrangement lacks significant federal tax effect, including loans made to the general public on customary business terms and qualifying government-subsidized loans made under a generally available public program. The exact exception and amount rules still require tax-specific review.
Bank insiders: preferential terms are restricted
Federal Reserve Regulation O generally bars a member bank from lending to its executive officers, directors, principal shareholders and covered related interests unless the credit is on substantially the same terms—including rate and collateral—and follows underwriting no less stringent than comparable transactions with non-insiders. A widely available employee benefit or compensation program may qualify for the regulation’s stated exception if it does not prefer insiders over other employees.
This is a direct example of a “below market” rate becoming impermissible because of who receives it and why, not because low rates are generally banned.
Fair lending: differences need lawful reasons
ECOA and Regulation B prohibit discrimination in credit transactions on protected grounds. Lenders may use lawful credit, income, debt, collateral and other risk factors, but may not charge a higher price because of a protected characteristic. A low rate for one person is therefore not automatically illegal; unexplained or protected-class pricing differences can be.
Special-purpose credit programs and other authorized programs can intentionally extend favorable terms to a defined eligible class, but the program must fit its governing legal route.
Safety and soundness: permission is not prudence
Banking law does not generally establish a single minimum profitable rate for ordinary borrowers. Supervisors do, however, examine underwriting, asset quality, interest-rate risk, concentration, capital, liquidity, expected credit losses, internal controls and insider transactions. A bank can legally make a low-rate loan yet face criticism if pricing does not recognize risk, creates unsafe concentrations, disguises losses or transfers value improperly.
Public credit: below-market terms are an authorized subsidy, not free money
Federal direct loans and guarantees can intentionally make credit cheaper or more available than private credit. OMB Circular A-129 says direct loans usually offer lower rates and longer maturities than private sources and instructs agencies to design interest and fee structures around program goals, risk and subsidy cost. Under the Federal Credit Reform framework, agencies estimate the present-value subsidy cost and need budget authority before obligating covered direct loans or committing guarantees.
The public process is therefore:
program authority → eligible class and purpose → rate/fee formula → subsidy-cost estimate → appropriation or other budget authority → underwriting/obligation → disbursement → servicing/performance
A policy announcement is not rate authority; authority is not an appropriation; an appropriation is not a loan commitment; a commitment is not disbursement; and a low contractual rate is not evidence that the program achieved its policy result.
Usury and disclosure: mostly ceilings and truth rules
State usury laws and federal bank rules commonly govern the maximum permissible interest rate, not a minimum. Which ceiling applies can depend on lender charter, location, borrower, product and federal preemption. National-bank rules under 12 U.S.C. §85 are one example of a federal/state maximum-rate framework.
Truth in Lending and Regulation Z govern how covered consumer credit costs and APRs are disclosed and advertised. A genuine zero-percent promotion can be lawful. The problem arises when conditions, deferred interest, fees or later rates are obscured or inaccurately advertised.

9. How to inspect any rate without getting fooled
For every rate claim, capture these fields:
Name and instrument: federal funds, SOFR repo, Treasury note, mortgage, card balance, tax AFR or something else?
Actor: FOMC, Board, Reserve Bank directors, New York Fed administrator/Desk, Treasury, market participants, lender, program agency, contract parties?
Rate function: target, administered offer, observed benchmark, coupon, yield, note rate, APR, discount rate, penalty rate or tax proxy?
Principal/claim: reserve balance, unsecured overnight funds, collateralized cash, Treasury debt, bank deposit or customer loan?
Term and reset: overnight, fixed term, demand, adjustable, floating; when and how does it change?
Security and priority: unsecured, Treasury collateral, mortgage lien, guarantee, insurance or subordinated claim?
Compounding/day count: simple, daily, monthly, semiannual; 360- or 365-day convention?
Fees and points: what is excluded from the note rate but included in APR or total cost?
Clock: announced, effective, observed, published, quoted, locked, contracted, accrued or paid?
Comparison set: which transactions make the asserted “market,” and are they truly comparable?
Institutional form and eligibility: bank, federal credit union, state credit union, finance company, government program or private person; public customer, eligible member, employee, official or related party?
Legal purpose: monetary implementation, cooperative member benefit, contract pricing, consumer disclosure, tax imputation, usury, insider control or public subsidy?
Evidence: controlling statute/rule/contract and the actual transaction or payment record.
10. Practical answers to the original questions
What is the difference between the Fed’s rate and other rates?
The Fed’s headline rate is an FOMC target range for one overnight market. Other rates are administered facilities, transaction benchmarks, auction results, traded yields or private contract prices. The target supplies a powerful short-term anchor; it is not the full price.
How are rates set?
By different mechanisms:
policy vote;
administrative rate action;
transaction-based benchmark calculation;
auction;
secondary-market trading;
lender pricing and underwriting;
negotiated contract; or
statutory/program formula.
The word “set” should always be replaced with the actual verb: voted, offered, calculated, bid, traded, quoted, negotiated, indexed or prescribed.
Can a lender charge less than market?
Generally yes, but the discount must be assigned to its real economic and legal source. It may be an ordinary competitive price, promotion, gift, compensation, insider preference, cross-subsidy or public subsidy. Each identity has different tax, governance, disclosure, fair-lending, prudential and budget consequences.
Does a Fed hike make all borrowing rates rise immediately?
No. Overnight rates usually respond most directly. Prime often changes quickly because banks choose to link it. Adjustable contracts move only on their reset clocks. Fixed contracts do not change. Long-market and mortgage rates reflect expected future policy, inflation, term premiums, credit and market structure and may already have moved—or may move the other way.
