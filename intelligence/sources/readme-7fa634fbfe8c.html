DOJ Judicial Misconduct Complaint Against Minnesota Judges — September 30, 2026

Research ID: doj-minnesota-judicial-misconduct-complaint-2026-09-30

Status: current Workbench research; not a judicial finding, recusal order, case disposition or Chronicle conclusion.

Evidence cutoff: September 30, 2026. Public Eighth Circuit records were checked after the filing was announced; no public order on this complaint had appeared at that cutoff.

Bottom line

The Department of Justice filed an administrative judicial-conduct complaint with the Eighth Circuit against Chief Judge Patrick J. Schiltz, Judge John R. Tunheim and five unnamed District of Minnesota judges. The complaint says comments attributed to the judges in a September 17 New York Times article improperly addressed pending or impending immigration matters and created an appearance of partiality. DOJ asks for an investigation and for the judges to recuse from matters involving the Department of Homeland Security.

The filing itself does none of those things. It does not vacate a ruling, stay a case, remove a judge, create a recusal order or establish misconduct. Chief Judge Steven Colloton must first conduct the Rule 11 review. He may dismiss or conclude the complaint, conduct a limited inquiry, or appoint a special committee. A Judicial Council can impose certain discipline after the statutory process, but it cannot remove an Article III judge.

My read is split:

  • The narrow public-comment claim is non-frivolous and better documented than DOJ's 2025 complaints against Judges Ana Reyes and James Boasberg. The statements here were public, attributed and attached to an article while at least one cited litigation chain remained live.
  • DOJ's requested blanket DHS recusal is procedurally vulnerable. The D.C. Circuit's 2025 Reyes order held that a party ordinarily must pursue recusal under 28 U.S.C. § 455 in the case itself and cannot use a misconduct complaint to sidestep that route.
  • DOJ leans heavily on White v. NFL, but that Eighth Circuit decision affirmed denial of recusal and found no Canon 3A(6) violation. Its caution about judges speaking to the press helps DOJ; its holding does not.
  • Large portions of the complaint argue that the judges' rulings were wrong or were later reversed. Rule 4(b)(1) excludes merits-related attacks from the conduct process. Those passages cannot do the work of the narrower speech allegation.
  • The five unnamed judges are not tied to individualized quotations in the filed complaint. Rule 6 requires concise, specific and checkable facts. That makes the anonymous-respondent portion substantially thinner than the allegations against Schiltz and Tunheim.
  • The attached complaint is six numbered pages, while Eighth Circuit Rule 6A says the statement of facts must not exceed five pages. The clerk nevertheless stamped the submission received. The overlength observation is a technical filing issue, not a merits ruling; the legal argument may also be treated separately from the factual statement.

What DOJ alleges

DOJ relies on 28 U.S.C. § 351(a), Canon 2A, Canon 3A(6), Advisory Opinion 55 and Judicial-Conduct Rule 4. It quotes or characterizes Chief Judge Schiltz as describing noncompliance with court orders, the government's litigation conduct, strain on the courthouse and threats to the rule of law. It quotes Judge Tunheim as saying that agents were not, in his view, arresting dangerous criminals on the street. DOJ says those comments overlap with pending or impending immigration litigation and reasonably call impartiality into question.

The complaint cites appellate matters including Velasquez, Anaya and Avila, plus district-court matters including United States v. Levy Armstrong, Ali v. Blanche and Tincher v. Noem. The Supreme Court docket for Avila was open at the cutoff, with a response due October 19, 2026.

Governing process

  1. Under Rule 11, the chief judge reviews the complaint and may make a limited inquiry. The inquiry may collect documents or interview people but may not decide reasonably disputed facts.
  2. The chief judge may dismiss a non-cognizable, merits-related, unsupported or otherwise defective complaint; conclude it after corrective action or an intervening event; or appoint a special committee.
  3. If a special committee is appointed, it investigates and reports to the Judicial Council. Rule 20 and 28 U.S.C. § 354 allow measures such as censure, reprimand or temporary non-assignment of new cases, subject to the statutory limits.
  4. A complainant may seek Judicial Council review of a chief-judge disposition under Rule 18. Final orders become public under Rule 24 after the review process; early-stage materials are generally confidential under Rule 23.

The Eighth Circuit's public orders page did not show an order for this complaint at the cutoff. That is not evidence that it was not docketed: the governing rules ordinarily make final orders public after review, not an immediate public complaint docket.

Precedent that matters

White v. NFL, 585 F.3d 1129 (8th Cir. 2009)

The panel included then-Judge Steven Colloton, now the chief judge receiving this complaint. The court affirmed denial of recusal. It held that the challenged public comments concerned historical and resolved matters, did not address the merits of the pending controversy and did not create a reasonable perception of bias. The opinion nevertheless warned that even seemingly innocuous press comments can create danger and that judges would be well advised not to opine on pending or impending litigation.

This makes White a two-edged authority. DOJ's best distinction is that the Minnesota comments allegedly describe conduct and disputes still moving through live appellate channels. The judges' best answer is that the article concerned court administration and institutional conditions, not the merits of a particular pending controversy.

2025 DOJ complaints against Judges Reyes and Boasberg

Chief Judge Sri Srinivasan dismissed DOJ's complaint against Judge Ana Reyes. His order held that when a litigant questions impartiality in a pending case, the ordinary route is a recusal motion under § 455 and appellate or mandamus review—not using the conduct process to obtain reassignment.

Chief Judge Jeffrey Sutton dismissed DOJ's complaint against Judge James Boasberg after transfer to the Sixth Circuit. The public-comment allegation lacked the cited attachment and concerned a private, non-case-specific remark; attacks on rulings were merits-related; and case reassignment belonged to ordinary recusal and appellate processes.

The current complaint is stronger than those two on proof of a public statement. It remains exposed to the same boundary wherever it seeks blanket recusal or repackages disagreement with rulings as misconduct.

Evidence limits

  • The file-stamped DOJ complaint and official court rules control the filing and procedure descriptions.
  • The full New York Times article could not be retrieved in this pass because direct access was blocked. The complaint supplies excerpts, and public metadata/snippets indicate that Schiltz declined to discuss ongoing cases. The complete article context remains an open evidence item.
  • No response from the named or unnamed judges, chief-judge order, docket number or special-committee order was located at the cutoff.
  • The complaint's quotations are allegations and selections by an interested litigant. They are not findings about context, intent, accuracy or prejudicial effect.
