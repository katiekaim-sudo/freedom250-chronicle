# Federal Reserve congressional change history — what a century of revision tells us

**Question:** Has Congress ever changed the Federal Reserve or its monetary role; what do those changes tell us about who controls the architecture; what do the Reserve Bank dissolution and forfeiture clauses mean; and what exactly is the statutory “dollar” to which a private dollar-denominated claim is tied?

**Scope:** U.S. federal law and institutional history from 1913 through September 20, 2026. This is a high-signal change history, not an inventory of every amendment to the Federal Reserve Act.

## Bottom line

Yes. The Federal Reserve of 2026 is not the institution Congress created in 1913.

Congress has repeatedly changed:

- how long the twelve Reserve Banks exist;
- whether regional Reserve Banks or the Washington Board dominate monetary policy;
- who sits on the central governing body;
- what emergency borrowers the Reserve Banks may reach;
- whether Federal Reserve notes carry a statutory gold reserve;
- which denominations may be issued as Federal Reserve notes;
- the macroeconomic objectives the Fed must pursue;
- which depository institutions face reserve rules and receive Fed services;
- whether the Fed must price payment services and consider competition;
- whether Reserve Banks may pay interest on reserve balances;
- how emergency lending, systemic utilities and disclosure are governed; and
- how private stablecoins and a possible retail central-bank digital currency are treated.

The history therefore rejects two opposite myths. The Fed is **not immutable or constitutionally independent of Congress**. But Congress also has not treated monetary architecture as a light switch: major changes usually preserve continuity while reallocating powers, liabilities, tools, oversight and transition duties.

Federal Reserve Act §31 makes the legal hierarchy unusually plain: “The right to amend, alter, or repeal this Act is hereby expressly reserved.” [Current compiled Federal Reserve Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/COMPS-270/pdf/COMPS-270.pdf#page=337)

## 1. The visible change arc

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[1913<br/>Create 12-Bank system<br/>and elastic currency] --> B[1927<br/>Reserve Banks made<br/>perpetual unless terminated]
    B --> C[1932–35<br/>Emergency lending expands;<br/>power centralizes in Board/FOMC]
    C --> D[1934–68<br/>Gold regime and note<br/>architecture rewritten]
    D --> E[1951<br/>Treasury–Fed Accord<br/>restores policy independence]
    E --> F[1977–80<br/>Mandate, reporting,<br/>universal reach and priced services]
    F --> G[2006–10<br/>Interest on reserves;<br/>crisis powers expanded then constrained]
    G --> H[2025–26<br/>Private stablecoin framework;<br/>retail-CBDC authority withheld]
```

The line is not a steady march toward either more or less Fed power. Congress has sometimes expanded Fed authority and later narrowed it; sometimes centralized authority in Washington and sometimes preserved regional implementation; sometimes insulated monetary policy and sometimes increased reporting and Treasury consent.

## 2. Full institutional change spine

“Full” here means every major change that altered the **public monetary constitution**—the issuer, governing body, policy objective, monetary instrument, currency backing, emergency-credit boundary, payment role, supervisory perimeter or accountability system. It does not mean every technical amendment, commemorative-coin law, personnel change or adjustment to an implementing regulation.

The **type** column matters. An Act of Congress can change legal authority. An executive action, court decision, FOMC policy or interagency agreement can profoundly change how authority operates, but cannot by itself amend the Federal Reserve Act.

### C. The recurring sequence

Across the full spine, the same five-step pattern appears repeatedly:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    S[Shock, gap or technology change] --> E[Emergency or improvised response]
    E --> C[Congress codifies, enlarges<br/>or constrains authority]
    C --> I[Board, FOMC, Treasury and<br/>Reserve Banks implement]
    I --> R[Later Congress repairs<br/>loopholes and consequences]
    R -. new shock .-> S
```

Examples include the 1907 panic leading to the 1908 commission and 1913 Act; Depression improvisation leading to the 1933–35 rewrite; wartime rate support leading to the 1951 Accord; membership erosion leading to the 1980 Monetary Control Act; 2008 emergency programs leading to Dodd–Frank's 2010 limits; and private digital-dollar growth leading to the separate 2025 stablecoin framework and 2026 retail-CBDC boundary.

## 3. The two Reserve Bank termination routes

Federal Reserve Act §4, codified at 12 USC §341, says each Reserve Bank has succession until it is **“dissolved by Act of Congress”** or its franchise is **forfeited for violation of law**. These are two different routes.

### A. Dissolution by Congress

This is a legislative route. Congress could enact a law ending one Reserve Bank, combining districts, replacing all twelve entities, or winding up the System. Section 31 expressly reserves Congress's power to amend, alter or repeal the Act.

The clause is not an automatic twenty-year sunset. Congress removed that expiration in 1927. Nor does “perpetual” mean Congress promised never to change the Banks. It means corporate succession continues without a fixed end date unless one of the statutory termination conditions occurs.

A dissolution statute would need to do much more than say “the bank is abolished.” It would have to allocate or discharge:

- Federal Reserve notes and other liabilities;
- reserve and Treasury accounts;
- assets, collateral and contracts;
- payment, settlement and cash operations;
- employees, property, records and pending litigation; and
- the successor authority for monetary and emergency-liquidity operations.

No Act dissolving one of the twelve operating Reserve Bank corporations was located in the official history reviewed for this answer.

### B. Forfeiture for violation of law

This is an enforcement route, not a shareholder vote and not a general political recall. Section 11(h), 12 USC §248(h), authorizes the Board, for a Reserve Bank's violation of the Federal Reserve Act, to suspend its operations, take possession, administer it during suspension and, when advisable, liquidate or reorganize it. The Board's official compilation says this language was part of the original Act and has never been amended. [Federal Reserve Act §11(h)](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section11.htm)

The §4 “forfeiture” phrase is terse. A 1969 Board counsel study observed that the Act did not separately specify a forfeiture procedure and treated §11(h)'s suspension/liquidation/reorganization power as the operative companion provision. [Board counsel recommendations, 1969](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/historical/frsbog/196901_hackley_FRActchanges_Part1.pdf#page=48)

No official record was located showing that the Board has ever used §11(h) to suspend, liquidate or reorganize one of the twelve Reserve Banks. That is a bounded source finding, not proof that no internal consideration has ever occurred. Ordinary examinations, supervision, removal of officers or directors, and correction of operations are distinct from franchise forfeiture.

## 4. What 31 USC §5101 actually defines

Section 5101 is titled **“Decimal system.”** It says United States money is expressed in dollars, dimes or tenths, cents or hundredths, and mills or thousandths. It defines the national **unit of account and its decimal subdivisions**:

- 1 dollar = 10 dimes;
- 1 dollar = 100 cents; and
- 1 dollar = 1,000 mills.

The official codification note explains that “money of account” meant the monetary system used for keeping financial accounts. It specifically says that phrase did not, by itself, require dollars or fractions of dollars to equal something with intrinsic or substantive value. [31 USC §5101 and codification notes](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2023&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-2023-title31-section5101)

That means three ideas must be kept separate:

1. **Nominal unit:** the legal counting unit in which prices, taxes, debts, accounts and instruments are stated.
2. **Redemption definition:** what, if anything, the issuer must deliver for a dollar claim. Historically this included fixed metal relationships; modern Federal Reserve notes are not generally redeemable in gold.
3. **Purchasing or exchange value:** what one dollar buys in goods, services or foreign currency. Section 5101 does not freeze this value.

The metal definition has changed before. The Coinage Act of 1792 specified the silver content of the “dollar or unit.” The Gold Standard Act of 1900 used a gold-dollar standard. Congress and the executive then dismantled the domestic gold-redemption system in the 1930s; Congress removed the last statutory gold-cover requirement for Federal Reserve notes in 1968; and the United States ended external official gold convertibility in 1971. The dollar unit survived each change. [Coinage Act of 1792](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-1/pdf/STATUTE-1-Pg246-3.pdf), [Gold Reserve Act history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/gold-reserve-act), and [Public Law 90-269](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-82/pdf/STATUTE-82-Pg50.pdf)

## 5. Is a private “$1” claim tied to a unit of value or to a Fed note?

The precise answer is: **first to the public dollar unit, then through a particular redemption promise to one or more accepted dollar settlement assets.** It is not necessarily tied to one physical Federal Reserve note.

For a payment stablecoin under the GENIUS Act:

- the token is designed to maintain a stable value relative to a fixed amount of “monetary value”;
- the issuer is obligated to convert, redeem or repurchase it for that fixed amount;
- “national currency” includes Federal Reserve notes and money standing to the credit of an account at a Federal Reserve Bank;
- permitted reserves can include coins and currency, reserve-account balances, bank deposits and short-term Treasury obligations; and
- the stablecoin itself is excluded from the statutory definition of national currency and is not automatically legal tender, federally insured or backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.

So a token labeled **$1** uses the sovereign dollar as its measurement standard, but the holder's direct claim is against the private issuer. Redemption may arrive as a bank deposit credit or another recognized dollar asset—not necessarily as a paper note. The issuer's reserves and access chain connect the promise to public and regulated dollar assets. If confidence in that promise, reserve quality or redemption mechanism fails, the token can trade above or below one dollar even though the statutory dollar unit has not changed.

The same logic applies to a bank-deposit dollar. A $1 deposit and a $1 Federal Reserve note share a unit of account and normally exchange at par, but they are liabilities of different issuers and have different legal protections and settlement paths.

## 6. What the history tells us about possible future change

### Congress can change the issuer architecture by ordinary legislation

No constitutional amendment is inherently required to reorganize the Fed, move note issuance, authorize broader Treasury notes, change the FOMC, add or remove monetary tools, alter legal tender, or authorize a digital form of public money. Congress would amend the Federal Reserve Act, Title 31 and related banking, payment, fiscal and transition statutes.

### Independence is designed, not absolute

The 1935 Act increased institutional insulation from Treasury in some respects. The 1951 Accord restored policy independence in practice. Yet Congress later specified the policy objectives, required testimony and disclosures, conditioned emergency lending on Treasury approval and can amend the Act again. Independence means protected operational decision-making within law—not sovereignty over the law.

### “Fed power” is not one dial

History shows at least six separate dials:

1. **corporate existence** of the Reserve Banks;
2. **governance** between Board, FOMC, Reserve Banks, Treasury and Congress;
3. **monetary objectives**;
4. **implementation tools** such as open-market operations, discount lending and interest on reserves;
5. **currency and account liabilities**; and
6. **payment, supervision and systemic-risk powers**.

Congress can turn one dial up while turning another down. Dodd–Frank is the cleanest example: it narrowed institution-specific emergency lending, expanded disclosure and Treasury consent, and simultaneously added new systemic-utility oversight.

### A real replacement law must preserve or replace functions

Changing the name of the issuer is not enough. Any proposal to have the United States “issue its own dollar” should identify, separately, who would:

- define the unit and legal tender;
- carry the currency or account liability;
- decide the quantity of central-bank money;
- supply intraday and emergency liquidity;
- settle bank-to-bank obligations finally;
- hold Treasury's account and act as fiscal agent;
- manage outstanding notes, assets and contracts; and
- absorb losses and maintain continuity during transition.

The United States already supplies the sovereign unit, public coins, Federal Reserve notes and central-bank balances through a divided statutory architecture. The reform question is not whether the dollar becomes “American”; it already is. The question is **which public institution receives which decision right, liability, asset and operating duty—and under what safeguards.**
