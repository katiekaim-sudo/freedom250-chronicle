The FSB is the bridge between the post-2008 rulebook and the next financial system

Bottom line

The Financial Stability Board is not merely a crypto standard setter. It is the international join that tries to keep banking, markets, clearing, non-banks, resolution and new technology inside one financial-stability frame.

The through-line is:

2008 crisis fault line
  → G20 priority
    → FSB cross-sector coordination
      → BCBS / CPMI-IOSCO / IOSCO / IAIS / FATF standards
        → national law, rules and supervision
          → firm and infrastructure operation
            → peer review, stress evidence and measured effect

Crypto, stablecoins and tokenisation sit at the right-hand edge of this older system. The FSB asks whether they reproduce, shift or concentrate the same risks that Basel III, derivatives reform, resolution and NBFI policy were built to control.

What the FSB is

The G20 re-launched the Financial Stability Forum as the FSB in April 2009 and endorsed its first Charter that September. The 2012 Charter strengthened its standard-setting and implementation role. In 2013 it became a Swiss non-profit association seated in Basel, with its Secretariat hosted by the Bank for International Settlements.

That legal form does not make it a world regulator. The FSB's own publications state that its decisions are non-binding and create no legal rights or obligations. Its power comes from membership, coordination, consensus, repeated G20 mandates, public monitoring, peer review and the expectation that members implement what they agreed.

Its unusual value is cross-sector sight. Finance ministries, central banks, supervisors, international financial institutions and standard setters sit in the same room. This lets the FSB follow risk when it moves from a bank to a fund, from bilateral derivatives to a CCP, from a deposit to a stablecoin, or from a regulated firm to a technology provider.

The six core systems

1. Banks and Basel III

Basel III is the centrepiece of the post-crisis bank-capital and liquidity rebuild, but the division of labour matters:

  • BCBS writes and interprets Basel standards. It monitors timeliness and consistency through the Regulatory Consistency Assessment Programme (RCAP).
  • The FSB designates Basel III as a priority implementation area, joins it to the wider G20 reform program and reports implementation to the G20.
  • National authorities enact and enforce domestic rules. A Basel text or FSB dashboard is not self-executing law.

The package includes the 2010 capital/liquidity reforms and the 2017 finalisation of post-crisis reforms. The 2025 FSB Implementation Monitoring Review found that the 2010 elements were widely adopted, often late, while the 2017 package was still incomplete. Credit risk, operational risk and the output floor were close to final in many places; market risk and credit-valuation-adjustment rules had wider gaps. The current September 30, 2026 BCBS dashboard says three quarters of member jurisdictions had published all final rules but only 47% had all final standards in force. The UK has final rules for January 2027; the United States has March 2026 proposals but no final rule or announced effective date.

Read the full bank-layer reconciliation at FSB_WAVE_2_BANK_CONSTITUTION_2009-2026.md and the exact jurisdiction rows at FSB_BASEL_III_JURISDICTION_STATUS_2026-09-30.csv.

This is not a technical side story. The FSB called Basel implementation a possible vulnerability in its own right when major markets delay or diverge.

2. Too-big-to-fail, SIFIs and resolution

The FSB identifies and monitors the global systemically important institution framework and owns the Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes. The resolution constitution includes:

  • a named resolution authority and powers;
  • loss absorption and Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity (TLAC);
  • crisis-management groups and cross-border cooperation agreements;
  • recovery and resolution plans and resolvability assessments;
  • continuity of critical functions and FMI access;
  • funding in resolution, bridge/transfer tools and bail-in execution; and
  • safeguards, creditor hierarchy, netting, collateral and client-asset rules.

The 2025 Resolution Report said the foundational frameworks are mostly in place, but actual readiness remains incomplete—especially funding in resolution and cross-border bail-in execution. The October 2026 backstop peer review found only about one in five FSB jurisdictions fully compliant with Key Attribute 6 and about half with material gaps in public last-resort funding arrangements.

This matters directly to digital finance. A tokenised claim or stablecoin may change the record and speed; it does not answer which entity fails, which law governs, who can transfer assets, how liquidity arrives or who bears loss.

3. Derivatives, CCPs and financial market infrastructure

The 2009 G20 derivatives bargain required:

  • trade-repository reporting;
  • central clearing of standardised OTC contracts;
  • exchange/electronic-platform trading where appropriate; and
  • higher capital and margin for non-centrally cleared contracts.

This moved risk as well as reducing it. Bilateral counterparty risk became more visible and collateralised, but more activity concentrated in central counterparties. The resulting infrastructure stack includes:

  • CPMI-IOSCO Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures;
  • CCP resilience and recovery;
  • FSB resolution standards and crisis-management groups for cross-border CCPs;
  • unique transaction/product identifiers and critical data elements;
  • trade repositories, legal-entity identifiers and cross-border data access;
  • margin and collateral liquidity; and
  • continuity of FMI access when a member firm fails.

The 2025 implementation review judged central clearing largely implemented, but said use of trade-repository data still lagged. The FSB's 2024–2026 work keeps moving from rule design to operational resolution, margin/collateral readiness and cross-sector failure testing.

The detailed Wave 3 reconstruction adds the current proof boundaries. The January 2026 PFMI Level 1 tracker shows broad legal adoption, with Argentina's CCP/CSD measures and a split U.S. trade-repository row still below full in-force status. A separate November 2025 Level 3 review found six serious general-business-risk gaps across a voluntary sample of 34 FMIs. The October 2024 SI>1 list contains 14 CCPs; the 2024 resolution standard adds a seven-tool resource toolbox, while the 2025 Resolution Report still finds uneven operational preparedness. See FSB_WAVE_3_FMI_DERIVATIVES_CONSTITUTION_2009-2026.md.

This is the institutional foundation under tokenised securities. A shared ledger can alter matching, records and delivery mechanics; it does not erase CCP, collateral, margin, trade-reporting, netting, access or resolution rules.

4. Non-bank financial intermediation

The FSB's NBFI program began as “shadow banking” reform and now covers five linked problems:

  1. bank–non-bank spillovers;
  2. money-market-fund run risk;
  3. securitisation transparency and incentives;
  4. procyclical securities-financing activity; and
  5. systemic risk in other non-bank entities and activities.

The March 2020 market shock exposed liquidity mismatch and margin/collateral demands. By 2025 the FSB added nine recommendations on non-bank leverage across risk monitoring, core markets, counterparty-credit controls, inconsistent regulatory treatment and cross-border cooperation. It also created a Nonbank Data Task Force, using leveraged sovereign-bond trading as a test case.

The current scale and the open effectiveness gap are now explicit. Using end-2024 data, the 2025 global monitor puts NBFI at 51.0% of total global financial assets and the narrower bank-like-risk measure at $76.3 trillion. The 2026 repo report maps $16 trillion of government-bond-backed repo, with large overnight, cross-border and zero-haircut segments. The 2026 private-credit report estimates a $1.5–2.0 trillion market, intertwined with banks, insurers, pensions and private equity but untested at current scale in a prolonged severe downturn. MMF and OEF reforms still have separate 2026 and 2028 implementation and effectiveness clocks. See FSB_WAVE_4_NBFI_CORE_MARKETS_2013-2026.md.

This lane joins the crypto story at leverage, funds, collateral, liquidity and interconnections with banks. A tokenised fund or repo does not stop being an NBFI exposure merely because the ownership record moves onto DLT.

5. Incentives, data, identity and operational resilience

The FSB also holds the connective tissue that makes the larger system visible and governable:

  • sound compensation practices;
  • the Legal Entity Identifier;
  • cross-border data standards and access;
  • cyber-incident reporting and operational resilience;
  • outsourcing and third-party technology risk;
  • climate and AI vulnerability work; and
  • implementation dashboards, country/thematic peer reviews and assessments of reform effects.

These are not decorative controls. If authorities cannot identify the legal person, compare exposures, see counterparties, obtain records, or keep a critical service running, a faster digital rail can make failure harder rather than safer.

The operational lane now has a defined sequence: 49 cyber response/recovery practices in 2020; fragmented-reporting diagnosis in 2021; 16 convergence recommendations and a third-party lifecycle/systemic-dependency toolkit in 2023; a final machine-readable FIRE format in 2025; and AI vulnerability, monitoring and institution-governance work through 2026. The June 2026 AI consultation proposed twelve practices, including meaningful human oversight, cyber/ICT controls and third-party AI risk. The October 12 letter says the final practices were delivered to the G20, but the standalone final text was not located. See FSB_WAVE_5_OPERATIONAL_TECHNOLOGY_CONSTITUTION_2017-2026.md.

6. Digital finance and payment-system change

The digital lane, mapped in FSB_DIGITAL_FINANCE_TRANSITION_MAP_2015-2026.md, starts inside this older constitution. From 2017 onward the FSB followed wholesale DLT, digital currencies, smart contracts, crypto assets, stablecoins, DeFi, tokenised traditional assets and cross-border payment infrastructure.

The governing continuity is striking:

Older control problem Digital form of the same question
Basel capital/liquidity What exposure does a bank have to crypto, a tokenised claim, stablecoin reserves or a service provider?
SIFI/resolution Can a wallet, issuer, exchange, custodian or multi-issuer stablecoin be recovered, transferred or wound down?
OTC/CCP/FMI Who is the authoritative record, where is margin, what money settles, and which infrastructure is critical?
NBFI leverage/liquidity Does tokenisation or DeFi increase leverage, maturity mismatch, correlated liquidation or fire-sale risk?
Data/LEI/reporting Can authorities identify entities, holders, geographies, flows and interconnections?

What the 2025 implementation review revealed

The October 2025 interim review is the best single whole-FSB map. It audited fifteen years of:

  • Basel III;
  • global systemically important institutions and TLAC;
  • resolution;
  • compensation;
  • NBFI;
  • OTC derivatives; and
  • crypto-asset recommendations.

Its conclusion was not that the reforms failed. It found major resilience gains and high implementation in several older areas. But “full, timely and consistent” implementation had not been achieved, and momentum had slowed.

The pattern was uneven:

  • Basel III remained incomplete in important markets;
  • TBTF work advanced, but resolution funding, legal powers and cross-border bail-in had gaps;
  • banks were ahead of insurers and asset managers on compensation;
  • NBFI liquidity and securities-financing data remained uneven;
  • central clearing was largely implemented, while trade-repository data use lagged;
  • LEI and commodity-market supervision had gaps; and
  • the crypto framework was too new and incomplete to judge for effectiveness.

That is the institutional backdrop to the 2026 modernisation effort. The FSB is not simply adding new rules. It is asking why agreed reforms stall, how policy design can make implementation easier, and how frameworks can adapt without discarding crisis-tested resilience.

The 2026 modernisation turn

The word “modernisation” refers to regulatory and supervisory method, not a single new financial infrastructure.

The public record has four steps:

  1. November 19, 2025: the Plenary agreed to examine national modernisation efforts and support international alignment.
  2. February 3, 2026: the work programme scheduled a symposium and an October report.
  3. June 9, 2026: the Basel symposium discussed drivers of change, cost-benefit analysis, tailoring/proportionality, technology and a level playing field.
  4. October 12, 2026: Andrew Bailey's G20 letter said a consultation report was being delivered with principles for frameworks that are effective, efficient, risk-focused and forward-looking while preserving or improving resilience. It said the report would be finalised in 2027.

As of the evidence cutoff, the FSB had not posted a standalone public report page or PDF for the modernisation consultation, and the current sitemap did not list it. The same letter said a 2026 Implementation Monitoring Review report was being delivered, but no standalone public object was located. Those are delivery statements, not a public-text receipt.

What is actually happening now

The FSB's 2025–2026 agenda is converging around one operating problem: older post-crisis safeguards must keep working while market structure, technology and the location of leverage change.

The important Freedom 250 reading

The FSB record changes our map in two ways.

First, digital finance is not a side corridor. The FSB has placed it inside the same constitution that governs bank loss absorption, clearing, collateral, entity identity, critical infrastructure, cross-border cooperation and failure. That strongly supports our “network of synchronized legal ledgers” reading.

Second, the FSB is now modernising the process that produces and monitors the rules. This is a meta-layer: not only new technologies and markets, but a review of cost-benefit analysis, proportionality, policy design, consultation, prioritisation and implementation monitoring. That may change how quickly new digital and traditional-finance rules converge—or expose why they do not.

Wave 6 turns that claim into a proof structure. CFIM, adoption dashboards, peer reviews, outcome assessments and post-implementation evaluations answer different questions. The capstone matrix therefore tracks mandate, international object, national law, operation, supervision, stress and effect on separate clocks. Return gates

  1. Locate the public consultation report on regulatory and supervisory modernisation; capture its principles, questions, consultation close and finalisation path.
  2. Locate the public 2026 Implementation Monitoring Review report; compare its process recommendations with the 2025 implementation findings.
  3. Track the 2027 final modernisation report and any resulting change to FSB policy design, consultation or implementation monitoring.
  4. Recheck BCBS RCAP and jurisdictional rules for the remaining Basel III gaps; do not infer national implementation from an FSB or BCBS statement.
  5. Track actual CCP, bank and stablecoin failure-preparedness tests, funding and cross-border execution—not just written plans.
  6. Join tokenised market objects to their legacy controls: capital, liquidity, margin, collateral, FMI access, authoritative ownership record, cash settlement, recovery and loss.
